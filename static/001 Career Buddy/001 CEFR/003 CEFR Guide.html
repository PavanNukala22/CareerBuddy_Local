CEFR Guide / Comprehensive Reference

CEFR Comprehensive Guide

A complete reference framework for language proficiency.

📖 Introduction to CEFR

ℹ️
What is CEFR? The Common European Framework of Reference for Languages (CEFR) is an internationally recognized standard developed by the Council of Europe for describing language proficiency. It spans six levels — A1 to C2 — and provides a transparent, coherent basis for curriculum design, teaching, and assessment across all languages.

The CEFR was first published in 2001 and has since been adopted by educational institutions, examination boards, and employers worldwide. It shifted language education from grammar-focused teaching to an action-oriented approach, viewing learners as "social agents" who accomplish tasks in real-world communicative situations.

The framework is organized around "can-do" descriptors — positive statements of what a learner is able to do at each proficiency level. This makes it applicable across all languages and educational contexts, from school curricula to professional certification.

"The CEFR's primary aim is to overcome the barriers to communication among professionals working in the field of modern languages." — Council of Europe

Key Features of the Framework

  • Non-prescriptive: It provides a framework for adaptation, not a rigid ruleset
  • Comprehensive: Covers all language skills — listening, reading, speaking, writing, and mediation
  • Transparent: Enables consistent, cross-cultural description of language skills
  • Scalable: Applicable from beginner to mastery level in any language
  • Action-oriented: Focus on real-world communicative tasks

🧭 Core Dimensions of the CEFR

The CEFR framework is built around three interconnected dimensions that together define language use and learning: Language Activities, Domains, and Competences.

1. Language Activities

Language activities describe what users do with language. The CEFR organizes these into four major categories:

👂 Reception

Processing spoken or written language produced by others. Includes listening comprehension and reading comprehension. Learners decode meaning from audio, video, or text.

🗣️ Production

Creating spoken or written language. Includes oral production (monologues, presentations) and written production (essays, reports, creative writing).

💬 Interaction

Two-way communication where participants alternate between producing and receiving. Includes spoken interaction (conversations, interviews) and written interaction (correspondence, chats).

🔄 Mediation

Facilitating communication between parties, including translation, interpreting, and summarizing. Expanded significantly in the 2020 Companion Volume to include plurilingual mediation.

2. Domains

Domains are context areas that determine where language use takes place. They guide the selection of topics, tasks, and texts appropriate for learners:

Domain Description Example Contexts
Personal Private life, family, friends, leisure Home conversations, social media, hobbies
Public General society, community activities Shops, transport, civic events, media
Educational Learning settings and academic life Classroom, lectures, academic writing
Professional Work-related contexts Meetings, emails, technical documentation

3. Competences

Competences refer to the knowledge, skills, and dispositions that a language user draws upon when communicating:

🎓
Linguistic Competence — Knowledge of phonology, vocabulary, grammar, spelling, and orthography.

Sociolinguistic Competence — Ability to use language appropriately in social situations; understanding of register, politeness, and cultural conventions.

Pragmatic Competence — Ability to structure discourse, use language for different functions, and achieve communicative purposes effectively.

🟦
Global descriptor: Can understand and use familiar everyday expressions and very basic phrases aimed at satisfying needs of a concrete type. Can introduce themselves and others and can ask and answer questions about personal details.

👑

📋 Curriculum & Syllabus Design

Effective CEFR-aligned curriculum design requires moving away from purely content-based instruction toward an action-oriented, competence-based approach centred on what learners can do in real life.

💡
Core Principle: Curriculum objectives should be expressed as "can-do" statements, not as lists of grammatical structures or vocabulary items. The CEFR shifts the question from "What grammar have students learned?" to "What can students accomplish using the language?"

Step-by-Step Curriculum Development

1
Identify Target Level and Learner Profile

Define the entry and exit level for the course. Analyse learner needs, domains of use (personal, professional, academic), and prior language experience. Tailor objectives to specific populations (young learners, adults, ESP students).

2
Define Learning Outcomes Using Can-Do Statements

Convert curriculum goals into CEFR-referenced can-do descriptors. Use the CEFR Companion Volume's updated illustrative descriptor scales for interaction, mediation, and online communication. Examples: "Can describe past experiences in simple connected sentences" (B1) or "Can write structured academic reports" (C1).

3
Sequence Topics and Tasks by Complexity

Organise content thematically with clear progression. Build from concrete to abstract topics, from simple single-clause sentences at A1 to complex multi-paragraph argumentation at C1–C2. Incorporate a spiral curriculum where topics recur with increasing depth.

4
Integrate All Four Skills + Mediation

Ensure that listening, speaking, reading, writing, and mediation tasks are integrated throughout the curriculum — not taught as separate modules. Design thematic units where a reading text feeds a discussion task, which then leads to a writing activity.

5
Incorporate Formative and Summative Assessment

Align assessment with can-do objectives. Use formative assessment (portfolios, self-assessment checklists) alongside summative tasks (CEFR-referenced exams). Include the European Language Portfolio as a self-assessment and reflection tool.

Interaction and Plurilingualism in the Curriculum

The 2020 Companion Volume expanded the CEFR's scope significantly. Modern curricula should incorporate:

  • Online interaction: Email, chat, collaborative tools, virtual discussions
  • Plurilingual competence: Drawing on the learner's entire linguistic repertoire, including L1 and other languages
  • Mediation tasks: Summarising, paraphrasing, explaining, relaying information for others
  • Emotional and cultural awareness: Understanding cultural context, not just linguistic form

📚 Material Development

Developing or selecting materials aligned with the CEFR involves careful attention to text complexity, skill integration, and cultural authenticity.

Text Selection Criteria

Level Text Complexity Sentence Structure Vocabulary Range Recommended Text Types
A1 Very simple Short, single clause ~500 words Labels, signs, postcards, simple forms
A2 Simple Linked simple sentences ~1,000 words Short messages, personal letters, simple menus
B1 Moderate Compound and some complex ~2,000 words News articles, personal narratives, brochures
B2 Challenging Complex and varied ~4,000 words Editorial articles, reports, contemporary fiction
C1 Advanced Sophisticated structures ~8,000 words Academic articles, specialist texts, literary works
C2 Expert Full stylistic range 16,000+ words Research papers, authentic literature, media analysis

Principles of Good Material Design

📌 Authenticity and Relevance ▶
Materials should include authentic texts adapted for level, not artificially simplified beyond recognition. A B1 newspaper article, for example, should retain coherent discourse structure even if vocabulary is pre-taught. Cultural content — beliefs, attitudes, social norms — should represent approximately 30% of thematic focus to build intercultural competence.
📌 Integrated Skill Activities ▶
Avoid designing materials that isolate single skills (e.g., grammar drills only). Effective CEFR-aligned materials use thematic units where reading a text feeds a discussion, which feeds a writing task. Listening tasks should be linked to speaking activities. Integrated tasks better reflect real-world language use.
📌 Scaffolding and Progression ▶
Materials should be structured so that each new task builds on previous knowledge. Provide vocabulary support (glossaries, visual aids) for lower levels. At higher levels, reduce scaffolding to build learner autonomy. Use pre-task, during-task, and post-task phases for complex activities.
📌 Task Typology ▶
Include a variety of task types: information-gap tasks, problem-solving tasks, creative tasks, role-plays, and project-based tasks. Task complexity should be calibrated to level using parameters such as cognitive load, interactional requirements, and production demands.

Project-Based & Mediation Tasks

🔄
Mediation tasks (particularly relevant B2–C2): Ask learners to summarise a long text for a classmate, relay information from one language to another, interpret a chart and explain it to the class, or facilitate a discussion between two parties. These develop plurilingual competence and real-world communication skills.

Digital and AI-Enhanced Practice

  • AI text generation: Use AI tools to generate texts calibrated to specific CEFR levels (controlling lexical density, sentence length, and topic familiarity)
  • Automatic gap-fill creation: Generate fill-in-the-gap exercises from target-level texts
  • Speech recognition tools: Practice pronunciation and fluency with automated feedback
  • Adaptive learning platforms: Systems that adjust task difficulty based on learner performance
  • Portfolio tools: Digital European Language Portfolios for self-monitoring and reflection

📝 Assessment Design

Assessment in the CEFR framework measures communicative performance, not merely grammatical accuracy. The assessment system is built on standardisation, validity, and transparency.

The CEFR Assessment Cycle

1
Familiarisation

Assessors and learners become familiar with the CEFR levels, can-do descriptors, and illustrative samples. Benchmark texts and speech samples are used to anchor understanding of level distinctions.

2
Specification

Define the scope and format of the test: which levels, which skills, which domains. Construct a test specification document aligning tasks with target CEFR levels. Link each task to a specific can-do descriptor.

3
Standardisation

Train raters using anchor samples (texts or recordings at specific CEFR levels). Conduct inter-rater reliability exercises. Use rating scales that operationalise CEFR descriptors into observable criteria (e.g., range, accuracy, fluency, coherence, interaction).

4
Validation

Gather evidence that the test measures what it claims to measure. Validate test scores against external CEFR benchmarks (e.g., Cambridge, DELF, IELTS). Apply statistical analysis (Rasch modelling, IRT) to confirm level appropriateness.

Assessment Criteria (Speaking & Writing)

Range

The variety and appropriateness of vocabulary and grammatical structures used; ability to express ideas without circumlocution.

Accuracy

The degree to which the learner uses correct grammar, vocabulary, and pronunciation without impeding communication.

Fluency

The ability to produce continuous connected language without undue hesitation; naturalness and speed of delivery.

Interaction

The ability to engage in discourse: initiating and responding, turn-taking, asking for clarification, supporting the interlocutor.

Coherence

The logical organisation of ideas; use of discourse markers and cohesive devices to link sentences and paragraphs.

Task Achievement

The degree to which the learner accomplishes the communicative goal of the task as specified in the prompt.

Task Types for Assessment

🎧 Listening Assessment Tasks ▶
  • Multiple choice: Select the correct answer based on spoken input
  • True/False/Not Given: Evaluate statements against audio
  • Gap-fill: Complete a transcript or notes while listening
  • Matching: Match speakers to opinions or items to descriptions
  • Ordering: Put events or information in correct sequence
📖 Reading Assessment Tasks ▶
  • Gist reading: Identify main topic or purpose
  • Scanning: Locate specific information quickly
  • Skimming: Identify writer's attitude or tone
  • Open-ended questions: Produce short answers in own words
  • Paragraph matching: Match headings, summaries, or missing sentences
🗣️ Speaking Assessment Tasks ▶
  • Monologue/presentation: Prepared or impromptu speech on a topic
  • Interaction: Paired discussion, negotiation, or role-play
  • Picture description: Describe and interpret visual prompts
  • Interview: Respond to examiner questions
  • Collaborative task: Joint planning or problem-solving activity
✍️ Writing Assessment Tasks ▶
  • Formal letter/email: Complain, request, apply
  • Report/proposal: Analyse a situation and make recommendations
  • Essay: Discuss, argue, or evaluate a topic
  • Review: Evaluate a product, experience, or creative work
  • Narrative/description: Recount events or describe experiences

⚖️ Quality Standards & Compliance

To ensure that materials and assessments are genuinely CEFR-aligned rather than simply labelled as such, rigorous quality standards must be applied throughout development and validation.

Alignment Standards

StandardDescriptionTools/Methods
Content Validity Tasks reflect the domains and competences appropriate to the target level CEFR grid analysis, expert review
Construct Validity Tests measure communicative competence, not just linguistic knowledge Task analysis, alignment grids
Criterion Validity Scores correlate with established external benchmarks Correlation with IELTS, DELF, Cambridge
Reliability Consistent scores across raters and time Inter-rater agreement, Cronbach's alpha
Washback Effect Test preparation encourages authentic communicative practice Curriculum review, learner feedback

Text Calibration Standards

When developing or selecting texts for CEFR-aligned materials or assessments, the following calibration tools and parameters should be applied:

  • Lexical density Proportion of content words to total words — higher at upper levels
  • Type-Token Ratio Diversity of vocabulary used — increases with level
  • Mean sentence length Average words per sentence — increases with level
  • Readability indices Flesch-Kincaid, Gunning Fog for approximate level calibration
  • Syntactic complexity Subordination, embedding, passive use — increases with level
  • Topic familiarity Known topics easier to process; unfamiliar topics raise effective difficulty

Pre-A1 Level

🌱
The Pre-A1 level is designed for absolute beginners, particularly relevant for very young learners or heritage language contexts. Can-do statements at this level include recognising printed letters and numbers, repeating single words or phrases, and understanding simple commands supported by gesture.

🚀 Implementation Principles & Guidelines

The Three Core Principles

🔓 Flexibility

The CEFR is non-prescriptive. It does not dictate specific methods or materials — it provides a reference framework to be adapted to specific contexts, whether ESP courses, school curricula, or adult education programmes.

🎯 Action-Oriented

The learner is treated as a "social agent" who performs tasks in real communicative situations. Language learning is in service of accomplishing meaningful goals, not merely accumulating linguistic knowledge.

🔍 Transparency

The framework enables consistent, understandable descriptions of language skills across countries, languages, and institutions. This supports recognition of qualifications, mobility, and fair assessment.

🌍 Plurilingualism

The CEFR recognises and values the learner's full linguistic repertoire. Multilingual competence is not a set of separate monolingual systems but an integrated, dynamic whole that can be leveraged in learning.

Practical Implementation Checklist

AreaActionIndicator of Success
Curriculum Map all learning outcomes to CEFR can-do descriptors Every objective linked to a specific level descriptor
Materials Audit texts for level-appropriate complexity Texts pass calibration checks (readability, vocabulary)
Teaching Adopt action-oriented, task-based lesson plans Lesson plans include real-world communicative tasks
Assessment Design tasks based on CEFR task typology Raters use CEFR-referenced rating scales
Learner Autonomy Use the European Language Portfolio Learners self-assess using can-do statements
Professional Development Train teachers in CEFR familiarisation Teachers can accurately place learners and calibrate materials

Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Level labelling without validation: Simply labelling materials "B2" without calibration is misleading and undermines the framework
  • Treating levels as rigid thresholds: Learners may be A2 in reading but B1 in speaking — proficiency profiles are uneven
  • Over-focus on grammar: The CEFR is competence-based; grammar serves communication, it is not an end in itself
  • Ignoring mediation: Pre-2020 materials often omit mediation entirely — this is now a core competence area
  • Neglecting plurilingualism: Forbidding L1 in the classroom contradicts the CEFR's plurilingual orientation
"A plurilingual approach emphasises the fact that as an individual person's experience of language in its cultural contexts expands, they do not keep these languages and cultures in strictly separated mental compartments." — CEFR (2001)

👂 Listening Assignments

B1Note-taking from a short announcement
"Attention passengers, the 10:45 service to Manchester has been delayed by twenty minutes due to a signalling fault near Crewe. We apologise for the inconvenience. Passengers travelling to Manchester should proceed to platform 4."
Task: Note the train time, destination, delay length, reason, and new platform.
Model notes: 10:45 → Manchester | delay: 20 min | reason: signalling fault (Crewe) | platform: 4
B2Following an argument in a workplace meeting
"I take your point about cost, but if we cut the QA phase short again, we're just moving the expense downstream — we'll pay for it in support tickets instead of testing hours."
Task: What is the speaker's real position, even though they open by agreeing?
Model answer: They disagree with cutting QA — "I take your point" is a polite concession before the speaker pivots to their actual objection (the cost doesn't disappear, it relocates).
C1Inference from an unscripted interview
"Sure, growth's been fine. I mean, if you'd told me two years ago we'd be here, I'd have taken it. But 'fine' isn't really where I wanted this to land."
Task: Identify the tone shift and what it reveals about the speaker's real satisfaction level.
Model answer: The speaker starts with surface agreement ("fine," "I'd have taken it") then undercuts it with "isn't really where I wanted this to land" — a hedge that signals disappointment they're reluctant to state directly.

📖 Reading Assignments

B1Finding the main idea
"Remote work has changed how teams collaborate. While some employees enjoy the flexibility, others report feeling isolated. Companies are now experimenting with hybrid models to balance both needs."
Task: What's the main idea in one sentence?
Model answer: Companies are testing hybrid work models to balance the flexibility employees want with the connection they're missing.
B2Identifying the author's purpose
"It's tempting to call this quarter's results a turnaround. But one good quarter after five weak ones is a data point, not a pattern — and treating it as proof of recovery risks repeating the same mistakes."
Task: Is the author's purpose to celebrate, warn, or simply report? Justify your answer.
Model answer: To warn. The author explicitly rejects the "turnaround" framing and flags a risk in over-interpreting one good result.
C1Reading between the lines
"The committee thanked the outgoing director for her 'unwavering commitment to stability' during her tenure."
Task: What might this praise be subtly implying, given the specific word choice?

🗣️ Speaking Assignments

B1Narrating an experience
Prompt: Describe a time you had to learn something new quickly for work or study.
Model outline: Set the scene → what made it urgent → how you approached it → how it turned out → one thing you'd do differently.
B2Giving a balanced opinion
Prompt: Should companies require employees to be in the office full-time?
Model outline: State your position → give the strongest opposing argument first → counter it → give your main reason → close with a nuanced conclusion.
C1Presenting under challenge
Prompt: Present a case for or against AI-assisted hiring, then respond to: "But that just automates existing bias."
Model outline: Open with your strongest case → anticipate the bias objection → concede its real force → distinguish it from your specific claim → close without overclaiming certainty.

Standardised testing is often defended on the grounds of fairness — a single measure applied equally to all. Yet this very uniformity is its central flaw: it treats students with vastly different backgrounds as though a single instrument could capture their ability fairly.

🔤 Vocabulary Assignments

B1Collocations
We say "make a decision", not "do a decision." Other examples: make progress, take a break, do research, have an argument.
B2Phrasal verbs in professional contexts
"Can you follow up on that?" is more natural in a work email than the overly formal "Could you subsequently pursue that matter?" — register matters as much as meaning.
C1Register and connotation
"He was frugal" vs "He was stingy" — both describe careful spending, but "frugal" is neutral-to-positive while "stingy" carries negative judgment.

📐 Grammar Assignments

B1Present perfect vs. simple past
"I've worked here for three years" (ongoing) vs "I worked there for three years" (finished) — the choice tells the listener whether the situation continues.
B2Second conditional (hypothetical)
"If I had more time, I would learn another language" — describes an unreal or unlikely present situation.
C1Inversion for emphasis
"Never have I seen such a disorganised launch" — formal, emphatic inversion used in writing and speeches for dramatic effect.
