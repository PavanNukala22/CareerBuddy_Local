    Module 01 · Course Info

    📐 B1 Course Overview

    A fully scripted collection of 50 classroom activities (10 per skill) for the Threshold / Intermediate level — covering Listening, Reading, Spoken Interaction, Spoken Production, and Writing. Designed for 60–80 contact hours across a structured course.

    📊 Level
    B1 — Threshold / Intermediate
    🎯 Skills
    🎧 Listening 📖 Reading 💬 Spoken Interaction 🗣️ Spoken Production ✍️ Writing
    👥 Target learners
    Adults / teens who completed A2; workplace English; exam preparation (PET/B1 Preliminary)
    ⏱ Course length
    60–80 contact hours (16–20 weeks at 4 hrs/week)
    📦 Vocabulary target
    ~2,000–2,500 words; academic & workplace collocations introduced
    🏁 Exit outcome
    Learner handles most travel situations, discusses familiar topics at length, produces coherent extended text, and understands the main points of clear standard speech.
    💡
    How to use this guide. Each skill section opens with its CEFR descriptor and micro-targets, then delivers 10 fully scripted activities with aims, procedures, scripts, answer keys, rubrics, and differentiation notes — all hidden in expandable toggles to reduce visual clutter. Colour coding is consistent throughout: Listening Reading Interaction Production Writing

    At B1 (Threshold / Intermediate), learners cross into genuine communicative independence. They can understand the main points of clear, standard input on familiar matters — work, school, leisure — and handle most travel situations. They produce connected discourse: not just sentences, but paragraphs organised by topic, opinion, and time.

    Listening
    Understands standard speech on familiar matters — including radio & podcasts
    Reading
    Reads texts on current affairs, jobs, and everyday topics with reasonable confidence
    Spoken Interaction
    Handles travel, conversations on familiar topics, and unexpected situations
    Spoken Production
    Narrates stories, plots, plans, dreams, hopes, and ambitions in extended turns
    Writing
    Writes connected paragraphs on personal interests, events, experiences, and arguments

    Module 03 · Skill 1

    🎧 1. Listening

    🎧
    CEFR B1 Descriptor
    Understands the main points of clear standard speech on familiar matters regularly encountered in work, school, leisure, etc. — including short radio news programmes and straightforward informational content.
    🎯
    Micro-targets: Follow extended monologues and conversations (2–4 min) at near-natural speed; understand gist, key arguments, and speaker attitude; identify contrast, cause-effect, and concession signals; tolerate unfamiliar vocabulary by using context; distinguish factual statements from opinions; follow narrative and chronological sequence.
    1

    Radio Documentary — "The Gig Economy": Gist → Detail → Inference

    ⏱ 40 min👥 Individual → pairs🎚 B1.1🧰 Audio (3 min), structured response sheet

    Aim: Follow an extended informational monologue; distinguish facts from opinions; identify the speaker's attitude using lexical and intonation cues.

    Full procedure (5 stages)
    1. Schema activation (5 min). Pairs brainstorm for 60 sec: "What jobs can you do online or from home, without a permanent contract?" Feed back. Introduce the term "gig economy."
    2. Listen for gist (7 min). Play the full 3-minute clip once at natural speed. Students choose the best one-sentence summary from three options: (a) The gig economy is great for everyone. (b) The gig economy has both benefits and drawbacks. ✓ (c) Gig workers earn more than traditional workers.
    3. Listen for detail (12 min). Play in two halves (1–1.5 min each), twice each. Students complete a structured notes grid: Problems mentioned / Benefits mentioned / Statistics heard / Speaker's overall view.
    4. Inference task (8 min). Students answer: "Does the reporter think the gig economy is mostly positive or negative? Find 3 pieces of evidence from the language used." Debrief focusing on evaluative adjectives and intonation.
    5. Reflection (8 min). Class discussion: "Do you agree with the reporter? What is the gig economy like in your country?" Bridges to spoken interaction.
    Audio script (~ 300 words, 3 min)
    "Welcome to Working Lives. I'm Sarah Chen, and today we're looking at the so-called gig economy — the world of freelancers, app-based workers, and self-employed contractors that has transformed the labour market over the last decade.

    The numbers are striking. In the UK alone, around five million people now work in what economists classify as non-traditional employment. For some, the appeal is obvious: flexibility, independence, and the ability to work for multiple clients simultaneously. Maria, a 34-year-old graphic designer in Manchester, told us she would never go back to a nine-to-five. 'I earn roughly the same,' she said, 'but I choose my own hours and I've never been more productive.'

    But the picture is far from uniformly rosy. Critics — and there are many — point to the absence of employee protections: no sick pay, no holiday entitlement, no employer pension contributions. Research from the University of Oxford suggests that gig workers earn, on average, 25% less per hour than equivalent employees once downtime is accounted for.

    Then there's the psychological toll. A survey published last year found that 60% of gig workers reported feeling isolated, and nearly half said financial uncertainty was a constant source of anxiety.

    The question, then, is not whether the gig economy exists — it clearly does — but whether society is prepared to regulate it fairly. Some countries, like France and Spain, have moved to extend basic worker protections to platform workers. Others, including the United States, continue to resist.

    The gig economy is here to stay. The real challenge is making it work for everyone — not just the platforms profiting from it."
    Answer key & differentiation

    Gist answer: (b).

    Detail grid: Problems — no sick pay/holiday/pension; earn 25% less; isolation (60%); anxiety (50%). Benefits — flexibility, independence, multiple clients. Statistics — 5 million UK gig workers; 25% less pay; 60% isolated; 50% anxious.

    Inference: Mostly critical — "far from uniformly rosy," "psychological toll," "real challenge," ends with implicit criticism of platforms.

    Support: Provide the script with key words blanked. Stretch: Students write a 100-word summary of the reporter's argument, then compare their summaries.

    2

    Podcast Opinions — "Is Social Media Good for Society?"

    ⏱ 35 min👥 Individual → groups of 3🎚 B1.2🧰 Audio, opinion tracking grid

    Aim: Track multiple speakers' contrasting opinions; identify agreement, partial agreement, and disagreement signals.

    Script, tasks & answer key
    Host: So, James — is social media doing more harm than good?
    James: I think it's a genuinely complex issue. On one hand, it connects people — especially those who are isolated, perhaps because of disability or geography. On the other hand, the evidence for its impact on teenage mental health is pretty alarming.
    Host: Dr Patel, do you agree?
    Dr Patel: Partly. I'd add, though, that the research is more nuanced than headlines suggest. It's not social media per se that causes harm — it's how people use it. Passive scrolling is damaging; active, meaningful interaction can actually improve wellbeing.
    James: Fair point. But isn't the platform design deliberately engineered to maximise passive consumption?
    Dr Patel: That's hard to argue against. The business model relies on attention, and attention is best captured by outrage and comparison. So I think both of us agree that regulation is overdue.
    Host: There's something both of you seem to agree on — individual responsibility isn't enough.

    Opinion grid task: Students fill in: James's views / Dr Patel's views / Points of agreement / Points of disagreement.

    Key answers: Agreement — regulation needed; individual use matters; platforms are problematic. Disagreement — James: more pessimistic overall; Dr Patel: distinguishes types of use.

    3

    Travel Problem — Understanding & Responding

    ⏱ 25 min👥 Individual → pairs🎚 B1.1🧰 Audio dialogue, task sheet

    Aim: Understand unexpected problems in travel situations and follow the resolution — a core B1 can-do.

    Dialogue, tasks & extension
    Customer: Excuse me, I booked a room for tonight — the name's Larsson, L-A-R-S-S-O-N.
    Receptionist: One moment… I'm sorry, I can see the reservation, but it seems there was a system error and your room was double-booked. I do apologise.
    Customer: Oh no. I've just come off a twelve-hour flight. Is there any way you can sort something out tonight?
    Receptionist: Absolutely, and again, I'm so sorry for the inconvenience. I have two options for you. I can offer you an upgraded suite at no extra charge, or, if you'd prefer, we can arrange a room at our partner hotel two blocks away — also at no cost to you — and provide a taxi.
    Customer: What's the suite like?
    Receptionist: It's significantly larger — a king-size bed, a lounge area, and a complimentary breakfast. Most guests love it.
    Customer: Right, I'll take the suite then. Could I also get a late checkout? My meeting finishes at 2 p.m.
    Receptionist: Of course. I'll arrange 3 p.m. for you. And here's a voucher for a drink at our bar — on the house.

    Comprehension tasks: 1) What was the problem? 2) What two solutions were offered? 3) Which did the customer choose and why? 4) What two extra requests did the customer make? 5) How do you think the customer felt at the end — why?

    Extension: Write the complaint email the customer might send the next day — or the receptionist's incident report.

    4

    News Bulletin — Main Point & Follow-Up Questions

    ⏱ 30 min👥 Groups of 3🎚 B1.1🧰 Audio (90 sec × 3 stories), question-formulation task

    Aim: Extract the 5 Ws (who, what, where, when, why) from short news items; then generate follow-up questions — shifting from receptive to critical thinking.

    Procedure & sample bulletin

    Stage 1: Play 3 short news stories. After each, groups have 90 seconds to write the 5 Ws on a mini-board.

    Stage 2: Groups write 2 "follow-up questions" a journalist would ask — questions the bulletin didn't answer.

    Stage 3: Groups share their follow-up questions. Class votes on the most important unanswered question for each story.

    Story 1 (Science): "Scientists at Cambridge University announced today that a new battery technology could allow electric vehicles to charge in under five minutes — potentially revolutionising the car industry. The technology, which uses a graphene-based electrode, is still in the testing phase but could reach consumers within three years, researchers say."
    Story 2 (Environment): "A new report from the United Nations warns that one third of the world's topsoil has been lost in the last forty years due to industrial farming practices. Experts are calling for urgent changes to global agriculture policy to prevent a food security crisis by 2050."

    Sample follow-up Qs: Story 1 — "How much will it cost?"; "What happens to existing charging infrastructure?" Story 2 — "Which countries are most affected?"; "What specific policy changes are being proposed?"

    5

    Lecture Notes — Understanding an Academic Talk

    ⏱ 40 min👥 Individual → pairs🎚 B1.2🧰 Audio lecture excerpt (4 min), Cornell note template

    Aim: Take structured notes from a partly academic monologue — using a Cornell note template — capturing main points, supporting details, and examples.

    Procedure & lecture topic

    Topic: "Why do we forget things?" (Memory & learning).

    1. Pre-teach 6 terms: encoding, retrieval, interference, spaced repetition, working memory, long-term memory.
    2. Introduce the Cornell template: left column = questions/key terms; right column = notes; bottom = summary.
    3. Play the lecture excerpt twice. Students complete the template.
    4. In pairs, students compare notes and fill any gaps.
    5. Each student writes a 3-sentence summary in the bottom box without looking at the main notes.
    6. Debrief: What note-taking strategies worked? What would they change?

    Key points learners should capture: (1) Two types of forgetting: retrieval failure vs trace decay. (2) Interference theory: old information blocks new. (3) Ebbinghaus forgetting curve — 70% lost within 24 hours. (4) Solution: spaced repetition — review at 1 day, 1 week, 1 month intervals.

    6

    Discourse Marker Bingo — Signpost Recognition

    ⏱ 20 min👥 Individual🎚 B1.1🧰 Bingo cards, lecture audio

    Aim: Tune in to discourse markers that signal structure — firstly, however, in contrast, to sum up — so learners can follow arguments even when vocabulary is unclear.

    Marker bank & procedure

    Bingo cards contain 9 of these 16 markers:

    • First of all
    • Furthermore
    • However
    • In contrast
    • On the other hand
    • As a result
    • For instance
    • In other words
    • It's worth noting
    • Moving on
    • To summarise
    • The key point is
    • Interestingly
    • Despite this
    • What's more
    • In conclusion

    Students listen to a 3-minute talk (on any familiar topic) and cross markers as they hear them. After bingo, replay and ask: "What idea came after 'however'?" — to test comprehension, not just recognition.

    Follow-up: Students categorise their markers: Adding / Contrasting / Exemplifying / Summarising. This bank is used actively in the Writing and Speaking sections later.

    7

    Interview with a Time Traveller — Narrative Listening

    ⏱ 30 min👥 Individual → groups🎚 B1.1🧰 Audio interview (2.5 min), timeline template

    Aim: Follow a complex personal narrative with mixed past tenses (past simple, past perfect, past continuous); reconstruct the chronological sequence.

    Script excerpt & tasks
    Interviewer: So when did you first realise you wanted to be a doctor?
    Speaker: It's a long story. I'd actually started a law degree — I was in my second year — when my father had a serious heart attack. I remember I was studying in the library when my brother called. I drove four hours to the hospital not knowing whether he'd survive. He did, thankfully. But watching the doctors work that night — the calm, the skill, the way they communicated with our family — I knew. I finished my law degree, but by then my mind was already made up. I applied to medical school the day I graduated.
    Interviewer: Was it difficult to start over?
    Speaker: Incredibly. I was 25 by the time I started medicine, surrounded by 18-year-olds. My classmates had been learning biology since they were teenagers; I hadn't touched a science textbook since I was 16. But I'd developed habits during law school — structured reading, argument analysis — that turned out to be surprisingly useful in clinical reasoning.

    Task 1: Put these events in chronological order: started law degree / father's heart attack / applied to medical school / graduated law / started medicine / brother's phone call.

    Task 2: Find 4 examples of past perfect in the interview. What do they show about the sequence of events?

    Task 3: What two unexpected skills did the speaker transfer from law to medicine?

    8

    Authentic Film Clip — Listening Without Subtitles

    ⏱ 35 min👥 Groups of 3🎚 B1.2🧰 Film clip (3 min), prediction & response sheets

    Aim: Build confidence with authentic, unscripted (or lightly scripted) speech — a major B1 challenge and a gateway to B2.

    Staged procedure for difficult authentic input
    1. Watch with no sound (2 min). Groups predict: What's the situation? Who are the people? What's the relationship? What might they be saying?
    2. Watch with sound, no subtitles (3 min). What did you understand? What didn't you catch? Groups pool what they heard.
    3. Focus listening (3 min). Teacher poses 3 specific questions; students watch again. Target 3 specific moments, not overall comprehension.
    4. Watch with subtitles (3 min). What surprised you? What did you miss? Discuss why.
    5. Language harvest (5 min). Groups find 3 phrases they want to learn from the clip. Write, drill, contextualise.

    Recommended clip types: A short scene from a British crime drama; a TED Talk opening 3 minutes; a documentary segment; a stand-up comedy setup (without offensive content). Always preview for appropriateness.

    9

    Second-Hand Information — Reported Speech Challenge

    ⏱ 25 min👥 Groups of 4🎚 B1.2🧰 "Chinese whispers" setup, topic cards

    Aim: Practise reported speech in the context of a communicative listening game — capturing and relaying information accurately.

    Game procedure

    Groups of 4 sit in a line. Only Student A hears the audio (via earphones or the teacher whispers a 3-sentence fact). A must relay the information to B using reported speech. B relays to C; C to D. D writes the final version. Compare to original.

    Sample input for relay: "The scientist said that climate change is accelerating faster than expected. She explained that the Arctic ice had melted by 40% since 1980. She warned that if governments didn't act within the next decade, sea levels would rise by at least one metre."

    Language focus — tense backshifts: is → was / has melted → had melted / will rise → would rise.

    After 3 rounds, debrief: What errors crept in? Is reported speech always perfectly accurate in real life? Why/why not?

    10

    Mood & Music — Listening for Atmosphere

    ⏱ 20 min👥 Individual → pairs🎚 B1.1🧰 3 short audio excerpts (different genres), vocabulary cards

    Aim: Expand descriptive vocabulary for atmosphere, mood, and narrative tone — builds vocabulary for Spoken Production and Writing.

    Procedure & vocabulary set

    Play 3 audio excerpts (1 minute each): a tense thriller scene / a calm nature documentary / an upbeat travel programme. For each, students individually choose from the vocabulary bank, then compare with a partner and justify choices.

    Vocabulary bank: melancholic, suspenseful, tranquil, frantic, nostalgic, whimsical, tense, uplifting, ominous, soothing, chaotic, reflective, playful, foreboding, serene, urgent.

    Extension: Students write 2 sentences describing what they imagined during each clip. Share with class. Discuss how filmmakers/journalists create mood through language choices.

    Module 04 · Skill 2

    📖 2. Reading

    📖
    CEFR B1 Descriptor
    Reads straightforward factual texts on subjects related to their field and personal interests with a satisfactory level of comprehension. Understands the main points of articles on high-frequency everyday and job-related topics.
    🎯
    Micro-targets: Skim articles (300–500 words) for gist in under 90 seconds; scan for specific data; read for argument structure; identify the author's stance; understand vocabulary from context; read between the lines (implication); compare multiple texts on the same topic.
    1

    Two-Article Compare — "Is University Worth It?"

    ⏱ 45 min👥 Individual → pairs🎚 B1.2🧰 Two 250-word articles, comparison matrix

    Aim: Compare two texts presenting opposing views; identify the type of evidence each uses; practise reading critically — not just reading for information.

    Articles, matrix & tasks
    Article A — "The Degree Dividend" (Pro-university)
    A university degree remains one of the most reliable investments a young person can make. According to the Office for National Statistics, graduates in the UK earn on average 35% more over their lifetime than those without a degree. Beyond salary, graduates report higher job satisfaction, greater social mobility, and better health outcomes. Critics who dismiss degrees as 'just a piece of paper' ignore the complexity of what universities actually provide: critical thinking skills, professional networks, exposure to diversity of thought, and the simple but underrated experience of learning how to learn.
    Article B — "The Debt Trap" (Critical view)
    In 2024, the average UK student graduates with over £45,000 in debt. For many — particularly those entering competitive fields like arts, media, or social work — this debt will never be fully repaid before it is written off. Meanwhile, Germany and Switzerland have built world-class skilled workforces through apprenticeship models that pay trainees from day one. The assumption that a degree is always the best path reflects a class bias that disadvantages those who cannot afford three years without full income. It is time to have an honest conversation about alternatives.
    CriterionArticle AArticle B
    Main claim
    Type of evidence
    Tone (formal/critical/persuasive?)
    Strongest argument
    Weakest point

    Discussion: Which article is more convincing? Which uses more reliable evidence? Which do you personally agree with?

    2

    Paragraph Reconstruction — Text Jigsaw

    ⏱ 30 min👥 Pairs🎚 B1.1🧰 Cut-up article (8 paragraphs), topic sentences highlighted

    Aim: Develop understanding of how well-written articles are organised — topic sentences, cohesion devices, logical progression of argument.

    Procedure & cohesion focus
    1. Individual ordering (8 min). Each student orders the paragraphs independently, noting the clues they use.
    2. Pair negotiation (6 min). Compare orders. Discuss and agree.
    3. Whole-class reveal (5 min). Show the correct order. Pairs score themselves.
    4. Cohesion analysis (11 min). Teacher highlights 5 connectors/pronoun references across paragraph boundaries. Students explain what each refers back to or forward to.

    Suggested text: An article on the impact of remote working — organised as: intro → historical context → benefit 1 → benefit 2 → problem 1 → problem 2 → counterargument → conclusion.

    Clues to discuss: Discourse markers (however, as a result, despite this); pronouns referring to previous paragraph's topic; questions answered in the next paragraph.

    3

    Implied Meaning — "Reading Between the Lines"

    ⏱ 30 min👥 Groups of 3🎚 B1.2🧰 Two short texts (letters or excerpts)

    Aim: Develop inference skills — understanding what a writer implies but does not state — a key B1→B2 bridge skill.

    Texts & inference questions
    Text 1 — A work reference letter:
    "Mr Davies worked with us for three years and was always punctual. He completed his assigned tasks thoroughly. Colleagues found him straightforward to work with. He may find a better fit in a smaller organisation."

    Questions: (1) Is this a positive or negative reference? Explain. (2) What does "completed his assigned tasks thoroughly" imply — but not state — about initiative? (3) What does the final sentence really mean? (4) Would you hire Mr Davies based on this letter?

    Text 2 — Travel review:
    "The hotel is conveniently located near many bus routes. The rooms are compact, offering a cosy atmosphere. The restaurant closes at 9 p.m., so guests are encouraged to plan ahead. The staff were very attentive — we rarely had to wait more than 15 minutes for anything."

    Questions: (1) What does "compact" really mean? (2) What problem does "encouraged to plan ahead" imply? (3) Is "rarely had to wait more than 15 minutes" positive? (4) Star rating: 2, 3, or 4 stars? Justify.

    4

    Job Profile Matching — Careers Article

    ⏱ 30 min👥 Individual🎚 B1.1🧰 5-job profiles article + 6 candidate descriptions

    Aim: Scan a longer text (350 words) efficiently for specific details to complete a matching task — mirrors real exam and real-life reading.

    Article extract & matching task

    Article profiles 5 career paths: UX Designer / Data Analyst / Sustainable Agriculture Manager / Mental Health Counsellor / Renewable Energy Engineer — each with: required skills, work environment, salary range, growth outlook.

    Candidate descriptions (match to job):

    1. Ana — maths degree; introverted; loves patterns in data; works best alone. → Data Analyst
    2. Riko — empathetic; studied psychology; wants to help people directly. → Counsellor
    3. Tom — creative; loves digital products; wants to make technology user-friendly. → UX Designer
    4. Sara — passionate about climate; studied engineering; outdoor work preferred. → Renewable Energy Engineer
    5. James — from a farming family; studied environmental science; entrepreneurial. → Agriculture Manager

    Extension: Students write 3 sentences explaining why they matched one candidate — using evidence from the text. This bridges to the writing skill.

    5

    Science Article — Understanding Charts & Data in Text

    ⏱ 35 min👥 Pairs🎚 B1.2🧰 Article with embedded data (stats, percentages), chart

    Aim: Read statistical data presented in prose and relate it to a visual — a workplace and academic literacy skill.

    Text excerpt & tasks
    "According to the 2023 Global Wellness Index, 67% of adults in high-income countries report sleeping fewer than seven hours per night — the minimum recommended by sleep scientists. The figures are more alarming among younger adults: 18 to 34-year-olds average just 6.1 hours, compared to 7.4 hours among those aged 55 and over. Researchers link chronic sleep deprivation to a 40% increase in the risk of cardiovascular disease, a 33% reduction in cognitive performance the following day, and — perhaps most surprisingly — a measurable decrease in the body's immune response within just three days of poor sleep."

    Tasks:

    1. How much less sleep do young adults get compared to older adults?
    2. List the three health risks mentioned. Which do you find most alarming?
    3. Draw a simple bar chart showing the age-group sleep comparison.
    4. The word "alarming" appears twice. Is this a factual or evaluative word? What does it tell you about the writer's attitude?
    5. Write one sentence summarising the data without using any numbers from the text.
    6

    Opinion Column — Identifying Argument Structure

    ⏱ 35 min👥 Individual → pairs🎚 B1.2🧰 400-word opinion column, structure template

    Aim: Map the argument structure of an opinion piece — thesis, evidence, counter-argument, rebuttal, conclusion — as preparation for producing the same structure in writing.

    Column excerpt & mapping task
    Topic: "Should smartphones be banned in schools?"

    Para 1 (Thesis): The debate over smartphones in classrooms has moved beyond distraction. Evidence now suggests a more serious problem: the systematic erosion of adolescent attention spans.

    Para 2 (Evidence 1): A 2023 meta-analysis of 72 studies found that the mere presence of a phone on a desk — even face down and silent — reduced cognitive capacity by an average of 20%...

    Para 3 (Counter-argument): Proponents of smartphone access in schools argue that devices are powerful learning tools: calculators, dictionaries, research portals...

    Para 4 (Rebuttal): This argument has merit for older students in specific subjects. But evidence that unrestricted access improves average academic outcomes is thin...

    Para 5 (Conclusion): A nuanced policy — restricted access, specific apps, defined times — seems more sensible than a blanket ban.

    Students label each paragraph's function and answer: "Is the author's conclusion consistent with the evidence presented? Do you agree?"

    7

    Short Story Extract — Reading for Feeling & Technique

    ⏱ 35 min👥 Groups of 3🎚 B1.2🧰 Fiction extract (200 words), annotation key

    Aim: Move beyond comprehension to literary analysis — identifying how a writer creates atmosphere, character, and tension through word choice.

    Extract & annotation tasks
    "She had not planned to stay long. The café was the kind of place that keeps its lights low on purpose — the kind that hopes you'll confuse intimacy with quality. She ordered a coffee she didn't want and looked for the door three times before her phone rang. It was him. She let it ring. Outside, the rain had started without asking."

    Annotation tasks (colour-coded):

    1. Circle words that describe the character's state of mind.
    2. Underline the sentences that use irony or indirect criticism.
    3. Star the most striking phrase. Explain why.
    4. Box the last sentence. What technique is this? (Pathetic fallacy.) What does it tell us?
    5. Write the next 3 sentences continuing the story — matching the tone and style.
    8

    Vocabulary from Context — Word Detective

    ⏱ 25 min👥 Pairs🎚 B1.1🧰 Article with 10 underlined words, deduction steps card

    Aim: Build the habit of intelligent guessing from context — the single most important reading skill for acquiring vocabulary independently.

    Deduction steps & practice text

    4-step deduction method (teach explicitly):

    1. What part of speech is it? (noun/verb/adjective/adverb)
    2. What does the sentence around it tell me?
    3. What does the wider paragraph context add?
    4. Replace the word with your guess — does the sentence still make sense?
    "The city's infrastructure had been neglected for decades. Roads were crumbling, water pipes antiquated, and the public transport system so erratic that commuters had taken to improvising — cycling, car-sharing, walking — rather than relying on a bus that might arrive at any of several unpredictable times. The mayor's new initiative promised change, but residents remained sceptical, having heard such rhetoric before. The discrepancy between promise and reality had become a familiar feature of city life."

    Students work through each underlined word using the 4-step method. Then use 3 of the words in their own original sentences on a different topic.

    9

    Gapped Text — Coherence & Cohesion Practice

    ⏱ 30 min👥 Individual → pairs🎚 B1.2🧰 Article with 5 removed sentences, bank of 6 options

    Aim: Choose the correct missing sentence from a bank — requires understanding of text cohesion, pronoun reference, and logical flow. Mirrors B1/B2 exam tasks.

    Procedure & teaching points
    1. Read once for gist (ignoring the gaps) to get the overall topic and structure.
    2. Analyse the gap: What comes before? What comes after? What grammatical structure would fit?
    3. Test each option: Read it in context — does the pronoun reference work? Does the topic flow?
    4. Eliminate: One option is always a distractor that fits grammatically but not logically.

    Key teaching point: At B1, the gap is usually signalled by a discourse marker just before or after it. Teach students to look for these bridges.

    Debrief by asking students to explain their reasoning for each choice — metalinguistic awareness is as important as the correct answer.

    10

    Extensive Reading Report — Sustained Independent Reading

    ⏱ Ongoing (10 min per week + homework)👥 Individual🎚 B1.1–2🧰 Graded reader or authentic short book

    Aim: Build reading stamina, vocabulary depth, and genuine engagement with English through sustained independent reading — the highest-return reading activity for B1 learners.

    Programme structure & reading journal prompts

    Setup: Each student chooses a graded reader (Level 4–5: Penguin, Oxford Bookworms, Cambridge Readers) or an authentic short non-fiction book on a topic they find genuinely interesting. 20 minutes of reading per day as homework. One class reading report per fortnight.

    Reading journal entry prompts (choose 2 per week):

    • Summarise what you read in 4 sentences.
    • Find one word or phrase you didn't know. How did you work out the meaning?
    • Did anything surprise you? Did you agree or disagree with anything?
    • What would you say to a character or person in this text?
    • Connect what you read to something in your own life.
    • Rate this section: 1–5 ⭐. Give reasons.

    Fortnightly class activity (10 min): Book-talk circles — 4 students, each with a different book, shares one interesting idea. Others ask questions. Pure spoken production, zero pressure, maximum engagement.

    Negotiation Simulation — Planning a Team Event

    ⏱ 35 min👥 Groups of 4–5🎚 B1.1🧰 Budget card, preference cards, venue options

    Setup & language focus

    Setup: Your team has a budget of £500 for an end-of-project celebration (10 people). You need to choose: Venue (restaurant / escape room / city tour / picnic in the park / cooking class).     Language targets:

    • Suggesting: "What about…? / How about we…? / I think we should consider…"
    • Conceding: "I see your point, but… / That's a fair point, however…"
    • Compromising: "What if we…? / Could we split the budget between…?"
    • Reaching agreement: "So we all agree on…? / Can everyone live with that?"

    Socratic Seminar — Text-Based Discussion

    ⏱ 40 min👥 Whole class (inner/outer circle)🎚 B1.2🧰 Short provocative text (shared reading), discussion question cards

    Aim: Sustain a teacher-free, text-based academic discussion — develops intellectual stamina and complex spoken interaction simultaneously.

    Procedure & question types
    1. Read (10 min). All students read the same short text (150–200 words) independently and annotate: circle 1 word that interests them; underline 1 idea they want to discuss; put ? next to something they don't understand.
    2. Inner circle (12 min). 5–6 students sit in a circle. They discuss the text — teacher does NOT intervene. Outer circle observes and notes: new ideas introduced / agreements / who built on whose ideas / any unanswered questions.
    3. Switch (12 min). Inner becomes outer. Outer discusses, using the notes from observing.
    4. Whole class debrief (6 min). What new understanding emerged? What was unresolved?

    Question types to prompt if discussion stalls: Opening (What does the author mean by…?) / Core (Why does this matter?) / Closing (What would you do differently if…?)

    StrategyPhrase
    Paraphrase attempt"So what you're saying is… is that right?"

    Opinion Statement — 1 Minute, No Preparation

    Procedure & opinion frame

    Student draws a card, has 5 seconds to read it, and speaks for exactly 60 seconds. The class listens and gives a thumbs up/sideways/down for: (a) Was the opinion clear? (b) Were there reasons? (c) Was it interesting?

    Opinion frame (on the board): Position → Reason → Example → Nuance → Conclusion. (PRENCO)

    Example topics: "Travelling teaches you more than school." / "Social media is making people less creative." / "It's never too late to change careers." / "Animals should not be kept in zoos." / "Money can buy happiness."

    Structure, connector bank & model paragraph

    Essay structure (200–240 words):

    1. Introduction — introduce the topic, explain why it is debated (no opinion yet).
    2. Arguments for — 2–3 points, each with a reason or example.
    3. Arguments against — 2–3 counter-points.
    4. Conclusion — your balanced view, supported with one final reason.

    Connectors bank (categorised):

    AddingContrastingConcedingConcluding

    Formal Email — Making a Request or Complaint

    Formal email conventions:

    • Opening: Dear Mr/Ms [Surname] (not Hi!)

    • Use full forms — no contractions (I would, not I'd).
    • Closing: I look forward to hearing from you. / Yours sincerely (if you know the name) / Yours faithfully (if you don't).

    Scenarios:

    1. You attended a language course that was not as described in the brochure. Write to the director requesting a partial refund.
    2. You want to do work experience at a company. Write to the HR Manager explaining who you are, why you are interested, and what you can offer.
    3. You ordered furniture online; it arrived damaged. Write to the company requesting replacement or compensation.

    Peer editing focus: Register consistency (has any informal language crept in?) / Is the purpose clear in paragraph 1? / Does every paragraph have one clear function?

    Narrative Writing — Using Complex Time Sequences

    ⏱ 45 min👥 Individual🎚 B1.2🧰 Tense timeline, narrative prompt

    Aim: Write a short story or personal narrative (200 words) that correctly sequences past simple, past continuous, and past perfect.

    Tense guide, prompt & error focus

    Tense guide (display on board):

    TenseUse in narrativeExample
    Past simpleMain story events (the sequence)"She walked in. He turned around."
    Past continuousBackground / interrupted action"It was raining when she arrived."
    Past perfectEvents before the main story time"She had never seen him before."

    Paragraph Surgery — Rewriting Weak Texts

    ⏱ 35 min👥 Pairs🎚 B1.1🧰 "Before" paragraphs, improvement checklist

    Aim: Diagnose and improve weak writing — building the self-editing skill that transforms B1 writers into B2 writers.

    Before paragraphs & surgery technique
    Weak paragraph 1: "I think social media is bad. It wastes time. People spend a lot of time on it. It can cause problems. Young people especially use it a lot. I think there should be rules about it."

    Surgery checklist:

    • Is there a clear topic sentence?
    • Is every sentence connected to the next?
    • Are there any specific examples?
    • Are connectors used?
    • Is the vocabulary precise and varied?
    • Is there a concluding sentence?

    After (model): "Social media, while useful for communication, can have a damaging effect on users' mental health. Research consistently shows that adolescents who spend more than three hours daily on social platforms report significantly higher rates of anxiety and low self-esteem. This is largely due to the culture of comparison that these apps actively encourage. For this reason, many experts argue that age restrictions should be more strictly enforced — a position that is difficult to argue against."

    Pairs rewrite the "before" paragraph, then compare their version to the model. They then apply the same surgery to a second weak paragraph on a different topic.

    8

    Informal Letter — Keeping in Touch

    ⏱ 40 min👥 Individual🎚 B1.1🧰 Letter prompt, register checklist

    Aim: Write an engaging informal letter (160–200 words) — describing experiences, asking questions, and maintaining a natural, warm tone.

    Structure, language & register tips

    Prompt: You haven't been in touch with a friend from school for two years. Write to them describing what has changed in your life, one thing you are planning to do, and asking them 3 genuine questions.

    Informal letter register features:

    • Contractions: I'm, I've, you'd, it's
    • Conversational phrases: "By the way…" / "Anyway…" / "You'll never believe…"
    • Questions that show real interest: "How's your mum? Is she still teaching?"
    • References to shared memories: "Do you remember when we…?"
    • Warm closing: "Write back soon!" / "Miss you loads." / "Can't wait to hear your news."

    Register checklist (peer edit): Are contractions used? Is it overly formal anywhere? Does it feel like a real letter from a real person? Are the questions genuinely interesting, not just formulaic?

    9

    Review Writing — Film, Book, or Restaurant

    ⏱ 45 min👥 Individual → class display🎚 B1.1🧰 Review structure, evaluative vocabulary bank

    Aim: Write an engaging, balanced review (170–200 words) using a range of evaluative language and a reader-oriented tone.

    Structure, vocabulary & model paragraph

    Review structure:

    1. Introduction — what is it? Brief context (don't reveal plot endings).
    2. What works well — 2 specific positive points with brief reasoning.
    3. What could be better — 1–2 honest critiques (avoids being purely promotional).
    4. Who would enjoy it — specific recommendation for a type of reader/viewer.
    5. Rating — justify in one sentence.

    Evaluative vocabulary bank:

    10

    Portfolio Reflection — "My Writing This Term"

    ⏱ 40 min (end of course)👥 Individual🎚 B1.1–2🧰 Portfolio of all written work, reflection prompts

    Aim: Write a metacognitive reflection (150–180 words) on their own progress — simultaneously practising connected writing AND developing self-awareness as a language learner.

    Reflection prompts & value

    Reflection prompts (choose 4):

    • Compare your first piece of writing to your most recent. What has changed?
    • Which writing task was most challenging? Why?
    • Which task are you most proud of? What makes it successful?
    • What grammar or vocabulary area do you still need to work on?
    • What strategy do you use when you don't know a word?
    • What kind of writing do you want to get better at next — and why?

    Pedagogical value: This task achieves three things at once: it produces genuine connected writing, it develops learner autonomy, and it gives the teacher richer feedback than any test. A well-written portfolio reflection is often a learner's best piece of writing because the topic is one they know perfectly — themselves.

    Share reflections in a "learning circle" (groups of 3) — each person shares one thing they discovered about themselves as a writer. Powerful closing activity for the B1 course.

    🧰 Resources & Materials Checklist

    Classroom essentials

    • 🔊 Clear audio playback at 120–140 wpm (natural conversational pace for B1)
    • 📰 A stock of short authentic newspaper/magazine articles (Guardian, BBC, The Conversation)
    • 📸 Photo bank: 20+ high-quality images on societal themes (for photo description tasks)
    • 🃏 Card banks: opinion statements, debate motions, dilemma scenarios, story twists
    • 📓 Student error log notebooks (one each, kept all course)
    • ⏱ Class timer (visible countdown for timed speaking and writing tasks)

    Digital & online resources

    • The Conversation (theconversation.com) — accessible academic articles on current topics, free
    • BBC Learning English — Intermediate — audio, video, grammar in context
    • TED Talks (ted.com) — with subtitles off; 5-minute clips recommended
    • Cambridge B1 Preliminary sample papers — authentic exam practice
    • Quizlet / Anki — student vocabulary study between sessions
    • Graded readers (Level 5–6): Penguin Active Reading, Oxford Bookworms Stage 3–4
    ⚠️
    Trainer reminders for B1. At this level, reduce teacher talking time to under 30% of each session — learners must generate language, not receive it. Treat errors as data, not failures: collect, analyse, and address patterns rather than correcting in the moment during fluency tasks. The biggest B1 trap is plateauing — keep raising the challenge by 10% each week. Expose learners to authentic input consistently, even if it's slightly above their level: discomfort is where acquisition happens.
    ✅
    End-of-B1 readiness benchmark. The learner can: maintain a conversation on any familiar topic for 5+ minutes without teacher support; write a structured 200-word essay or report with coherent paragraphs; understand the main argument of an authentic radio or podcast segment; narrate a complex past event using past simple, continuous, and perfect correctly. They handle unexpected situations with language rather than avoidance. They are ready for B2 / Upper-Intermediate challenges.
