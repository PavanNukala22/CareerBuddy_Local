2. Grammar in use

Structures that move your writing and speaking from "understandable" to "polished" — each one common in real, everyday use, not just textbooks.

B2 · Intermediate

The passive voice for objectivity

Use the passive (be + past participle) when the action matters more than who did it — very common in reports, instructions, and formal updates.

✓"The report was submitted yesterday." — sounds neutral and professional.
✗"Someone submitted the report yesterday." — vaguer and less natural in a formal update.
✓"New guidelines have been introduced to reduce errors."

Common mistake: forgetting the correct form of "be." Not "The report submitted yesterday" — you need "was submitted."

B2 · Intermediate

Reported speech with modal shifts

When you report what someone said, modal verbs often shift back: will→would, can→could, must→had to. This is essential for relaying meetings and instructions accurately.

✓"I can finish it today" → She said she could finish it that day.
✗She said she can finish it today. — mixes present reporting verb with no time/modal shift, sounds like direct speech left unchanged.
✓"You must submit the form" → He told us we had to submit the form.

Tip: the shift isn't automatic if the situation is still true or general — "She said the office opens at 9" can stay unchanged.

C1 · Advanced

Mixed conditionals

Mixed conditionals combine a past condition with a present result, or a present condition with a past result — one of the clearest markers of advanced-level fluency.

✓"If I had taken that job offer, I would be living in Singapore now." (past condition → present result)
✓"If she weren't so risk-averse, she would have invested in the startup." (present condition → past result)
✗"If I had taken that job, I would living in Singapore now." — missing "be."

Tip: ask which timeframe the condition is in and which timeframe the result is in — they don't have to match.

C1 · Advanced

Inversion for emphasis

Starting a sentence with a negative or limiting adverbial (not only, rarely, no sooner, seldom, under no circumstances) and inverting the subject and auxiliary adds formal emphasis.

✓"Not only did we hit the target, but we exceeded it by 12%."
✓"Under no circumstances should customer data be shared without consent."
✗"Not only we hit the target..." — the auxiliary "did" must come before the subject.

Where to use it: presentations, formal reports, persuasive writing. Overusing it in casual conversation sounds stiff.

C1 · Advanced

Cleft sentences for emphasis

"It is/was ... that ..." and "What ... is/was ..." let you highlight exactly which part of a sentence matters most — a natural way advanced speakers add emphasis without raising their voice.

✓"It was the delayed shipment that caused the client complaint" — not the pricing, not the design, specifically the shipment.
✓"What we need is a clearer approval process, not more meetings."
✗"It was the delayed shipment who caused the client complaint." — use "that," not "who," for a non-person subject like this.

Tip: cleft sentences are especially useful in writing when you can't rely on stress or intonation to show emphasis.

The Quiet Return to the Office

When remote work became the norm in 2020, many predicted the traditional office would never fully recover. Five years on, the picture is more complicated. Some companies have gone fully remote and never looked back; others have mandated a full return, citing lost collaboration and a weaker sense of company culture. The majority, though, have settled somewhere in between — a hybrid model, typically two or three days in the office, with the rest worked from home.

What's interesting is not the split itself but the reasoning behind it. Employees consistently report that the flexibility of remote work outweighs almost any other benefit, including salary increases of up to 8%, according to several recent workplace surveys. Employers, meanwhile, increasingly argue that junior staff in particular lose out when they aren't physically present to observe how experienced colleagues handle difficult situations — a kind of informal learning that's hard to replicate over video calls.

Neither side is entirely wrong. The real shift may be less about where people work and more about how companies measure performance: fewer are tracking hours, and more are tracking outcomes.

2. Grammar in use

Structures that move your writing and speaking from "understandable" to "polished" — each one common in real, everyday use, not just textbooks.

B2 · Intermediate

Present perfect vs. past simple for tech updates

Use the present perfect when the exact time doesn't matter or the result still matters now; use the past simple for a finished action at a specific past time.

✓"Have you updated the app?" — asking about the current state, time unspecified.
✓"I updated it yesterday morning." — specific, finished action.
✗"I have updated it yesterday." — don't mix present perfect with a specific past time word like "yesterday."

Quick check: if a specific time word appears (yesterday, in 2023, last week), use the past simple.

C1 · Advanced

Relative clauses in technical description

Non-defining relative clauses (set off by commas) add extra detail about a system or problem without interrupting the main point — very common in technical writing and status updates.

✓"The bug, which affected roughly 3% of users, was fixed within a day."
✓"The vendor, whose platform we've used since 2021, is releasing a major update next month."
✗"The bug, that affected 3% of users, was fixed." — use "which," not "that," in a non-defining clause set off by commas.

Tip: if you can remove the clause and the sentence still makes full sense, it's non-defining — use commas and "which/whose," not "that."

C1 · Advanced

The causative: have/get something done

Use "have/get + object + past participle" when someone else performs an action for you — extremely common when describing outsourced or delegated technical work.

✓"We had the system upgraded last month." (someone else did it for us)
✓"We're getting the servers migrated to the cloud this quarter."
✗"We had upgraded the system last month" (if a vendor did it) — this implies you did it yourself; use the causative instead.

Tip: compare "I upgraded the system" (I did it) with "I had the system upgraded" (someone else did it, on my instruction).

When the AI Stopped Being Optional

For most of the last decade, adopting artificial intelligence tools at work was framed as a competitive advantage — something ambitious companies did to get ahead. That framing has quietly shifted. In a growing number of industries, AI-assisted tools for drafting, coding, and data analysis are no longer a differentiator; they're closer to a baseline expectation, not unlike having a reliable internet connection.

This shift has changed what employers look for. Fewer job postings ask specifically for 'AI experience' — instead, the expectation is folded into ordinary tasks. A marketing role might list 'ability to produce first drafts efficiently' rather than naming any particular tool. For employees, this can be freeing: the tool matters less than the judgement used to direct and check it.

The risk, several workplace researchers note, is a widening gap between people who use these tools to remove genuinely repetitive work and those who use them as a substitute for understanding the underlying task at all. The systems that will matter most in the next few years, most agree, are not the AI tools themselves but the processes companies build around checking their output.

2. Grammar in use

Structures that move your writing and speaking from "understandable" to "polished" — each one common in real, everyday use, not just textbooks.

B2 · Intermediate

Quantifiers with uncountable nouns

"Money" is uncountable, so it takes quantifiers like "much," "a great deal of," "a large amount of" — not "many" or "a number of," which are for countable nouns.

✓"We don't have much money left in the budget."
✓"They spent a great deal of money on the rebrand."
✗"We don't have many money left." — "money" is uncountable, so "many" is wrong here; use "much."

Tip: "a lot of" works with both countable and uncountable nouns, so it's a safe default if you're unsure.

C1 · Advanced

The third conditional for financial regret

The third conditional (If + past perfect, ... would have + past participle) describes an unreal past situation and its unreal past result — extremely common when discussing missed financial opportunities.

✓"If we had invested earlier, we would have doubled our return."
✓"She wouldn't have lost money if she had diversified her portfolio."
✗"If we would have invested earlier, we would have doubled our return." — don't use "would have" in the if-clause; use the past perfect ("had invested").

Tip: the if-clause always uses the past perfect (had + past participle), never "would have."

C1 · Advanced

Nominalisation for formal writing

Turning a verb into a noun (nominalisation) makes writing sound more formal and objective — standard in financial reports, though it should be used with restraint in speech.

✓Instead of "We reduced costs by 12%," write "The reduction of costs by 12% reflected..."
✓Instead of "We will invest more," write "Further investment is planned for Q3."
✗Overusing nominalisation in spoken conversation — "The implementation of the reduction" sounds unnatural if you're just chatting with a colleague.

Tip: nominalisation belongs in reports and formal writing — dial it back in speech, or you'll sound stiff.

Why 'Cheaper' Isn't Always the Better Deal

When budgets tighten, the instinct is to chase the lowest price. But a growing body of research into corporate purchasing suggests this instinct often backfires. Teams that select suppliers purely on cost report, on average, higher long-term spending than teams that weigh cost alongside reliability and total cost of ownership — the full expense of a decision once maintenance, delays, and replacement are factored in.

A cheaper vendor that misses deadlines can end up costing more once the knock-on effects are added up: a delayed shipment might mean overtime pay to catch up, or a discount offered to a frustrated client. None of this appears on the original invoice, which is exactly why it's so often missed.

Financial analysts increasingly recommend that procurement decisions be made using a simple three-year total cost estimate rather than a single upfront figure. It rarely changes the final decision entirely, but it consistently changes how confidently teams can defend that decision later.

Future forms for travel plans

"Going to" for intentions already decided, present continuous for fixed arrangements, and "will" for spontaneous decisions or predictions — choosing the right one signals how settled a plan is.

✓"We're going to visit Japan next spring." — a decided intention.
✓"We're flying out on the 14th." — a fixed, booked arrangement.
✗"We will fly out on the 14th" for something already booked — "will" undersells how fixed the plan actually is; the present continuous fits better.

Tip: if tickets are booked, prefer the present continuous over "will" — it signals the plan is already fixed.

C1 · Advanced

Formal suggestion structures with the subjunctive

In formal writing (guides, official advice, recommendations), verbs like "suggest," "recommend," and "essential" are often followed by the subjunctive base form — the same for all subjects, with no "-s."

✓"It is essential that travellers obtain a visa in advance." (not "obtains")
✓"We recommend that visitors book excursions ahead of peak season."
✗"It is essential that travellers obtains a visa." — the subjunctive base form doesn't take "-s," even for third person singular.

Tip: this structure is mostly for formal or written advice — everyday spoken English often just uses "should" instead.

C1 · Advanced

Participle clauses for concise narrative

Present or past participle clauses (Having + past participle / -ing) let you combine two related actions into one smoother sentence — common in descriptive travel writing.

✓"Having arrived late, we missed the first excursion." (= because we arrived late)
✓"Walking through the old quarter, we noticed how quiet it had become."
✗"Having arrive late, we missed the excursion." — the participle needs the past participle form: "arrived," not the base form.

Tip: use these to avoid repeating "we" or "I" twice in one sentence — they tighten up narrative writing noticeably.

The Rise of the Slower Trip

For much of the last twenty years, travel was measured in checklists: how many countries, how many landmarks, how efficiently a trip could be planned. That mindset is now being quietly challenged by a growing number of travellers choosing fewer destinations and longer stays — sometimes a single region for the length of what used to be a multi-country tour.

The reasons are practical as much as philosophical. Constant movement between cities is exhausting and expensive, and many travellers report that they remember a place more vividly after staying five nights than after a rushed 24-hour visit. There's also a growing awareness of the strain that high-volume, short-stay tourism places on popular destinations, from housing costs to overcrowded landmarks.

Tourism boards in several regions have started actively promoting this shift, offering incentives for longer stays and highlighting excursions that are genuinely off the beaten track rather than the same handful of landmarks every visitor photographs. Whether this becomes a lasting shift in habits, rather than a passing trend, is something the industry is still watching closely.

4. Expressions & idioms

Natural phrases that native speakers use constantly. Recognising and using these is often what separates "technically correct" English from English that sounds effortless.

Expression Meaning Example
off the beaten track away from places usually visited by tourists "We found a fantastic little café off the beaten track."
live out of a suitcase to travel constantly, without a settled home base "After three months of conferences, she felt like she was living out of a suitcase."
catch some rays to spend time relaxing in the sun "We spent the afternoon on the beach catching some rays."
jet-set to travel frequently and glamorously, often for business or leisure "He's part of the jet-set crowd, flying somewhere new every month."
a home away from home a place where you feel as comfortable as at home "That little guesthouse became a home away from home for us."
see the sights to visit the famous or notable places in an area "We only had one day, so we just saw the sights quickly."
on a shoestring (budget) travelling or living with very little money "They backpacked across Europe on a shoestring."
a bucket list a list of things you want to do before you die "Visiting the northern lights has always been on my bucket list."

5. Writing task

Task: Write a short recommendation email (120–180 words) to a friend or colleague suggesting a destination for their next trip, based on their interests. Use at least two words from the vocabulary list and one grammar structure from Section 2.

  • Open by referring to what they told you they're looking for, so it feels personal, not generic
  • Recommend one destination clearly, not a vague list of five
  • Include one specific, non-obvious detail (a neighbourhood, a season, a local custom)
  • Mention one practical consideration honestly (cost, best time to go, one drawback)
  • End with an inviting, low-pressure closing line
✍️
Model opening line (B2): "I think you'd really enjoy Lisbon — the food is great and it's not too expensive."
Model opening line (C1): "Given what you said about wanting somewhere cosmopolitan but still affordable, I'd genuinely recommend Lisbon — ideally staying long enough to get off the beaten track beyond the main tram routes."
