    Pattern Recognition (Non-Verbal) High Weight

    Figure series, rotation, reflection, shading, odd one out, embedded figures, paper folding, cube/dice visualization.

    Rotation & Reflection Patterns

    The most common non-verbal pattern involves a figure rotating by a fixed angle (45°, 90°, 135°, 180°) at each step, or reflecting (flipping) across a horizontal or vertical axis. Always check: does the shape spin clockwise or anti-clockwise? Does it flip?

    Fig 1.1 — Triangle rotates 90° clockwise each step. After ◀ it returns to ▲.
    📗 Example 1 — 90° Rotation

    In the series above, the arrow rotates 90° clockwise each step: Up → Right → Down → Left → Up. The answer is the upward-pointing triangle, completing one full 360° cycle.

    📗 Example 2 — Reflection Pattern

    A figure shows: L, Γ, L, Γ, ? — the letter "L" alternates with its mirror image "Γ" (horizontal reflection). Answer: L.

    Fig 1.2 — Shading increases by 25% each step, then cycles back to empty.
    📗 Example 3 — Progressive Shading

    Each square gains an extra quarter of shading. After reaching 100% (fully dark), the pattern resets to 0% (empty). Answer: Empty square.

    📗 Example 4 — Element Addition

    Step 1: One dot. Step 2: Two dots. Step 3: Three dots forming a triangle. Step 4: Four dots forming a square. Step 5: Five dots forming a pentagon. The number of dots increases by 1 and they arrange into the next regular polygon.

    Odd One Out

    Identify the figure that doesn't belong. Common differentiators: all others rotate the same way, all others have the same number of sides, all others are shaded the same way, all others are open/closed, or all share a symmetry axis the odd one doesn't.

    📗 Example 5 — Odd One Out by Symmetry

    Figures: (A) Circle (B) Square (C) Equilateral Triangle (D) Regular Pentagon (E) Parallelogram.

    All except (E) have at least one line of symmetry. A parallelogram has NO lines of symmetry — only rotational symmetry. Answer: (E).

    📗 Example 6 — Odd One Out by Count

    Five figures each contain small shapes inside a box: (A) 3 circles (B) 3 triangles (C) 3 squares (D) 4 diamonds (E) 3 stars. All have 3 elements except (D) which has 4. Answer: (D).

    Embedded & Hidden Figures

    You are given a simple target shape and a complex figure. You must find where the target shape is hidden within the complex figure, maintaining exact size and orientation.

    Fig 1.3 — The target rectangle with diagonal is embedded in the complex figure (shown with a gold dashed outline).
    📗 Example 7 — Embedded Figure

    The target shape (rectangle with a diagonal from top-left to bottom-right) can be found in the middle of the complex figure. The key is to ignore extra lines and circles that distract.

    Paper Folding & Cutting

    A square paper is folded one or more times, then a piece is cut or a hole is punched. When unfolded, the cuts/holes appear symmetrically across each fold line.

    📗 Example 8 — Paper Folding

    A square paper is folded in half (top to bottom). A small triangle is cut from the folded edge at the center. When unfolded: the triangle cut appears on BOTH the top half and bottom half, forming a diamond/rhombus shape at the center.

    Rule: Each fold creates a mirror. 1 fold = 2 copies. 2 folds = 4 copies. The cuts mirror across every fold line.

    Cube & Dice Visualization

    Fig 1.4 — Standard cross-shaped cube net. Faces separated by exactly one face in a straight line are opposite.
    📗 Example 9 — Cube Net Opposite Faces

    In the cross net above: Face 3 is the center. Face 1 is above, Face 6 is below → 1 opposite 6. Face 2 is left, Face 4 is right → 2 opposite 4. Face 5 is at the far right → 3 opposite 5.

    📗 Example 10 — Dice Problem

    A standard die has opposite faces summing to 7. If you see faces 2 and 3 on two visible faces, the face opposite to 2 is 5 and opposite to 3 is 4. The hidden bottom face can be deduced from the orientation.

    📗 Example 11 — Water Image

    A water image is the reflection of a figure in a horizontal mirror placed below it. The word "LOGIC" becomes: L stays L, O stays O, G becomes G, I stays I, C stays C — but the entire word flips upside-down while left-right stays the same. Letters like A, M, T, U, V, W, Y look the same in water images.

    Solved Exercises

    📘 Exercise 1

    A figure series shows an arrow: ↑, ↗, →, ↘, ↓, ↙, ←, ?. What comes next?

    The arrow rotates 45° clockwise each step. After ← (pointing left), the next 45° step gives ↖ (north-west), then back to ↑.

    📘 Exercise 2

    Figures: (A) Pentagon (B) Hexagon (C) Circle (D) Octagon (E) Heptagon. Which is the odd one out?

    All are polygons except (C) Circle, which has no sides or vertices. Answer: (C)

    📘 Exercise 3

    A square paper is folded diagonally once (making a triangle), then a small circle is punched at the center of the hypotenuse. How many holes appear when unfolded?

    One fold means 1 mirror line. The punch on the fold line produces 1 hole (it's on the fold itself, so both layers share it).

    📘 Exercise 4

    On a cube net: Top row has face A. Middle row (left to right): B, C, D, E. Bottom row has face F — directly under C. Which face is opposite to C?

    In a cross-shaped net, the center face and the face 2 steps away in a straight line are opposite. A is above C, F is below C. But A and F are only 1 step from C. So we look at the row: B-C-D-E. B is opposite D (gap of 1 from C on each side), and A is opposite F. C is opposite E (the face 2 positions to its right).

    📘 Exercise 5

    A series: ○, ◐, ●, ◐, ○, ◐, ?. What's next?

    Pattern: empty → half → full → half → empty → half → ● (full). The cycle is ○→◐→●→◐ repeating.

    📘 Exercise 6

    A figure series: Square with 1 inner circle → Square with 2 inner circles → Triangle with 3 inner circles → Triangle with 4 inner circles → Pentagon with 5 inner circles → ?

    Two patterns: (1) Inner circles increase by 1 each step. (2) Outer shape: Square(2), Triangle(2), Pentagon — changes every 2 steps with increasing sides. Next: Pentagon with 6 inner circles.

    📘 Exercise 7

    Which letter looks the same in its water image? (A) M (B) R (C) G (D) L

    Water image = horizontal reflection. Letters with horizontal symmetry look the same: B, C, D, E, H, I, K, O, X. M has vertical symmetry, not horizontal. But wait — in water reflection, M becomes W-like (flipped upside-down). None of these have perfect horizontal symmetry. However, conventionally, letters like C, D, E, H, I, O, X are water-image invariant. Among options: None is perfect, but the closest typical answer is context-dependent. If simplified: (A) M is often accepted in basic exams since it appears similar when reflected.

    📘 Exercise 8

    A square paper is folded twice (in half, then in half again forming a quarter). A corner cut is made at the doubly-folded corner. How many holes appear when unfolded?

    2 folds = 4 layers. Cut at the folded corner = the cut appears in the center of the original paper. When unfolded: 1 hole at the center (all 4 layers shared that point).

    📘 Exercise 9

    A die shows: Top = 3, Front = 5. What is the bottom face?

    On a standard die, opposite faces sum to 7. Bottom = 7 − 3 = 4.

    📘 Exercise 10

    A figure series has shapes: △ with 1 dot, □ with 2 dots, ⬠ with 3 dots, ⬡ with 4 dots, ?. The outer shapes have 3, 4, 5, 6 sides. What comes next?

    Next shape: 7 sides (heptagon) with 5 dots. Answer: Heptagon with 5 dots inside.

    Deductive Reasoning

    Syllogisms, Venn diagrams, logical connectives (if-then, unless, either-or), assumptions, conclusions, cause & effect.

    Syllogisms & Venn Diagrams

    A syllogism consists of two premises and a conclusion. We test whether the conclusion necessarily follows from the premises using Venn diagrams.

    Fig 2.1 — The four categorical statement types (A, E, I, O) with Venn representations.
    📗 Example 1 — Basic Syllogism

    Premise 1: All dogs are animals. Premise 2: All animals are living things.

    Conclusion: All dogs are living things → Valid (dogs ⊂ animals ⊂ living things).

    📗 Example 2 — Syllogism with Particular

    Premise 1: All roses are flowers. Premise 2: Some flowers are red.

    Conclusion: Some roses are red → Invalid. The "some flowers" that are red may not include roses. We cannot guarantee overlap.

    📗 Example 3 — No + All Combination

    Premise 1: No fish is a bird. Premise 2: All sparrows are birds.

    Conclusion: No sparrow is a fish → Valid. Sparrows ⊂ Birds, and Birds ∩ Fish = ∅, so Sparrows ∩ Fish = ∅.

    Logical Connectives

    📐 Key Logical Translations
    StatementLogical Meaning
    If P then QP → Q. Contrapositive: ¬Q → ¬P
    Only if"P only if Q" means P → Q
    Unless"P unless Q" means "If not Q, then P" (¬Q → P)
    Either P or QAt least one is true (inclusive or)
    Neither P nor QBoth are false: ¬P ∧ ¬Q
    📗 Example 4 — If-Then

    "If it rains, the ground is wet." It rained. ∴ Ground is wet → Valid (Modus Ponens).

    The ground is wet. ∴ It rained → Invalid (Affirming the consequent — sprinkler could cause wetness).

    📗 Example 5 — Unless

    "You will fail unless you study." Translation: If you don't study → you will fail. Contrapositive: If you didn't fail → you studied.

    📗 Example 6 — Only If

    "The match will be played only if it doesn't rain." Meaning: Match played → No rain. Contrapositive: Rain → No match. So if it rains, no match.

    Assumptions & Conclusions

    📗 Example 7 — Hidden Assumption

    Statement: "Government should ban junk food to improve public health."

    Assumption: Junk food is a significant cause of poor public health. (Without this assumption, the statement makes no sense.)

    📗 Example 8 — Conclusion vs. Inference

    Statement: "Sales increased 40% after the ad campaign." Conclusion: The campaign was effective → Possible but not certain (other factors may have contributed). Inference: Sales were lower before the campaign → Definitely true.

    Cause & Effect

    📗 Example 9 — Cause and Effect

    Statement I: The river flooded. Statement II: Heavy rainfall occurred for 3 days.

    II is the cause, I is the effect. II causes I.

    📗 Example 10 — Independent Events

    Statement I: Stock market crashed. Statement II: A new coffee shop opened downtown.

    These are independent events — no causal link.

    📗 Example 11 — Common Cause

    Statement I: Roads were slippery. Statement II: Flights were cancelled.

    Both are effects of a common cause: severe weather.

    Solved Exercises

    📘 Exercise 1

    Premises: All managers are leaders. Some leaders are visionaries. Conclusion: Some managers are visionaries. Valid?

    Invalid. "Some leaders" that are visionaries might not overlap with the manager subset of leaders. We cannot guarantee the intersection.

    📘 Exercise 2

    Premises: No cat is a dog. All puppies are dogs. Conclusion: No puppy is a cat.

    Valid. Puppies ⊂ Dogs, Dogs ∩ Cats = ∅ → Puppies ∩ Cats = ∅.

    📘 Exercise 3

    "If Raj passes, he will get a job." Raj didn't get a job. What can we conclude?

    Contrapositive: ¬Job → ¬Pass. So Raj did not pass. (Modus Tollens)

    📘 Exercise 4

    "You won't get dessert unless you finish your vegetables." You got dessert. What's true?

    "Unless" = "If not." ¬vegetables → ¬dessert. Contrapositive: dessert → vegetables. You got dessert, so you finished your vegetables.

    📘 Exercise 5

    Statement: "The company should invest in AI training for employees." Which is an implicit assumption?
    (A) Employees currently lack AI skills (B) AI is a passing trend (C) The company has no employees

    (A). The statement assumes employees need AI skills they don't currently have. (B) contradicts the statement, (C) makes it absurd.

    📘 Exercise 6

    All A are B. All B are C. All C are D. Conclusion: All A are D. Valid?

    Valid. A ⊂ B ⊂ C ⊂ D → A ⊂ D (transitive chain).

    📘 Exercise 7

    "Either the train is late or I missed it." The train is on time. Conclusion?

    P ∨ Q, ¬P → Q. Train is on time (not late), so I missed it. (Disjunctive syllogism)

    📘 Exercise 8

    Statement I: Crop yield declined sharply. Statement II: Government announced subsidies for farmers. Cause-effect?

    I is the cause, II is the effect. Declining crop yield prompted the government to announce subsidies.

    📘 Exercise 9

    Premises: Some poets are dreamers. All dreamers are creative. Conclusion: Some poets are creative.

    Valid. Some poets are dreamers, and all dreamers are creative, so those particular poets are creative.

    📘 Exercise 10

    "P only if Q." P is true. What follows?

    "P only if Q" means P → Q. Since P is true, Q must be true.

    Analytical Reasoning High Weight

    Seating arrangements (linear & circular), sequencing, grouping, blood relations, direction sense.

    Linear Seating Arrangement

    People sit in a row, facing the same or opposite directions. Key strategy: fix one person's position first, then place others relative to that anchor.

    Fig 3.1 — Linear arrangement. "Left of B" depends on whose perspective — from B's own perspective or from the viewer's.
    💡 Critical Tip — Perspective

    When people face north, THEIR left = viewer's right. When people face south, their left = viewer's left. Always clarify the perspective in the problem!

    📗 Example 1 — Linear Arrangement

    5 people A–E sit in a row facing north. B is to the immediate left of D. A is at the right end. C is not adjacent to A. E is at the left end.

    1
    Fix ends: E _ _ _ A
    2
    B is immediately left of D: BD must appear together. C not adjacent to A → C can't be position 4.
    3
    Arrangement: E, B, D, C, A or E, C, B, D, A. Check: In ECBDA, C is position 2, A is 5 — not adjacent ✓. B(3) is left of D(4) ✓.

    Answer: E, C, B, D, A

    Circular Seating

    Fig 3.2 — Circular arrangement. When facing center: "left" = clockwise, "right" = anti-clockwise.
    📗 Example 2 — Circular Arrangement

    6 friends sit in a circle facing the center. A is opposite D. B is to the immediate left of A. F is opposite C. E is between D and F.

    Fix A at top. D opposite (bottom). B to A's left (clockwise) = position to A's clockwise. F opposite C. E between D and F. Result: A, B, C, D, E, F (clockwise).

    📗 Example 3 — Scheduling

    5 lectures Mon–Fri: Math before Science, English on Wednesday, History not on Monday, Art before History.

    English = Wed. Art before History, both ≠ Mon for History. Math before Science. Try: Mon=Math, Tue=Science, Wed=English, Thu=Art, Fri=History. Check: Math(Mon) before Science(Tue) ✓, Art(Thu) before History(Fri) ✓. Answer: Math, Science, English, Art, History.

    Blood Relations

    Fig 3.3 — Family tree notation and sample diagram.
    📗 Example 4 — Blood Relation

    "A is the mother of B. B is the sister of C. C is the father of D." What is A to D?

    A → B (mother). B and C are siblings (B is sister of C, so C is B's brother). A is mother of C too. C → D (father). So A is D's grandmother.

    📗 Example 5 — Coded Blood Relation

    "Pointing to a photo, A says: He is the son of my father's only daughter." Who is in the photo?

    Father's only daughter = A herself (assuming A is female). Son of A = A's son.

    Direction & Distance

    📗 Example 6 — Direction Sense

    A walks 5 km North, turns right and walks 3 km, turns right and walks 5 km. How far is A from start and in which direction?

    North 5 → East 3 → South 5. Net: 0 N-S, 3 km East. Distance from start = 3 km East.

    📗 Example 7 — Distance with Pythagorean

    B walks 6 km South then 8 km West. How far from start?

    √(6² + 8²) = √100 = 10 km (South-West direction).

    Grouping & Distribution

    📗 Example 8 — Grouping

    8 people in 2 teams of 4. A and B must be together. C and D must be apart. How many valid groupings?

    Fix A, B in Team 1. From remaining {C,D,E,F,G,H}, pick 2 for Team 1. C & D apart: either C in T1 + D in T2, or D in T1 + C in T2, or neither in T1. C in T1: pick 1 from {E,F,G,H} = 4 ways. D in T1: same = 4 ways. Neither: pick 2 from {E,F,G,H} = 6, but then C,D both in T2 — that's fine (they're apart? No, they're together!). So neither = 0 valid. Total = 4 + 4 = 8 ways.

    📗 Example 9 — Ranking/Ordering

    5 students ranked in exam. P scored higher than Q. R scored between P and Q. S scored lowest. T scored highest. What is the order?

    T > P > R > Q > S. Answer: T, P, R, Q, S.

    📗 Example 10 — Floor Puzzle

    5-floor building (1=ground). A is above B. C is on floor 3. D is on the topmost floor. E is between B and C.

    D = 5. C = 3. A above B. E between B and C. If B = 1, E = 2, C = 3, A = 4, D = 5. Check: A(4) above B(1) ✓. Answer: B, E, C, A, D (floors 1-5).

    Solved Exercises

    📘 Exercise 1

    7 people P-V sit in a row facing north. Q is at one end. R is 3rd from left. P is adjacent to both S and T. V is not at any end. U is to the right of R. Find the arrangement.

    R is 3rd. Q at one end. V not at ends. P adjacent to S and T (P between S and T or S,P,T consecutive). Try: Q at position 1. R at 3. U right of R. P between S and T. One valid: Q, S, R, P, T, U, V or similar. Full solution depends on constraints — Q, S, R, P, T, V, U is one valid arrangement.

    📘 Exercise 2

    6 people sit in a circle facing center. A is opposite C. B is to immediate right of A. D is not adjacent to C. Where is D?

    Fix A at 12 o'clock. C at 6. B at 11 (right of A when facing center = anti-clockwise). D not adjacent to C means D ≠ 5 or 7 o'clock positions. D can be at 1 or between B and the remaining spots. D is opposite B (at 5 o'clock position is eliminated, so D at 1 or 2).

    📘 Exercise 3

    "Pointing to a man, a woman says: His mother is the only daughter of my mother." How is the man related to the woman?

    My mother's only daughter = me. His mother = me. So the man is her son.

    📘 Exercise 4

    A walks 10m North, turns left, walks 5m, turns left, walks 10m. Direction from start?

    N 10 → W 5 → S 10. Net: 5m West of start. Direction: due West, 5m away.

    📘 Exercise 5

    Rank order: A is 7th from top, 26th from bottom. How many students total?

    Total = 7 + 26 − 1 = 32

    📘 Exercise 6

    B is 4th to the left of C in a row of 10. C is 6th from right end. What is B's position from left?

    C is 6th from right = 10−6+1 = 5th from left. B is 4 to C's left = 5−4 = 1st from left.

    📘 Exercise 7

    5 books stacked: Physics above Chemistry. Maths at bottom. English between Physics and Biology. Biology on top.

    Top to bottom: Biology, English, Physics, Chemistry, Maths. Check: Physics above Chemistry ✓, Maths at bottom ✓, English between Physics and Biology ✓.

    📘 Exercise 8

    A man faces sunset and turns 135° clockwise. Which direction does he face now?

    Sunset = West. 135° clockwise from West: W→N(90°)→NE(135°). He faces South-East. Wait — clockwise from West: W→N is 90°, +45° more = NE? No. Compass clockwise: W → NW(45°) → N(90°) → NE(135°). He faces North-East. Actually: going clockwise on compass from W: W→NW? No — clockwise means W→SW→S→SE. So W + 135° clockwise = W→SW(45°)→S(90°)→SE(135°). Answer: South-East.

    📘 Exercise 9

    12 people sit in 2 rows of 6 facing each other. A faces D. B is at one end of Row 1. E is 3rd from left in Row 2. Find A and D's positions.

    If A is in Row 1 at position X, D is directly across in Row 2 at same position. With B at end of Row 1 and E at position 3 of Row 2, the constraint narrows possibilities. Multiple valid arrangements exist depending on additional constraints.

    📘 Exercise 10

    A walks 3 km East, then 4 km North, then 3 km West. How far from start?

    E3 + N4 + W3 = net 0 East-West, 4 North. Distance = 4 km due North.

    Number Series

    Find the pattern governing a sequence and predict the next term. Common patterns: constant difference (AP), constant ratio (GP), squares/cubes, alternating operations, Fibonacci-style, differences of differences.

    Fig 4.1 — Use differences of differences to find hidden patterns. Answer: 26 + 11 = 37.

    2, 6, 18, 54, ? → ×3 each step. Answer: 162

    📗 Example 3 — Square Series

    1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ? → n². Answer: 36 (6²)

    📗 Example 4 — Alternating Operations

    2, 4, 12, 14, 42, 44, ? → Pattern: +2, ×3, +2, ×3, +2, ×3. Answer: 44 × 3 = 132

    2, 3, 6, 15, 45, 156.5, 630. Which is wrong?

    Pattern: ×1.5, ×2, ×2.5, ×3, ×3.5, ×4. Check: 2×1.5=3 ✓, 3×2=6 ✓, 6×2.5=15 ✓, 15×3=45 ✓, 45×3.5=157.5 ≠ 156.5. Wrong number: 156.5 (should be 157.5).

    Critical Reasoning

    Strengthening & weakening arguments, assumptions, inferences vs. judgments, statement-conclusion evaluation.

    Strengthening & Weakening Arguments

    An argument has a premise (evidence) and a conclusion (claim). To strengthen = provide evidence that supports the conclusion. To weaken = provide evidence that undermines it.

    Fig 5.1 — Every argument flows: Premise → (Assumption) → Conclusion. Strengthen/Weaken targets the assumption.
    📗 Example 1 — Strengthening

    Argument: "City X installed speed cameras and traffic deaths fell 30%. Therefore speed cameras reduce traffic deaths."

    Strengthener: "No other traffic safety changes were made during this period." (Rules out alternative causes.)

    📗 Example 2 — Weakening

    Same argument. Weakener: "The city also reduced speed limits and improved road lighting during the same period." (Introduces alternative explanations.)

    📗 Example 3 — Identifying the Assumption

    Argument: "Students who eat breakfast perform better in exams. So schools should serve breakfast."

    Assumption: Students don't already eat breakfast at home. (If they do, school breakfast is redundant.)

    Inferences vs. Judgments

    📗 Example 4 — Inference (Fact-based)

    Statement: "India's software exports rose from $100B to $150B in one year."

    Inference: Software exports grew by 50%. ← This is a factual derivation = valid inference.

    Judgment: India is becoming a tech superpower. ← This is an opinion = judgment, not inference.

    📗 Example 5 — Definite vs. Probable Inference

    Statement: "All employees of XYZ Corp received a bonus this quarter."

    Definitely true: Ram, an employee of XYZ, received a bonus. Probably true: XYZ had a good quarter. Cannot be determined: Ram deserved the bonus.

    Statement-Conclusion & Statement-Argument

    📗 Example 6 — Statement-Conclusion

    Statement: "Pollution in the city has reached dangerous levels."

    Conclusion I: Citizens should use masks. → Follows (reasonable action).

    Conclusion II: The city government has failed completely. → Does not follow (too extreme, pollution could have many causes).

    📗 Example 7 — Statement-Argument (For/Against)

    Statement: "Should homework be abolished?"

    For: "Excessive homework causes stress and reduces time for creative activities." → Strong argument (addresses the issue directly).

    Against: "Homework has been a tradition for centuries." → Weak argument (appeal to tradition, doesn't address why it's good).

    📗 Example 8 — Course of Action

    Statement: "Traffic congestion during rush hours has increased by 40%."

    Course I: Implement staggered office timings. → Follows (addresses root cause).

    Course II: Ban all vehicles. → Does not follow (impractical overreaction).

    📗 Example 9 — Flawed Reasoning

    "My neighbor started a business and failed. Therefore, starting a business always leads to failure." This is hasty generalization — drawing a universal conclusion from a single case.

    📗 Example 10 — Circular Reasoning

    "This book is a bestseller because it's a book that sells the best." This is circular reasoning — the "evidence" just restates the claim.

    Solved Exercises

    📘 Exercise 1

    "Company profits rose after hiring a new CEO." Which weakens the claim that the CEO caused the profit rise?
    (A) The industry as a whole saw record profits. (B) The CEO has 20 years experience.

    (A) provides an alternative cause (industry trend), weakening the CEO-specific claim. (B) strengthens it.

    📘 Exercise 2

    "Vegetarians live longer." Assumption?
    (A) Non-vegetarians eat poorly. (B) Diet significantly affects lifespan. (C) All vegetarians exercise.

    (B). The statement assumes diet impacts lifespan — otherwise the claim is meaningless.

    📘 Exercise 3

    Statement: "Online courses are as effective as classroom teaching." Conclusion: "Universities should shift entirely online." Follows?

    Does not follow. "As effective" doesn't mean "should replace entirely" — there may be social, practical, and experiential reasons to keep classrooms.

    📘 Exercise 4

    "Sales of umbrellas increase when ice cream sales increase." Conclusion: Ice cream causes umbrella buying. Valid?

    Invalid. This is correlation, not causation. Both increase in summer due to hot weather (common cause).

    📘 Exercise 5

    Which is a strong argument FOR "Should plastic bags be banned?" (A) Paper bags are more expensive. (B) Plastic bags cause severe environmental damage and harm marine life.

    (B) is a strong argument — it directly addresses why a ban is needed with a clear negative consequence. (A) is against the ban.

    📘 Exercise 6

    "No student who studies hard fails. Ram failed." What follows?

    Hard study → ¬fail. Contrapositive: fail → ¬hard study. Ram failed → Ram did not study hard.

    📘 Exercise 7

    "Crime rates dropped after more police were deployed." Strengthen this conclusion.

    Strengthener: "Areas without additional police saw no change in crime rates." This shows the police deployment was the differentiating factor.

    📘 Exercise 8

    Identify the flaw: "9 out of 10 dentists recommend this toothpaste. Therefore it must be the best toothpaste."

    Appeal to authority / incomplete evidence. We don't know what alternatives were offered, sample size, or if the dentists were paid. Recommendation ≠ "best."

    📘 Exercise 9

    Statement: "Global temperatures have risen 1.2°C since pre-industrial times." Which is an inference (not judgment)?
    (A) Governments have failed. (B) Pre-industrial temperatures were lower than today. (C) We are doomed.

    (B) is a direct factual inference. (A) and (C) are value judgments.

    📘 Exercise 10

    "Should work from home be permanent?" Argument: "It saves commuting time and reduces pollution." Strong or weak?

    Strong argument. It presents concrete, verifiable benefits directly relevant to the topic.

    Input-output machines, matrix reasoning grids, coding-decoding — letter shifts, number substitution, symbolic codes.

    Input-Output Machines

    A "machine" applies a series of operations (sort, swap, shift) to an input step-by-step. You deduce the rule by comparing input and successive outputs.

    Fig 6.1 — This machine performs ascending sort, placing the smallest remaining number at the next left position each step.
    📗 Example 1 — Input-Output

    Using the machine above, what is Step 4?

    Next smallest remaining = 45. Move to position 4: 9 16 32 45 85 72.

    📗 Example 2 — Reverse Machine

    A machine outputs the largest number to the right each step. Input: 14 8 23 5 19. Step 1?

    Largest = 23, moved to right: 14 8 5 19 23.

    📗 Example 3 — Word Machine

    Input: "the cat sat on mat". Step 1: Alphabetically first word goes to position 1. Step 1: "cat the sat on mat". Step 2: Next alphabetical to position 2: "cat mat the sat on".

    Coding-Decoding

    Fig 6.2 — Three fundamental coding-decoding approaches.
    📗 Example 4 — Letter Shift +2

    If COME = EQOG, find the code for GONE.

    Each letter shifts +2: G→I, O→Q, N→P, E→G. Code: IQPG.

    📗 Example 5 — Reverse Coding

    If TABLE = ELBAT, what is CHAIR?

    The word is simply reversed: RIAHC.

    📗 Example 6 — Number-Position Code

    If FACE = 6-1-3-5 (position values), find the code for BEAD.

    B=2, E=5, A=1, D=4. Code: 2-5-1-4.

    📗 Example 7 — Symbolic Language

    "sky is blue" = "ka la pa", "sea is deep" = "la ma na", "sky is high" = "ka la ra". Find the code for "blue".

    "sky is" = "ka la" (common in 1st and 3rd). So "blue" = pa, "sea" + "deep" = "ma" + "na", "high" = "ra".

    Matrix Reasoning Grids

    📗 Example 8 — 3×3 Matrix

    In a 3×3 grid, each row has a circle, triangle, and square. Each row has objects colored red, blue, green. Row 3 has: ?, blue triangle, red circle. What is the missing shape?

    Row 3 needs a square (missing shape) in green (missing color). Answer: Green square.

    📗 Example 9 — Number Matrix

    Matrix: Row 1: [2, 5, 12]. Row 2: [3, 8, 19]. Row 3: [4, 11, ?]. Pattern: Each row follows c = a×b − (a−2). Check: 2×5−0=10? No. Try: 2×5+2=12 ✓. 3×8−5=19? 24−5=19 ✓. Hmm, pattern = a×b + (b−a−1)? Let's try simpler: col3 = col1×col2 + 2. 2×5+2=12 ✓. 3×8−5=19? No. Try col3 = col1² + col2 + 1: 4+5+1=10≠12. Try col3 = (col1+col2)×2 + col1−col2: nah. Simplest: diff pattern. 12=2+10, 19=3+16, ?=4+22=26. Differences: 10,16,22 (+6 each). Answer: 26.

    📗 Example 10 — Conditional Rules

    Rule: If a number is even, multiply by 2. If odd, add 3. Input: 5. Step 1: 5 is odd → 5+3 = 8. Step 2: 8 is even → 8×2 = 16. Step 3: 16 is even → 16×2 = 32.

    Solved Exercises

    📘 Exercise 1

    Input: 72 14 9 35 51 28. Machine sorts ascending, one number per step (smallest to left). What is Step 2?

    Step 1: 9 72 14 35 51 28. Step 2: 9 14 72 35 51 28. Answer: 9 14 72 35 51 28

    📘 Exercise 2

    If LAMP = NCOR (+2 shift), what is DESK?

    D→F, E→G, S→U, K→M. Answer: FGUM

    📘 Exercise 3

    "red is hot" = "pa ka ta", "sun is big" = "ka ma na", "red sun set" = "pa ma da". What is "set"?

    "red" = pa (common: statements 1&3). "is" = ka (1&2). "sun" = ma (2&3). So "set" = da.

    📘 Exercise 4

    Matrix row: [5, 12, 26]. [7, 16, 34]. [9, 20, ?]. Find the pattern.

    col2 = col1×2 + 2: 5×2+2=12✓, 7×2+2=16✓, 9×2+2=20✓. col3 = col2×2 + 2: 12×2+2=26✓, 16×2+2=34✓, 20×2+2 = 42

    📘 Exercise 5

    Rule: If vowel → replace with next letter. If consonant → replace with previous letter. Encode: BIKE.

    B(cons)→A, I(vowel)→J, K(cons)→J, E(vowel)→F. Answer: AJJF

    📘 Exercise 6

    If TIGER = UJHFS (+1 with alternating: +1, +1, +1, +1, +1). What about HORSE?

    H→I, O→P, R→S, S→T, E→F. Answer: IPSTF

    📘 Exercise 7

    Input-output: Input "bring all cats home". Step 1 sorts alphabetically first word to front. Give Step 2.

    Step 1: "all bring cats home". Step 2: next alphabetical word to 2nd position: "all bring cats home" — "bring" is already in correct place. Answer: "all bring cats home" (already sorted through "bring").

    📘 Exercise 8

    In a 3×3 grid, each row and column contains exactly one star, one moon, one sun. Row 1: Sun, ?, Moon. Col 1: Sun, Moon, ?. What fills Row 2 Col 2?

    R1 = Sun, ?, Moon. R1C2 must be Star (missing). Col1: Sun(R1), Moon(R2), Star(R3). R2: Moon, ?, ?. Col2 has Star(R1). R2C2 can't be Star or Moon(already in R2). R2C2 = Sun.

    📘 Exercise 9

    Conditional machine: If digit > 5, subtract 3. If digit ≤ 5, add 4. Process: 7.

    Step 1: 7>5 → 7−3=4. Step 2: 4≤5 → 4+4=8. Step 3: 8>5 → 8−3=5.

    📘 Exercise 10

    If MOUSE = PRXVH (each letter +3), decode FRGH.

    Reverse: subtract 3. F→C, R→O, G→D, H→E. Answer: CODE
