    Module 01 · Foundations

    What is Hypothesis Testing?

    Start from zero: hypotheses, experiments, and why we test ideas before committing.

    🧪 What is a Hypothesis?

    A hypothesis is a testable statement about what you believe is true and what will happen if you act. Experimentation is the structured way of checking it with evidence instead of opinion.

    🔴 Opinion-led

    "I think a bigger Resume button will help." The team debates, the loudest voice wins.

    🟢 Hypothesis-led

    "We believe a prominent Resume button will increase lesson restarts by 10%. We'll test it for 2 weeks."

    📐 Anatomy of a Good Hypothesis

    PartQuestionExample
    BeliefWhat do we believe?Learners drop off because it is hard to continue where they stopped
    ChangeWhat will we do?Add a "Continue" card on the home screen
    OutcomeWhat will change?Weekly returning learners
    Measure and targetHow much, how measured?+10% within 14 days
    ReasonWhy?Reduces effort to restart
    EXAMPLE · Template

    We believe [change] for [segment] will [outcome]. We'll know we're right when [measure] reaches [target].

    🗂️ Types of Tests and Experiments

    TypeWhat it isUse
    A/B testTwo versions, random splitCompare one change
    MultivariateSeveral elements varied togetherMany combinations, high traffic
    Smoke / fake door testButton or page for a feature not built yetDemand validation
    Concierge / Wizard of OzHuman simulates the serviceTest value before building
    Prototype testClickable mock with usersUsability and desirability
    Survey experimentRandomised questions or conceptsMessaging and pricing
    Pre/post & quasi-experimentCompare before vs after or matched groupsWhen randomisation is impossible

    📖 Key Terms

    Null hypothesis (H0)No effect or difference.
    Alternative (H1)There is an effect.
    Control / variantOriginal vs changed version.
    MetricWhat is measured (conversion, retention, task time).
    RandomisationRandom assignment that avoids selection bias.
    Statistical significanceResult unlikely to be due to chance alone.
    Sample sizeNumber of users needed to detect an effect.
    Q. Which is the control in an A/B test?
    • A The new design
    • B The current version
    • C A competitor
    • D The survey

    Module 02 · Experiment Design

    Designing a Sound Experiment

    Choose metrics, set up groups, size samples and avoid common pitfalls.

    🧭 The Experiment Lifecycle

    Figure 2.1 · Experiment lifecycle

    📏 Choosing Metrics

    Metric typeMeaningExample
    PrimaryDecides success; choose ONELesson restart rate
    SecondarySupporting insightTime to restart
    GuardrailMust not get worseSupport tickets, load time, refund rate
    Leading vs laggingEarly signal vs final outcomeWeek-1 activity vs 90-day retention
    ⚠️
    Beware vanity metrics (page views, sign-ups) that look good but do not measure value.

    🔢 Sample Size and Duration

    Baseline rateCurrent conversion, e.g. 20%.
    Minimum detectable effect (MDE)Smallest change worth detecting, e.g. +2 points.
    Significance level (α)Usually 0.05 — accepted false-positive risk.
    PowerUsually 80% — chance to detect a real effect.
    Duration ruleRun full weekly cycles (7, 14, 21 days) to cover weekday patterns.
    EXAMPLE · Rough scale

    Baseline 20%, want to detect +2 points at 95% confidence and 80% power → roughly 6,500 users per variant. With 1,000 weekly eligible users a variant, that is 6–7 weeks; consider a bigger change or a different method.

    ⚠️ Common Pitfalls

    PitfallFix
    Peeking and stopping earlyPre-set duration and sample size
    Many metrics, cherry-pickingDeclare primary metric in advance
    Novelty effectRun long enough; check returning users
    Sample ratio mismatchVerify split is as designed (e.g. 50/50)
    Interference between groupsRandomise at the right unit (user, class, account)
    Testing tiny changes with low trafficUse qualitative or prototype tests
    Q. How many primary metrics should a test have?
    • A As many as possible
    • B Ten
    • C None
    • D One, chosen in advance

    Module 03 · Triangulation & Psychology

    Triangulation, Segments and User Psychology

    Combine behaviour, market segment data and psychology to form stronger hypotheses.

    🔺 What is Triangulation?

    Triangulation uses several independent sources to confirm a finding. Each method has blind spots; agreement across them increases confidence.

    SourceTells youBlind spot
    Behavioural data (analytics, logs)What people doWhy
    Attitudinal data (interviews, surveys)What people say and feelSays vs does gap
    Market / segment data (reports, CRM)Who, how many, market sizeIndividual motivation
    Psychology / behavioural scienceWhy people decide this wayContext specifics
    EXAMPLE · Triangulating a drop-off
    • Analytics: 45% leave at the payment step.
    • Interviews: Learners fear unknown extra charges.
    • Market data: Competitors show the full price on the first screen.
    • Psychology: Loss aversion and ambiguity aversion.
    • Hypothesis: Showing total price up front increases payment completion.

    🎯 Segments and Market Data

    SegmentationBasisExample
    DemographicAge, location, roleWorking adults in tier-2 cities
    BehaviouralUsage, frequencyDaily vs weekly learners
    Needs / JTBDGoal or job"Get a promotion" vs "switch careers"
    Value-basedRevenue or lifetime valueCorporate vs individual buyers

    Always analyse test results by segment; an overall flat result can hide a big win for one group and a loss for another (Simpson's paradox risk).

    🧠 User Psychology Principles for Hypotheses

    📉
    Loss aversion
    People feel losses about twice as strongly as gains.
    ⚓
    Anchoring
    First number seen shapes judgement of later ones.
    👥
    Social proof
    People follow what others like them do.
    🧩
    Choice overload
    Too many options reduce decisions.
    ⏳
    Present bias
    Immediate rewards beat future ones.
    🏁
    Goal gradient
    Effort rises as the goal gets closer.
    ⚠️
    Use psychology ethically. Avoid dark patterns (fake scarcity, hidden costs, confirm-shaming).
    Q. Why triangulate?
    • A To avoid analysis
    • B Each method has blind spots; agreement raises confidence
    • C To get more data for its own sake
    • D To save money

    Module 04 · Analysis & Statistics

    Analysing Results Properly

    Interpret p-values, confidence intervals and effect sizes; decide with discipline.

    📊 Core Statistical Ideas

    p-valueProbability of seeing a result this extreme if there were truly no effect. p < 0.05 is the common threshold.
    Confidence intervalRange likely to contain the true effect, e.g. +1.2 to +4.8 points.
    Effect sizeHow big the difference is — not just whether it exists.
    Type I errorFalse positive: claiming an effect that is not real.
    Type II errorFalse negative: missing a real effect.
    Practical significanceWhether the effect is large enough to matter to the business.
    📌
    A p-value is not the probability your hypothesis is true. A tiny effect can be "significant" with a huge sample yet worthless.

    🧮 Worked Example

    EXAMPLE · A/B result
    ControlVariant
    Users6,8006,800
    Conversions1,360 (20.0%)1,496 (22.0%)
    Lift+2.0 points (+10% relative)
    p-value≈ 0.002
    95% CI for difference+0.7 to +3.3 points

    Conclusion: the lift is statistically significant and meaningful. Check guardrails (refunds, tickets) and segments before shipping.

    🔀 Decision Framework

    ResultTypical decision
    Significant, positive, guardrails OKShip and monitor
    Not significant, CI includes zeroInconclusive — extend, redesign or stop
    Significant, negativeRoll back, learn why
    Mixed by segmentTargeted rollout; investigate
    Positive but tinyWeigh cost of maintenance vs value
    • Correct for multiple comparisons when testing many variants/segments (e.g. Bonferroni).
    • Consider Bayesian methods for probability-of-being-best statements.
    • Use sequential testing if you must monitor continuously.

    🎯 Check Understanding

    Q. A p-value of 0.03 means…
    • A The effect is large
    • B If there were no real effect, a result this extreme would occur 3% of the time
    • C The hypothesis is proven
    • D 97% chance the variant is better

    Module 05 · Expert Practice

    Experimentation Programmes and Strategy

    Scale from single tests to a learning culture and prioritised portfolio.

    🏆 Prioritising Hypotheses

    FrameworkScoring
    ICEImpact × Confidence × Ease (1–10 each)
    PIEPotential × Importance × Ease
    RICE(Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort
    EXAMPLE · RICE

    Idea: "Continue card". Reach 20,000 learners/quarter, Impact 2, Confidence 80%, Effort 2 person-months → (20,000 × 2 × 0.8) ÷ 2 = 16,000. Compare against other ideas.

    🧪 Qualitative Plus Quantitative

    • Use research to generate hypotheses and quantitative tests to measure them.
    • After a test, run interviews to understand why the winner won.
    • When traffic is low, use unmoderated prototype tests and fake-door tests.
    • Pre-register hypothesis, metric and decision rule.

    🏛️ Building an Experimentation Culture

    Experiment logSearchable record of hypotheses, results, learnings.
    Learning goalValue learning, not just "wins" — most tests fail.
    GovernanceReview ethics, privacy and guardrails before launch.
    PlatformsOptimizely, VWO, LaunchDarkly, GrowthBook, Statsig, Google Optimize alternatives.
    Win rateTypically 10–30% of tests; failures save money.

    ⚖️ Ethics in Experimentation

    • Inform users in privacy terms; avoid harmful or deceptive variants.
    • Exclude vulnerable groups from risky tests; protect data.
    • Never test pricing or access in ways that unfairly discriminate.

    Practice: Test Whether Showing Price Up Front Helps

    Form, design and read an experiment end to end.

    0 / 0 correct

    💳 Scenario

    45% of learners abandon at checkout. Interviews suggest fear of hidden fees. Competitors show total price early. You will design the experiment.

    1️⃣ Task 1 · Hypothesis

    ✍️ Write a hypothesis with segment, change, outcome, metric and target.
    Model answer: We believe showing the total price (incl. taxes) on the course page for new visitors will increase checkout completion from 55% to 60% within 3 weeks. Guardrails: refund rate, support tickets.

    2️⃣ Task 2 · Metrics

    Q. Which is the best PRIMARY metric?
    • A Page views
    • B Checkout completion rate
    • C Social likes
    • D Time on homepage
    ✍️ Name 2 guardrail metrics.
    Model answer: Refund rate and support tickets about pricing; optionally page load time or revenue per visitor.

    3️⃣ Task 3 · Design

    Q. Best way to assign visitors?
    • A Show variant on weekends only
    • B Randomly split 50/50 at user level
    • C Let the sales team choose
    • D Show to returning users only

    4️⃣ Task 4 · Interpret

    ✍️ Result: control 55.0% vs variant 56.0%, p = 0.20, CI −0.5 to +2.5. What do you conclude?
    Model answer: Inconclusive: not significant and CI includes zero. Don't claim a win. Options: extend for more power, test a stronger design, or add qualitative checks to understand hesitation.

    5️⃣ Task 5 · Triangulate

    ✍️ Name 3 other data sources to strengthen the finding.
    Model answer: Funnel analytics by segment, exit-survey responses at checkout, competitor pricing review, customer support transcripts about fees, and behavioural-science literature on price transparency.

    Module 07 · Interview Preparation

    Interview Preparation

    Click a question to reveal a model answer.

    🧪 Fundamentals

    What makes a good hypothesis?
    It is specific and testable, names the change, segment, outcome, metric and target, and is grounded in evidence.
    A/B test vs usability test?
    A/B tests measure behaviour at scale on a metric; usability tests explain why people struggle with small samples. They complement each other.
    What is a fake-door test?
    A visible entry point for a feature not yet built, used to measure real demand before investing.

    📊 Statistics

    What does p < 0.05 mean?
    If there were no true effect, a result this extreme would occur less than 5% of the time. It does not prove the hypothesis.
    What is statistical power?
    The probability of detecting a true effect of a given size; commonly set at 80%.
    Why not stop a test when it looks significant?
    Peeking inflates false positives. Pre-set sample size/duration or use sequential methods.

    🏛️ Strategy

    How do you decide when traffic is too low to A/B test?
    Use prototype tests, fake doors, interviews and larger-effect designs, or longer runs with fewer variants.
    How do you triangulate?
    Combine behavioural analytics, attitudinal research, market data and psychological theory to confirm a finding from several angles.
    How do you handle a failed experiment?
    Record the learning, investigate why with qualitative work, and feed it into the next hypothesis.
