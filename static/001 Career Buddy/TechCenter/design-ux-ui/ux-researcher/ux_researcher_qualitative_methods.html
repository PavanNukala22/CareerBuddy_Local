    Module 01 · Foundations

    What is Qualitative UX Research?

    Start from zero: what UX research is, how qualitative differs from quantitative, and the main families of research methods.

    🧭 What is UX Research?

    UX research is the systematic study of users — their behaviours, needs, motivations and context — so teams can design products that actually work for people. A UX Researcher plans studies, collects evidence, turns it into insights and helps the team act on them.

    🔎
    Understand
    Who are the users and what are they trying to do?
    🧩
    Discover
    What problems and needs exist that nobody has solved?
    🧪
    Evaluate
    Does our design work for real people?
    📣
    Influence
    Share evidence so teams make better decisions.

    ⚖️ Qualitative vs Quantitative

    QualitativeQuantitative
    AnswersWhy? How? What is it like?How many? How much? How often?
    DataWords, observations, video, artefactsNumbers, percentages, counts
    SampleSmall (5–20)Large (100s–1000s)
    MethodsInterviews, contextual inquiry, diary studies, observationSurveys, analytics, A/B tests, benchmarks
    OutputThemes, insights, stories, needsStatistics, trends, benchmarks
    💡
    Qualitative research explains the why behind numbers. Quantitative research tells you how widespread a qualitative finding is. Strong researchers combine both (mixed methods).

    🗺️ The Research Landscape

    Figure 1.1 · Left → right: what people say → what they do. Top → bottom: qualitative → quantitative
    📌
    Say vs do: what people say they do often differs from what they actually do. Observation-based methods (right side) close that gap.

    🗂️ Types of Qualitative Methods

    MethodWhat it isBest for
    Semi-structured interviewGuided 1:1 conversation with planned topics and flexible follow-upsMotivations, mental models, past experiences
    Contextual inquiryInterview + observation while users do real work in their environmentComplex workflows, workarounds, tools used
    Diary studyParticipants log experiences over days or weeksBehaviour over time, habits, infrequent events
    Field observation / ethnographyWatching people in natural settings with little interferenceReal context, social and physical environment
    Focus groupModerated group discussionAttitudes and language (weak for behaviour)

    📖 Key Terms (What is What)

    Generative researchOpen research to discover needs and opportunities.
    Evaluative researchResearch to assess a design or product.
    Research questionWhat the study must answer, e.g. "How do clinic staff manage appointments today?"
    Discussion guideThe planned topics and questions for a session.
    ParticipantA person who takes part in the study.
    SaturationThe point where new sessions stop revealing new themes.
    InsightA non-obvious finding, backed by evidence, that changes decisions.
    RapportTrust between researcher and participant that enables honest answers.
    Q. Which question is best answered by qualitative research?
    • A How many users clicked Buy?
    • B Why do users abandon the form at step 3?
    • C What % of users are on Android?
    • D What is the average order value?

    Module 02 · Semi-Structured Interviews

    Semi-Structured User Interviews

    Plan, run and analyse interviews that reveal real behaviour, mental models and needs.

    🎙️ What is a Semi-Structured Interview?

    A semi-structured interview follows a discussion guide of key topics but lets the researcher change order, skip questions and follow interesting threads. It sits between a rigid questionnaire and an unplanned chat.

    TypeStructureUse when
    StructuredFixed questions, fixed orderComparing answers across many people
    Semi-structuredTopic guide + flexible probingMost UX research — depth with consistency
    UnstructuredOpen conversationVery early exploration, expert interviews

    📝 Writing the Discussion Guide

    Figure 2.1 · A 45–60 minute semi-structured session
    EXAMPLE · Guide excerpt — online grocery
    1. "Tell me about the last time you bought groceries online."
    2. "What happened just before you decided to order?"
    3. "Walk me through how you chose the items."
    4. "What was the hardest part?" → probe: "What did you do then?"
    5. "How did that compare with buying at the local kirana store?"

    ❓ Question Types

    TypeExamplePurpose
    Open"Tell me about…"Get stories, not yes/no
    Specific past"The last time you…"Ground answers in real events
    Probe"Why was that important?" "Can you give an example?"Go deeper
    Echo"…confusing?"Encourage more without leading
    LadderingRepeated "why" from feature → benefit → valueReach underlying motivation
    Show me"Can you show me how you do that?"Turn talk into observation

    🔴 Avoid

    • Leading: "Wasn't that frustrating?"
    • Hypothetical: "Would you use…?"
    • Double: "How did you search and pay?"
    • Closed: "Do you like it?"

    🟢 Use

    • "How did that feel?"
    • "Tell me about the last time…"
    • One question at a time
    • "What did you think of it?"

    🎧 Running the Session

    • Build rapport: explain there are no wrong answers and you didn't design the product.
    • Listen 80%, talk 20%. Embrace silence — wait 3–5 seconds.
    • Use a note-taker so the moderator can focus.
    • Note exact quotes and timestamps; separate observation from interpretation.
    • Do a 10-minute debrief immediately after each session.

    🧠 Check Yourself

    Q. Which is a laddering sequence?
    • A "Do you like it? Yes? Great."
    • B "Why do you use filters?" → "To save time." → "Why does saving time matter?" → "I shop during my lunch break."
    • C "Rate it 1–5."
    • D "Would you pay ₹99?"
    ✍️ Rewrite these as good interview questions: (a) "Would you use a budgeting app?" (b) "Don't you hate hidden fees?"
    Model answer: (a) "Tell me about the last time you checked how much you had spent in a month. How did you do it?" (b) "Tell me about a recent payment where the final amount surprised you. What happened?"

    Module 03 · Contextual Inquiry

    Contextual Inquiry

    Learn how people really work by observing and interviewing them in their own environment.

    🏢 What is Contextual Inquiry?

    Contextual inquiry combines observation and interviewing in the user's real environment while they do real tasks. The researcher acts as an apprentice and the participant as the master who teaches how the work is done.

    🎙️ Interview in a meeting room

    Users describe an idealised version of their work. Workarounds, sticky notes and interruptions are forgotten.

    👀 Contextual inquiry

    You see the paper ledger next to the laptop, the WhatsApp group used for approvals and the 4 interruptions in 20 minutes.

    📐 The Four Principles

    🌍
    Context
    Go to where the work happens: shop floor, clinic, home office.
    🤝
    Partnership
    Master–apprentice relationship; the user leads.
    💭
    Interpretation
    Check your interpretations with the participant on the spot.
    🎯
    Focus
    A clear research focus guides what you observe.

    🔄 Session Structure

    Figure 3.1 · Typical 2-hour contextual inquiry

    🗒️ What to Capture

    CaptureExample
    Steps and sequenceReceptionist checks register → calls patient → updates software
    Tools and artefactsPaper register, sticky notes, Excel, WhatsApp
    BreakdownsSoftware freezes; uses paper as backup
    WorkaroundsPrints the day's list each morning because the app is slow
    EnvironmentNoisy counter, shared computer, frequent walk-ins
    People & rolesDoctor approves refunds verbally
    ✅
    Photograph artefacts (with permission) — a sticky note on a monitor is often the best insight of the day.

    🗂️ Models to Build Afterwards

    ModelShows
    Flow modelPeople, roles and how information moves between them
    Sequence modelSteps taken to complete a task, including breakdowns
    Artefact modelPhysical/digital objects used and how
    Physical modelLayout of the workspace
    Cultural modelNorms, policies and pressures influencing work
    EXAMPLE · Pharmacy billing

    Sequence model reveals the pharmacist enters each medicine twice — once in billing software, once in a stock notebook — because the software's stock count is wrong after returns. Insight: stock accuracy, not billing speed, is the real problem.

    Q. In contextual inquiry, the researcher should act as…
    • A A teacher
    • B An apprentice learning from the user
    • C A salesperson
    • D A silent camera

    Module 04 · Diary Studies

    Diary Studies

    Capture behaviour, feelings and context over days or weeks, as it happens.

    📔 What is a Diary Study?

    In a diary study, participants record their activities, thoughts and feelings over time (typically 1–4 weeks) — usually in an app, via messages or short videos. It captures behaviour that is spread over time or too infrequent to see in one session.

    📅
    Longitudinal
    How behaviour changes across days and weeks.
    🕑
    In the moment
    Less memory bias than a later interview.
    🌍
    Real context
    Captured where and when it happens.
    🔁
    Habits
    Learn routines and triggers.

    🗂️ Types of Diary Studies

    TypeHow entries are triggeredExample
    Interval-contingentAt fixed timesEvery evening at 8 pm: "How did you manage expenses today?"
    Signal-contingentWhen the researcher sends a promptRandom push notification 3× a day
    Event-contingentWhen a specific event happensEach time you order food online
    Feedback / elicitationShort capture now, detail laterSnap a photo now; explain it in the evening

    🔄 Study Phases

    Figure 4.1 · Diary study lifecycle

    🧰 Design Tips

    ChallengeSolution
    Participants stop loggingShort entries (2–3 min), reminders, staged incentives
    Shallow entriesSpecific prompts + photo/video options
    Too much dataLimit to 10–15 participants; tag as you go
    Tool frictionUse what they already use (WhatsApp, dScout, Indeemo)
    EXAMPLE · Fitness app diary — 14 days, 12 participants
    • Prompt (event): "Each time you skip a planned workout, tell us why and where you were."
    • Prompt (interval): "Each night, rate your energy 1–5 and add one sentence."
    • Finding: skips cluster on days with late meetings → opportunity for "10-minute fallback workouts".
    Q. You want to learn why people abandon meal plans over a month. Best method?
    • A One 30-minute interview
    • B Diary study
    • C Card sorting
    • D Tree test

    Module 05 · Field Observation

    Field Observations & Ethnography

    Observe people in their natural setting to understand context, culture and unspoken behaviour.

    👀 What is Field Observation?

    Field observation means watching people in real settings — streets, shops, homes, offices — with little or no interference. Ethnography is a deeper, longer form where researchers immerse themselves in a community or culture.

    🗂️ Types of Observation

    TypeResearcher roleExample
    Passive / naturalisticObserves without interactingWatching how commuters buy metro tickets
    Participant observationTakes part in the activityWorking a shift as a delivery rider
    OvertPeople know they are observedShadowing a bank teller with consent
    CovertPeople don't know (ethically restricted)Only public, anonymous behaviour; never private
    ShadowingFollows one person through their dayA field sales agent visiting shops
    Fly-on-the-wallUnobtrusive, short sessionsObserving a hospital waiting area

    🗒️ The AEIOU Observation Framework

    A — ActivitiesWhat are people doing? Goals and steps.
    E — EnvironmentsThe physical and social setting.
    I — InteractionsBetween people, and between people and things.
    O — ObjectsTools, devices, documents used.
    U — UsersWho is there, their roles and relationships.

    📸 Field Notes

    🔴 Interpretive (weak)

    "The shopkeeper hates the app."

    🟢 Descriptive (strong)

    "Shopkeeper sighed, closed the app after 40 s, wrote the sale in a notebook. Said: 'It's faster this way.'"

    💡
    Write descriptive notes first, add interpretations separately (e.g. in a different colour) during debrief.

    🧠 Example & Check

    EXAMPLE · UPI payments at a vegetable market

    Observation over 3 mornings: vendors show a printed QR, then listen for the speaker announcement ("₹120 received") instead of checking the phone, because their hands are busy. Insight: audio confirmation is the trust signal, which shaped a louder, language-specific confirmation feature.

    Q. Which field note is descriptive?
    • A The user was confused
    • B The user scrolled up and down 4 times, then asked a colleague where the export button was
    • C The UI is bad
    • D The user is lazy

    Module 06 · Interview Preparation

    Interview Preparation

    Click a question to reveal a model answer.

    🧭 Qualitative Research Basics

    Qualitative vs quantitative research?
    Qualitative explains why and how, using small samples, words and observation. Quantitative measures how many, how much and how often, using larger samples and numbers. Mixed methods combine both.
    Generative vs evaluative research?
    Generative discovers needs and opportunities before a solution exists; evaluative assesses a specific design or product.
    How do you choose a qualitative method?
    From the research question: motivations → interviews; real workflows → contextual inquiry; behaviour over time → diary study; natural context and social behaviour → field observation.
    What is saturation?
    The point where additional sessions stop revealing new themes — usually after 5–8 participants per segment.

    🎙️ Interviews & Contextual Inquiry

    What is a semi-structured interview?
    A conversation guided by a topic guide, with flexibility to change order and follow interesting threads with probes.
    How do you avoid leading questions?
    Use neutral, open wording grounded in past behaviour ("Tell me about the last time…"), ask one thing at a time, and pilot the guide with a colleague.
    What is laddering?
    Repeatedly asking "why is that important?" to move from features to benefits to underlying values.
    Explain contextual inquiry.
    Observing and interviewing users in their real environment while they work, in a master–apprentice relationship, then building models like sequence, flow and artefact models.
    Why observe instead of only asking?
    What people say they do often differs from what they actually do; observation reveals workarounds, tools and interruptions they forget to mention.

    📔 Diary Studies, Field Work & Rigour

    When would you run a diary study?
    To capture behaviour and feelings over days or weeks — habits, infrequent events and changes over time — with less memory bias than a later interview.
    How do you keep diary participants engaged?
    Short entries, clear prompts, reminders, using tools they already use (e.g. WhatsApp), and staged incentives.
    What is the AEIOU framework?
    An observation framework: Activities, Environments, Interactions, Objects and Users.
    How do you reduce bias in qualitative research?
    Screen on behaviour, peer-review guides, have two people code data, look for disconfirming evidence and triangulate with other sources.

    Practice: Plan a Qualitative Study

    Design a mixed qualitative study for a real-feeling product and justify each choice.

    0 / 0 correct

    🚚 Scenario: KiranaLink B2B Ordering App

    KiranaLink lets small grocery shop owners reorder stock from distributors. Only 30% of onboarded shops order through the app more than twice; the rest go back to phone calls with the distributor's salesperson. The team asks: "Why don't shopkeepers keep using the app?"

    1️⃣ Task 1 · Research Questions

    ✍️ Write 3 research questions for this study.
    Model answer: RQ1: How do shop owners decide what and when to reorder today? RQ2: What role does the salesperson relationship play (credit, trust, advice)? RQ3: What happens during the first 3 app orders that leads people to continue or stop?

    2️⃣ Task 2 · Choose Methods

    Q. Which combination fits best?
    • A Survey only
    • B Contextual inquiry at shops + diary study across 2 order cycles + interviews with salespeople
    • C Card sorting
    • D A/B test of button colours
    ✍️ Justify each method and give the sample size.
    Model answer: Contextual inquiry (8 shops, 2 cities): see reordering in context — notebooks, peak hours, cash flow. Diary study (12 shops, 3 weeks, WhatsApp voice notes in local language): capture each reorder decision as it happens. Semi-structured interviews (5 salespeople): understand credit and relationship factors.

    3️⃣ Task 3 · Discussion Guide

    ✍️ Write 4 interview questions for shop owners.
    Model answer: 1. "Tell me about the last time you ran out of a product. What did you do?" 2. "Walk me through your most recent order — how did you decide quantities?" 3. "What does the salesperson do for you besides taking orders?" 4. "Tell me about the last time you used KiranaLink. What happened next?"

    4️⃣ Task 4 · Observation

    Q. During the visit, the owner places an order by phone while the app is open. Best note?
    • A Owner prefers phone
    • B Owner opened app, scrolled 30s, closed it, called salesperson; asked "Can I pay next week?" on the call
    • C Owner dislikes technology
    • D App is broken

    5️⃣ Task 5 · Bias & Ethics

    ✍️ Name 2 biases you could introduce and how you'll avoid them, plus 1 ethical consideration.
    Model answer: Sampling bias: distributors may suggest only friendly shops → recruit independently, include low-usage shops. Observer effect: owners may use the app because you're there → observe a full morning, revisit. Ethics: don't record customers in the shop without consent; anonymise credit and financial details.
