    Module 01 · Foundations

    What is Product Discovery?

    Start from zero: what discovery means, why it exists, how it differs from delivery, and the types of discovery a Product Designer runs.

    🧭 What is Product Discovery?

    Product discovery is the work a team does to decide what to build before spending time and money building it. It answers four questions: Is this a real problem? Will people use our solution? Can we build it? Is it worth it for the business?

    Full-cycle discovery means the designer owns the whole loop: framing the problem, talking to users, listing what the team is unsure about, and testing ideas with prototypes — then repeating as you learn.

    ❓
    Is it a real problem?
    Do enough users feel this pain, often enough, strongly enough?
    🙋
    Will they use it?
    Does our idea actually solve the pain in a way people want?
    🛠️
    Can we build it?
    Is it technically possible with our team, time and tools?
    💰
    Is it worth it?
    Will it support revenue, retention or another business goal?
    💡
    In one line: discovery reduces the risk of building the wrong thing. Delivery reduces the risk of building the thing wrong.

    ⚖️ Discovery vs Delivery

    🔵 Discovery

    Learning fast and cheaply. Outputs are evidence: insights, validated problems, tested prototypes, decisions. Most ideas are expected to fail here — that is success.

    🟢 Delivery

    Building and shipping reliably. Outputs are working software: designs, code, releases. Failure here is expensive.

    DiscoveryDelivery
    Main questionShould we build this?How do we build it well?
    Typical artefactsInterview notes, assumption maps, prototypesFinal UI, specs, code, release notes
    Speed of one loopHours to daysWeeks to months
    Cost of being wrongLowHigh
    Who leadsProduct trio (PM, Designer, Tech lead)Whole squad

    🗂️ Types of Discovery

    TypeWhat it meansWhen you use it
    Problem discoveryUnderstand users, their context and pain points.New market, unclear needs, falling metrics with no known cause.
    Solution discoveryTest whether a specific idea solves a known problem.Problem is validated; you have several solution ideas.
    Generative researchOpen-ended research to generate new insights.Early: "What do small shop owners struggle with?"
    Evaluative researchResearch to evaluate an existing design or idea.Later: "Can users complete checkout on this prototype?"
    Project-based discoveryA fixed discovery phase (e.g. 4 weeks) before a big build.Large new products, agency work.
    Continuous discoverySmall weekly research and testing that never stops.Mature product teams improving a live product.

    📖 Key Terms (What is What)

    ProblemA situation where users fail to achieve a goal, or achieve it with high effort, cost or frustration.
    OpportunityAn unmet need or pain point that, if solved, creates value for users and the business.
    InsightA non-obvious truth about users, backed by evidence, that changes how you design.
    AssumptionSomething the team believes is true but has not proven.
    HypothesisA testable prediction: if we do X for Y, we expect result Z, because of evidence E.
    PrototypeA cheap representation of an idea built to learn, not to ship.
    ExperimentA planned test with a success criterion decided in advance.
    Product trioPM + Designer + Tech lead who make discovery decisions together.

    📣

    Module 02 · Problem Framing

    Problem Framing

    Turn vague requests into clear, user-centred problems your team can solve and measure.

    🎯 What is Problem Framing?

    Problem framing is defining the right problem, for the right user, in a way the team agrees on. Stakeholders often bring solutions ("add a chatbot"). Framing pulls the conversation back to the problem underneath.

    🔴 Solution disguised as a problem

    "We need a dashboard redesign."

    🟢 Framed problem

    "New store managers can't see which orders are late, so 18% of late orders are noticed only after customers complain."

    🧱 Parts of a Good Problem Statement

    PartQuestion it answersExample
    UserWho exactly?New store managers in their first 30 days
    Need / goalWhat are they trying to do?Spot late orders before customers complain
    ObstacleWhat gets in the way?Late orders are mixed with all orders, no alerts
    EvidenceHow do we know?18% of late orders found via complaints; 6/8 interviews
    ImpactWhy does it matter?Refunds cost ₹3.2L/month; CSAT down 9 points
    📌
    Template: [User] needs a way to [goal] because [obstacle]. We know this because [evidence]. Solving it matters because [impact].

    🧰 Types of Framing Tools

    ToolWhat it isBest for
    5 WhysAsk "why?" repeatedly to reach the root cause.Symptoms that hide a deeper cause.
    Jobs-to-be-Done (JTBD)"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]."Understanding the real progress users want.
    Point-of-View (POV)User + need + insight in one sentence.Aligning the team on one user.
    How Might We (HMW)Reframe the problem as an open question.Kicking off ideation.
    Problem treeCauses (roots) → problem (trunk) → effects (branches).Complex problems with many causes.
    Opportunity Solution TreeOutcome → opportunities → solutions → experiments.Choosing what to work on next (expert level).

    🔁 Example: 5 Whys + JTBD

    EXAMPLE · Food delivery app — "Users don't use scheduled orders"
    1. Why? → They don't see the option.
    2. Why? → It is hidden under the time selector.
    3. Why do they need it? → They want lunch delivered at 1 pm sharp at the office.
    4. Why don't they order earlier? → They forget in the morning rush.
    5. Why does that matter? → They end up eating unhealthy canteen food.

    JTBD: "When I'm busy at work, I want my lunch to arrive at a fixed time without thinking about it, so I can eat well without breaking my focus."

    HMW: How might we help busy office workers get lunch at the same time every day with zero daily effort?

    ✍️ "How Might We" — Getting the Scope Right

    Too narrowJust rightToo broad
    HMW add a schedule button to the cart?HMW help office workers get lunch on time without daily effort?HMW improve food?
    💡
    A good HMW leaves room for many solutions but stays tied to one user and one need.

    🧠 Check Yourself

    Q. Which is the best-framed problem?
    • A Build a referral feature
    • B Users hate our app
    • C Freelancers who invoice monthly lose track of unpaid invoices, so 30% get paid late (support data, 7/10 interviews)
    • D Improve the UI
    ✍️ A stakeholder says: "We need a chatbot on the help page." Reframe it as a user problem and a HMW question.
    Model answer: Ask what the chatbot should fix. If data shows "40% of help-page visitors still raise a ticket for order tracking", the problem is: customers can't find their order status on their own. HMW: How might we help customers know where their order is without contacting support? A chatbot is just one possible solution.

    Module 03 · User Interviews

    User Interviews

    Plan, run and synthesise interviews that reveal real behaviour — not polite opinions.

    🎙️ What is a User Interview?

    A user interview is a structured 1-on-1 conversation (usually 30–60 minutes) to understand a person's behaviour, context, goals and pain points. The golden rule: ask about past behaviour, not future opinions.

    🔴 Opinion question

    "Would you use an app that tracks your expenses?" — People say yes to be polite.

    🟢 Behaviour question

    "Tell me about the last time you checked how much you had spent this month." — Reveals what really happens.

    🗂️ Types of Interviews

    TypeGoalExample question
    Generative / exploratoryDiscover needs and pain points"Walk me through how you plan your week."
    Contextual inquiryObserve users in their real environmentWatch a pharmacist process prescriptions at the counter.
    JTBD / switch interviewLearn why people switched products"What was going on when you started looking for a new tool?"
    Evaluative / usabilityTest a design or prototype"Try to add a new payee. Think aloud."
    Stakeholder interviewUnderstand business goals and constraints"What would success look like in 6 months?"
    Churn / exit interviewUnderstand why users left"What made you stop using the app?"

    👥 Recruiting & Screeners

    Talk to the right people. A screener is a short survey that filters participants.

    • Recruit 5–8 people per segment for qualitative interviews — patterns usually appear by interview 5.
    • Screen on behaviour ("Paid a utility bill online in the last month") not identity alone.
    • Include a mix: power users, new users, churned users, non-users.
    • Offer a fair incentive (gift card, product credit) and get consent for recording.
    EXAMPLE · Screener question

    "How did you last pay your electricity bill?" ○ Cash at counter ○ UPI app ○ Bank website ○ Auto-debit ○ Someone else pays → Recruit only the first three for a study on bill-payment friction.

    📝 The Discussion Guide

    Figure 3.1 · Structure of a 45-minute discussion guide

    🧲 Bias to Avoid

    🧭
    Leading questions
    Wording that suggests the answer you want.
    🤝
    Social desirability
    Users say what sounds good, not what they do.
    🔍
    Confirmation bias
    You notice only quotes that support your idea.
    🧑
    Sampling bias
    Interviewing only friendly power users.

    Figure 3.2 · Synthesis pipeline

    Insight: Owners don't trust the app's stock count because staff forget to record sales, so they keep a paper backup. → HMW make recording a sale so fast that staff never skip it?

    Module 04 · Assumption Mapping

    Assumption Mapping

    Make hidden beliefs visible, find the riskiest ones, and decide what to test first.

    🗺️ What is Assumption Mapping?

    Every idea rests on beliefs that might be wrong. Assumption mapping is a team exercise to (1) list those beliefs, (2) plot them by importance and evidence, and (3) test the riskiest first.

    💡
    Turn "We think…" sentences into assumptions. If the idea fails, which belief was probably wrong?

    🗂️ Types of Assumptions

    TypeQuestionExample
    DesirabilityDo users want it?"Parents want weekly progress reports for their kids."
    UsabilityCan users use it?"Parents can set up the report in under 2 minutes."
    FeasibilityCan we build it?"We can pull grades from schools' systems via API."
    ViabilityDoes it work for the business?"Schools will pay ₹40 per student per year."
    EthicalShould we build it?"Sharing grades with parents won't harm student privacy."

    📌
    Leap-of-faith assumptions are the ones in the top-right: if they are wrong, the whole idea fails, and you have little proof yet.

    Module 05 · Hypothesis-Driven Prototyping

    Hypothesis-Driven Prototyping

    Build the smallest thing that can prove or disprove a hypothesis — and decide the result before you test.

    🧪 What is Hypothesis-Driven Prototyping?

    Instead of prototyping "to see what people think", you prototype to test a specific hypothesis. Every prototype has a question, a method and a pass/fail rule decided in advance.

    📌
    Hypothesis template: We believe that [solution] for [user] will achieve [outcome]. We will know we are right when we see [measurable signal] from [test].

    🧰 Types of Prototypes

    TypeFidelityWhat it testsTime
    Paper / sketchLowConcept, flow, layout ideasMinutes
    Wireframe (clickable)Low–midNavigation, information architectureHours
    High-fidelity prototypeHighUsability, visual clarity, micro-copyDays
    Fake doorReal productDesirability: a button for a feature that doesn't exist yetHours
    Wizard of OzLooks realValue: humans secretly do the "automated" workDays
    ConciergeManual serviceValue: you deliver the service by hand, openlyDays
    Landing page / smoke testMarketing pageDemand and willingness to sign up or payHours
    Coded prototype / betaVery highPerformance, real data, edge casesWeeks

    🎚️ Choosing the Right Fidelity

    If the riskiest question is…Use…
    "Do people want this at all?"Fake door, landing page, concierge
    "Does the flow make sense?"Paper or clickable wireframe
    "Can people complete the task?"High-fidelity prototype + usability test
    "Will the algorithm feel smart?"Wizard of Oz
    "Will it work with real data at scale?"Coded prototype / limited beta
    💡
    Use the lowest fidelity that can answer the question. High fidelity too early makes people comment on colours instead of the idea.

    📋 The Test Card

    HypothesisShowing a "Reorder last basket" card on the home screen will help repeat grocery shoppers check out faster.
    TestUsability test, 8 repeat shoppers, high-fidelity prototype.
    MetricTime to checkout and task success.
    Pass criteria7 of 8 complete in under 60 seconds without help.
    Learn / decidePass → build MVP and A/B test. Fail → review recordings, iterate, retest.

    🔁 Example: Fake Door → Prototype → Decision

    EXAMPLE · Budgeting app — "Bill split" feature
    1. Fake door: "Split a bill" button added for 10% of users for 1 week. 14% tapped it (target ≥ 8%). ✅ Desirability.
    2. Clickable prototype: 6 users tested. 4/6 failed when splitting unequally. ❌ Usability.
    3. Iterate: added "Split by amount / by %" tabs. Retest: 6/6 succeeded. ✅
    4. Decision: build MVP for 2 sprints, then A/B test against the success metric "bills settled within 7 days".
    ⚠️
    Ethics of fake doors: always show an honest message after the click ("Coming soon — want early access?"). Never take payment for something that does not exist.

    Module 06 · Interview Preparation

    Interview Preparation

    Click a question to reveal a model answer.

    🎯 Discovery & Problem Framing Questions

    What is product discovery and how is it different from delivery?
    Discovery decides what is worth building by testing desirability, usability, feasibility and viability cheaply. Delivery builds and ships it reliably. Discovery reduces the risk of building the wrong thing; delivery reduces the risk of building it wrong.
    Walk me through the Double Diamond.
    Discover (explore the problem broadly), Define (converge on one clear problem), Develop (explore many solutions), Deliver (test and converge on the best). The first diamond is about the problem, the second about the solution.
    A stakeholder asks you to "add a chatbot". What do you do?
    Reframe to the underlying problem: ask what outcome they want and what evidence shows the problem. Write a problem statement and a How Might We question, then explore several solutions — the chatbot is only one.
    What makes a good problem statement?
    A specific user, their goal, the obstacle, the evidence and the business impact — without assuming a solution.
    What is Jobs-to-be-Done?
    A framing that focuses on the progress people want to make: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." It reveals real needs beyond features.

    🎙️ User Interview Questions

    How do you write good interview questions?
    Ask about specific past behaviour ("Tell me about the last time…"), keep questions open and neutral, ask one thing at a time, and probe with "why" and "can you show me". Avoid hypotheticals and leading questions.
    How many users do you interview?
    Usually 5–8 per distinct segment, stopping when new sessions stop adding themes (saturation).
    How do you turn interview notes into insights?
    Atomise notes, cluster them in an affinity map, name themes, then write insights that explain why users behave as they do, backed by evidence, and turn them into HMW opportunities.
    Which biases do you guard against in interviews?
    Leading questions, confirmation bias, social desirability and sampling bias — by peer-reviewing guides, recruiting on behaviour and looking for disconfirming evidence.

    🧪 Assumptions & Prototyping Questions

    What is assumption mapping?
    Listing the beliefs an idea depends on (desirability, usability, feasibility, viability, ethics), plotting them by importance and evidence, and testing the important, least-proven ones first.
    How do you pick prototype fidelity?
    Use the lowest fidelity that answers the riskiest question: fake doors or concierge for demand, wireframes for flow, high fidelity for usability, coded betas for real data and scale.
    What is a fake-door test and what are its ethics?
    A button or entry point for a feature that doesn't exist yet, measuring interest. Always show an honest message after the click and never take money for it.
    How do you know a prototype test passed?
    Pass/fail criteria are written before testing, e.g. "7 of 8 users complete checkout in under 60 seconds unaided".

    🏥 Scenario: MediSlot Clinic Booking App

    MediSlot lets patients book appointments at small clinics in Tier-2 Indian cities. Clinics pay a monthly subscription. Leadership says: "Clinics are cancelling. Build a WhatsApp reminder feature."

    Data you haveValue
    Clinic churn (monthly)11%
    No-show rate for booked appointments27%
    Exit survey top reason"Patients book but don't come" (46%)
    Patients who book 3+ days aheadNo-show 38%
    Patients who book same dayNo-show 9%
    Support tickets"Patient says they never got a confirmation"

    1️⃣ Task 1 · Frame the Problem

    Q. What is the best framing of the real problem?
    • A Clinics need WhatsApp reminders
    • B Clinics lose income because patients who book days ahead often don't show up, which drives clinic churn
    • C The app's colours are not trustworthy
    • D Patients want video consultations
    ✍️ Write a problem statement and one HMW question.
    Model answer: Problem: Clinic owners need patients to turn up for booked slots, because 27% of bookings (38% when booked 3+ days ahead) are no-shows. We know this from booking data and the exit survey (46%). It matters because clinic churn is 11%/month. HMW help patients who book in advance remember and keep — or easily cancel — their appointments?

    2️⃣ Task 2 · Plan User Interviews

    ✍️ Who will you interview, and write 4 good interview questions.
    Model answer: Participants: 6 clinic owners/receptionists and 8 patients (4 who missed an appointment, 4 who attended). Questions: "Tell me about the last appointment you missed — what happened that day?" · "How did you find out the time of your appointment?" · "What did you do when your plans changed?" · (clinic) "Walk me through what you do when a patient doesn't turn up."
    Q. Which question would you remove from your guide?
    • A What happened on the day you missed the appointment?
    • B How did you remember your last appointment?
    • C Wouldn't WhatsApp reminders be great?
    • D What did you do when your plans changed?

    3️⃣ Task 3 · Map the Assumptions

    ✍️ List 4 assumptions (one of each type) behind the WhatsApp reminder idea and mark the riskiest.
    Model answer: Desirability: patients miss appointments mainly because they forget 🔥 (riskiest — they may miss due to cost, travel or feeling better). Usability: patients can confirm/cancel from the message. Feasibility: we can use the WhatsApp Business API within budget. Viability: fewer no-shows will reduce clinic churn enough to justify cost.

    4️⃣ Task 4 · Hypothesis & Prototype

    Q. Cheapest way to test "patients miss because they forget"?
    • A Build the full WhatsApp integration
    • B Concierge test: receptionists manually send reminders to half of next week's patients
    • C Redesign the booking screen
    • D Run a survey asking "Do you forget things?"
    ✍️ Write the hypothesis and pass/fail criterion for your test.
    Model answer: We believe that a reminder 24 hours before the appointment, with one-tap confirm/cancel, for patients who book 3+ days ahead, will reduce no-shows. We will know we are right when the reminded group's no-show rate is at least 10 points lower than the non-reminded group (e.g. 38% → ≤ 28%) over 2 weeks of concierge testing.

    5️⃣ Task 5 · Decide & Share

    ✍️ The concierge test cut no-shows from 38% to 21%, and 30% of reminded patients cancelled in advance (freeing slots). What do you recommend to leadership?
    Model answer: Decision: build the reminder feature with confirm/cancel and auto-release of cancelled slots. Evidence: no-shows −17 points; early cancellations let clinics refill slots. Confidence: medium-high (2 clinics, 2 weeks). Next: MVP in 2 sprints, A/B test across 40 clinics, primary metric no-show rate, guardrail opt-out rate, long-term metric clinic churn.
