    🔀 BPMN Basics & Swimlanes

    BPMN gives a shared notation. Swimlanes show who owns each step, exposing handoffs where delays and errors hide.

    SymbolMeaning
    CircleEvent (start / intermediate / end)
    Rounded rectangleTask or activity
    DiamondGateway — decision (XOR), parallel (AND), inclusive (OR)
    Pool / LaneParticipant / role or department
    Dashed arrowMessage flow between pools

    🧭 AS-IS vs TO-BE & Flowcharts

    AS-IS

    Documents today's reality — including workarounds. Validate by walking the process with the people who run it.

    TO-BE

    The target design. Remove waiting, rework, and redundant approvals; assign clear ownership.

    💡
    Look for the classic waste signals: handoffs, queues, rework loops, duplicate data entry, and approvals nobody ever rejects.

    📊 Measuring Processes

    MetricMeaning
    Cycle timeActive time to complete one unit of work
    Lead timeTotal elapsed time from request to delivery, including waiting
    ThroughputUnits completed per period
    First-pass yield% completed right first time, without rework
    Process efficiencyValue-adding time ÷ lead time
    📌
    Typical finding: a 10-day lead time contains only 2 hours of real work — the rest is queues and handoffs. That is where improvement lives.

    ⚙️ Advanced BPMN 2.0: Events, Sub-processes & Exceptions

    ElementUseExample
    Message eventCommunication between poolsCustomer sends order message
    Timer eventElapsed time or date triggersEscalate if no reply in 48 h
    Boundary event (interrupting)Cancels the activity when it firesPayment timeout cancels checkout
    Boundary event (non-interrupting)Spawns a parallel path, task continuesSend reminder while approval waits
    Error event / compensationHandle failure; undo completed workRefund after shipment fails
    Event sub-processHandles events anywhere in a scopeOrder cancelled at any time
    Call activity vs sub-processReusable global process vs embeddedReusable 'Credit Check'
    ⚠️
    Model the unhappy paths. Models that only show the happy path hide 80% of the operational pain. Every task should answer: what happens on error, timeout, and rejection?

    📉 Lean Waste, Little's Law & Process Simulation

    Lean classifies waste as TIMWOODS: Transport, Inventory, Motion, Waiting, Overproduction, Over-processing, Defects, Skills (under-used talent).

    Little's LawWIP = Throughput × Lead time

Example: 120 open cases, 20 closed per day
Lead time = 120 / 20 = 6 days
To reach 3 days at same throughput → WIP limit of 60

    Capacity bottlenecks (Theory of Constraints) set the whole system speed: improving a non-bottleneck step changes nothing. Use simulation or spreadsheet queue models to test a TO-BE design before building.

    💡
    Rework loop impact: if first-pass yield is 80% and rework takes the same effort, effective workload is about 1.25× the nominal demand.

    🤖 Process Mining & RPA Candidate Screening

    Process mining rebuilds the real process from event logs (case ID, activity, timestamp) and reveals variants, bottlenecks, and rework — compared with the designed model by conformance checking.

    RPA suitability testGood candidatePoor candidate
    RulesDeterministic, well-documentedJudgment-heavy
    InputStructured, digitalHandwritten, unstructured
    VolumeHigh, repetitiveRare, low volume
    StabilityStable systems and screensFrequent UI change
    ExceptionsFew, definableMany, unpredictable
    ⚠️
    Never automate a broken process. Fix and simplify first, then automate; otherwise you build a faster mess.

    🧼 Modeling Standards & Swimlane Heuristics

    • Name tasks verb + object (Validate invoice); events as states (Invoice received).
    • One start event per process; every path reaches an end event.
    • Keep a diagram to about 7–15 tasks; push detail into sub-processes.
    • Flow left to right; minimise crossing lines; a lane is a role, not a person.
    • Gateways: split and merge with the same type; label every outgoing flow.
    • Validate the model by walking a real case through it with the process owner.

    🔀 BPMN 2.0: Events, Gateways and Swimlanes

    PointDetail
    What it isThe standard notation for process diagrams: events, tasks, gateways, sequence and message flows, pools and lanes, sub-processes.
    Why it is usedA shared notation removes ambiguity between business and IT, supports automation, and makes AS-IS and TO-BE comparable.
    How it worksStart, intermediate and end events trigger and finish flow; exclusive (XOR) gateways choose one path, parallel (AND) gateways fork all, inclusive (OR) gateways choose one or more; message flows cross pool boundaries; boundary events handle timeouts and errors.
    How to use itFix scope and level first; one lane per role or system; label every gateway condition; model exceptions with boundary events; keep one end state per path; validate with walkthroughs against real cases.
    Common mistakesMixing levels of detail; unlabelled gateways; using sequence flow across pools; no error or timeout paths; diagrams that cannot be read without the author.
    Real-world usageAn order-to-cash model shows a 48-hour payment timeout as a boundary event that routes to cancellation and refund.
    Interview pointsDraw a gateway on the whiteboard; explain XOR versus AND; explain message flow versus sequence flow; justify a sub-process.

    ⏱️ Process Measures: Cycle Time, Throughput and Little's Law

    PointDetail
    What it isQuantitative measures of a process: cycle time, lead time, touch time, throughput, WIP, first-pass yield and cost per transaction.
    Why it is usedYou cannot improve or prove a TO-BE without a baseline. Measures turn opinions into evidence and rank improvement candidates.
    How it worksLittle's Law: WIP = Throughput × Cycle time. If 60 cases are in progress and 12 finish per day, average cycle time is 5 days. Process mining reconstructs real paths from event logs and reveals variants and rework.
    How to use itCollect timestamps per step, calculate averages and percentiles, find the biggest queue, test a change with simulation, then re-measure after go-live.
    Common mistakesReporting only averages; mixing touch time and elapsed time; measuring after the fix only; ignoring variants and exceptions; optimising a non-bottleneck step.
    Real-world usageA claims team cuts cycle time from 9 to 5 days by limiting WIP and removing a duplicate approval found by process mining.
    Interview pointsApply Little's Law to a worked example; explain why a percentile beats an average; name two RPA candidate criteria.
