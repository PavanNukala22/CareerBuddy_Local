    What is a Target Operating Model?

    The bridge between what a company wants to achieve (strategy) and how it actually runs day to day (operations) — explained from first principles.

    🧭 Strategy vs Operations: the gap TOM closes

    A company has a strategy (where to compete and how to win) and operations (the people, processes and systems that do the daily work). Most failures happen in the gap between them: the strategy says "be customer-centric" but the organisation is still built around internal departments.

    A Target Operating Model (TOM) is a blueprint of how the organisation will need to run in the future to deliver its strategy. "Target" = the future state you aim for. "Operating model" = how value is created and delivered: who does what, using which processes, technology, data and locations, under which governance.

    The TOM translates strategic intent into an executable design, which the roadmap then delivers.
    💡
    Simple test: if you can read the strategy and still not know what should change in departments, processes or systems, you are missing a TOM.

    📖 What is what: core vocabulary

    TermMeaningExample
    Business modelHow a company makes money: customers, value proposition, revenue streamsA bank earns interest and fees
    Operating modelHow the company organises itself to deliver that modelBranches plus central operations centre
    Current (as-is) stateHow the organisation works todayLoan approval takes 9 days across 5 teams
    Target (to-be) stateThe designed future way of workingLoan approval in 1 day by one digital team
    Transition stateIntermediate steps between as-is and to-bePilot in one region, then scale
    CapabilityWhat the business can do, independent of who or howCredit Risk Assessment
    ProcessSequence of activities delivering an outcomeApply, verify, decide, disburse
    GovernanceWho decides what and how performance is controlledCredit committee approves loans above 1M
    📌
    Business model = what and for whom. Operating model = how we organise to deliver it. Mixing the two is the most common beginner error.

    🧱 Why organisations build a TOM

    • Strategy alignment: every initiative can be traced to a strategic objective.
    • Clarity: one shared picture of the future for executives, IT and operations.
    • Investment focus: stops random projects; funds what the target design needs.
    • Change enabler: gives change managers a concrete destination.
    • Triggers: new strategy, merger or acquisition, cost pressure, digital transformation, regulatory change, new leadership.
    🎯
    Strategy-led
    Starts from strategic goals and customer promises.
    🧩
    Holistic
    Covers people, process, technology, data, location and governance together.
    🛣️
    Executable
    Links to a roadmap, business case and measures.

    Layers and Components of a TOM

    The building blocks every TOM describes, and the classic frameworks used to structure them.

    🧩 The dimensions of an operating model

    Different consultancies use different labels, but a complete TOM answers the same questions. A common structure is PPOLTI (Process, People, Organisation, Location, Technology, Information) plus Governance and Service delivery / Sourcing.

    DimensionQuestion answeredTypical artefacts
    Customer and value propositionWho do we serve and what do we promise?Personas, journey maps, value proposition
    Processes and value streamsHow is value produced end to end?Process maps, value stream maps, service catalogue
    CapabilitiesWhat must we be good at?Capability map, heatmap
    Organisation structureHow are teams, roles and reporting lines arranged?Org charts, role profiles, spans and layers
    People and cultureWhich skills, behaviours and leadership are needed?Skills matrix, capability building plan
    Technology and applicationsWhich systems enable the processes?Application landscape, target architecture
    Data and informationWhich data is needed, owned and shared?Data domains, data ownership model
    Locations and sourcingWhere is work done and by whom (in-house, shared service, outsourced)?Location strategy, sourcing model
    Governance and performanceWho decides, how is performance measured?Decision rights, KPIs, forums

    ⭐ Galbraith Star Model and McKinsey 7-S

    Two classic design lenses prevent designing structure alone:

    Galbraith Star Model

    • Strategy
    • Structure
    • Processes
    • Rewards
    • People

    Use when designing the organisation: all five points must stay consistent.

    McKinsey 7-S

    • Hard: Strategy, Structure, Systems
    • Soft: Shared values, Skills, Style, Staff

    Use as a diagnostic checklist for alignment gaps.

    ⚠️
    A new structure without matching rewards, skills and processes usually fails: people keep behaving according to the old incentives.

    🔎 Level of detail: three altitudes

    AltitudeAudienceContent
    Conceptual (1-2 pages)Board, CEODesign principles, operating model on a page, key shifts
    Logical (10-30 pages)Executives, programme leadsCapabilities, processes, org, tech, sourcing, governance per domain
    Detailed (implementation)Project teams, HR, ITRole descriptions, process designs, systems, SLAs, KPIs

    An architect works mainly at the conceptual and logical levels and hands detailed design to domain teams.

    Types of Operating Models

    Ross and Weill's four archetypes, structural archetypes and how to choose between them.

    🏛️ Ross and Weill: four enterprise operating model types

    MIT CISR research classifies operating models on two axes: business process standardisation (do units run processes the same way?) and business process integration (do units share data and processes?).

    TypeStandardisationIntegrationFitsExample
    DiversificationLowLowUnrelated businesses, local autonomyConglomerate with separate brands
    ReplicationHighLowSame business repeated in many placesFast-food franchise, retail bank branches
    CoordinationLowHighShared customers, unique productsWealth firm sharing client data across lines
    UnificationHighHighGlobal single-process businessGlobal manufacturer with one ERP and one process set
    💡
    Architect's rule: choose the type first; it determines how much to standardise and integrate. Over-engineering a Diversification company with unification-style IT is a classic waste.

    🏗️ Structural archetypes

    ArchetypeHow it is organisedStrengthWeakness
    FunctionalBy discipline (Finance, Ops, IT)Deep expertise, efficiencySilos, slow cross-functional work
    Divisional / ProductBy product or marketAccountability for resultsDuplication of functions
    GeographicBy regionLocal responsivenessInconsistent global standards
    MatrixTwo reporting dimensionsBalances local and globalUnclear authority, conflict
    Customer-centricAround customer segments or journeysSuperior experienceNeeds strong shared platforms
    Platform / Agile at scaleStable cross-functional teams on value streamsSpeed, ownershipRequires mature governance and funding change
    Hub and spokeCentral hub for shared services; spokes for local deliveryScale plus local fitHub can become bottleneck

    🧪 Choosing the right model: decision factors

    1. Strategic priority: cost leadership favours standardisation; differentiation favours flexibility.
    2. Customer needs: are customers served the same way everywhere?
    3. Regulation: data residency or licensing can force local entities.
    4. Scale economics: does sharing really lower unit cost?
    5. Organisational maturity: can the culture run a matrix or autonomous teams?
    📌
    There is no best model; there is only the model that fits the strategy, market and maturity. State the trade-offs explicitly in the TOM.

    Designing a TOM: Method and Artefacts

    A repeatable, step-by-step approach from strategy intake to a validated target design.

    🛠️ The TOM design lifecycle

    Iterative: findings in later steps often reshape earlier ones.
    1. Understand strategy: objectives, target customers, value proposition, constraints, ambition (growth, cost, risk).
    2. Assess the as-is: interviews, process and org data, cost-to-serve, pain points, capability maturity.
    3. Set design principles: 5-8 short statements that guide every decision.
    4. Design options: two or three coherent alternative models.
    5. Evaluate and select: score options against criteria; decide with sponsors.
    6. Detail and plan: expand the chosen option; create roadmap, benefits and risks.

    📏 Design principles: how to write good ones

    A design principle is a rule that resolves trade-offs. Weak principles are slogans; strong ones are testable.

    WeakStrong
    "Be customer-focused""Every customer-facing process is owned by one accountable team and designed from the customer journey"
    "Use technology well""Buy before build; build only where it differentiates"
    "Be efficient""Standardise commodity processes globally; allow local variation only with an approved business case"
    Principle templatePrinciple: Single source of truth for customer data
Rationale: Duplicate records cause errors and rework (est. 4% of orders)
Implications: One customer data domain owner; systems must consume the master, not copy it
Test: Can a new system be approved if it stores its own customer record? -> No, unless exempted by the design authority

    📦 Key artefacts the architect produces

    Operating Model on a PageCapability heatmapValue stream mapOrganisation design optionsService catalogueApplication and data landscapeSourcing and location modelDecision rights matrixKPI treeTransition roadmap
    ✅

    Translating Strategy into Execution

    Cascading strategy to capabilities, KPIs, OKRs and funding so that nothing in the TOM is unanchored.

    🪜 The strategy cascade

    LevelQuestionTools
    Vision and missionWhy do we exist?Strategy statement
    Strategic objectivesWhat must be true in 3-5 years?Strategy map, Business Motivation Model
    Value proposition and customer promiseWhat do we promise to whom?Value proposition canvas
    CapabilitiesWhat must we be able to do?Capability map and heatmap
    Operating model designHow do we organise to build those capabilities?TOM, org design, process design
    Initiatives and fundingWhat do we change first?Roadmap, portfolio, business case
    Performance measuresHow do we know it works?Balanced Scorecard, OKRs, KPI tree
    💡
    Traceability rule: every TOM component should trace up to an objective and down to a measure. Components that trace to nothing are candidates for removal.

    📊 Balanced Scorecard, OKRs and KPI trees

    Balanced Scorecard

    Four perspectives: Financial, Customer, Internal Process, Learning and Growth. Strategy map shows cause-and-effect between objectives.

    Best for: long-term strategy governance.

    OKRs

    Objective (qualitative, ambitious) plus 2-5 Key Results (quantitative). Set quarterly or annually.

    Best for: alignment and focus in fast-moving teams.

    OKR to TOMObjective: Become the easiest bank to open an account with
  KR1: Reduce time to open account from 3 days to 10 minutes
  KR2: Increase digital onboarding completion from 52% to 80%
  KR3: Cut onboarding cost per customer by 35%
TOM implication: one onboarding value stream team, single KYC service, automated identity checks

    ⚖️ Operating model trade-offs the architect must make explicit

    TensionOption AOption BDecision driver
    Standardise vs localiseOne global processLocal variantsRegulatory and customer difference vs cost
    Centralise vs decentraliseShared service centreEmbedded teamsScale benefit vs speed and proximity
    Build vs buy vs partnerOwn capabilityVendor or allianceDifferentiation, speed, risk
    Efficiency vs resilienceLean, single siteRedundancy, multi-siteRisk appetite and regulation
    Control vs autonomyCentral approvalTeam-level decisionsRisk exposure and maturity

    Governance, Sourcing and Decision Rights

    The "who decides, who delivers, where" layer that determines whether a TOM works in practice.

    🧑‍⚖️ Decision rights frameworks

    Unclear decision rights are the hidden cause of slow organisations. A TOM must state who recommends, agrees, decides, performs and is informed.

    FrameworkRolesUse
    RACIResponsible, Accountable, Consulted, InformedTask and process ownership
    RAPID (Bain)Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, DecideCritical cross-functional decisions
    DACIDriver, Approver, Contributors, InformedProject and product decisions
    RASCIRACI plus SupportiveShared service operations
    RAPID exampleDecision: Approve new application purchase over 100k
Recommend: Domain architect     Agree: Security, Finance
Input: Business owner           Decide: Architecture Review Board
Perform: Procurement
    ⚠️
    RACI rule: exactly one Accountable per item. Two A's means nobody is accountable.

    🌍 Sourcing and location strategy

    ModelDescriptionProsCons
    In-houseOwn staff and sitesControl, knowledgeFixed cost, scale limits
    Shared service centre (SSC)Internal centralised service for many unitsScale, standard processesNeeds strong service management
    Global Business Services (GBS)Multi-function SSC with end-to-end ownershipCross-functional efficiencyComplex to govern
    Outsourcing (BPO/ITO)Third party performs the serviceVariable cost, expertiseVendor dependency, knowledge loss
    Offshore / nearshoreWork moved to lower-cost locationLabour arbitrage, 24x7Communication and risk overhead
    Joint venture / partnershipShared ownership with partnerShared risk and capabilityGovernance conflicts

    Location decisions weigh talent availability, cost, time zone, language, political and regulatory risk, tax and resilience rather than cost alone.

    📜 Service model and performance governance

    • Service catalogue: list of services with owner, consumers, SLA and cost.
    • SLA / OLA: service level agreement (with customers) and operational level agreement (between internal teams).
    • Forums: strategic steering committee, architecture review board, design authority, operational performance review.
    • Funding model: project-based vs persistent product/value stream funding; chargeback vs showback.
    • Three lines model: business (1st), risk and compliance (2nd), internal audit (3rd).

    🚦 Transition architecture and roadmap

    A TOM is never implemented in one move. The architect defines transition states — stable intermediate plateaus — each delivering measurable value and each safe to stop at.

    Each plateau has its own capabilities, risk profile and benefits.
    • Sequencing logic: dependencies first (data, platforms), quick wins early, high-risk moves in pilots.
    • Wave planning: by region, product or business unit.
    • Run vs change: protect business-as-usual during migration; plan parallel running.
    • Exit criteria: define what "done" means per plateau.

    🔄 Change management and benefit realisation

    ModelCore ideaUse in TOM
    Kotter 8 stepsUrgency, coalition, vision, communication, empowerment, quick wins, consolidate, anchorProgramme-level change story
    ADKARAwareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, ReinforcementIndividual adoption tracking
    Bridges transitionEnding, neutral zone, new beginningHandling emotional impact of reorganisation
    Benefit profileBenefit: Reduce order-to-cash cycle time
Baseline: 14 days       Target: 5 days       Owner: VP Finance Operations
Measure: average days from order to cash received (monthly)
Realisation date: Q3 after Transition 2
    📌
    Benefits without an owner, baseline and date are wishes. Track realisation after go-live; most programmes stop measuring too early.

    🚫 Anti-patterns and expert insights

    PointDetail
    What it isA TOM is the designed future way of running the organisation, spanning capability, process, people, technology, data, location, sourcing and governance, tied to strategy and delivered via a roadmap.
    Why it is usedIt prevents disconnected projects and gives leadership one coherent destination with measurable outcomes.
    How it worksStrategy is cascaded into capabilities and design principles; options are evaluated; the chosen design is detailed per dimension and sequenced into transition states with benefits and owners.
    How to use itStart with a one-page conceptual model, validate with executives, then deepen the logical design by domain; keep it live through a design authority.
    Common mistakesBoxes-and-lines org chart only; no design principles; ignoring culture and incentives; "big bang" go-live; strategy not traceable; 200-page document nobody reads; no benefit owner.
    Real-world usageA retailer moving from channel-based silos to a customer-journey operating model consolidates three e-commerce, store and call-centre teams into journey-aligned squads on a shared order and customer platform, cutting order errors and release time.
    Interview pointsExplain Operating Model vs Business Model; name Ross and Weill's four types with examples; describe how you would handle a TOM when a merger is announced; explain how you prove value after go-live.

    Special contexts

    • M&A: decide integration archetype (absorb, preserve, best of both, transform) before designing.
    • Digital / agile at scale: funding follows value streams, platform teams serve product teams.
    • AI-enabled operations: redesign decision rights and roles around human-in-the-loop controls.

    Red flags in a TOM

    • No link from design element to strategic objective
    • No sizing of cost and benefit
    • Governance forums without decision rights
    • Technology chosen before capabilities defined
