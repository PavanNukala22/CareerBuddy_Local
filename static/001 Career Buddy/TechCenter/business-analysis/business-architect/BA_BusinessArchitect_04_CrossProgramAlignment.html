    Redundancy and Why It Happens

    What cross-program alignment means, what "redundant" really is, and the root causes in large organisations.

    🧭 What is cross-program alignment?

    Large organisations run many programs and projects across many business divisions. Without a shared view, each builds or buys its own applications, data stores and processes. Cross-program alignment is the architecture practice of making these efforts consistent, non-duplicating and aimed at the same enterprise outcomes. Rationalisation is the act of reducing and simplifying the application and technology estate to eliminate redundancy.

    💡
    Plain version: ten teams each building their own "customer database" is a rationalisation problem; getting them to agree on one is the alignment problem.

    📖 What is what: key vocabulary

    TermMeaningExample
    Application portfolioThe inventory of all applications with attributes1,200 applications, owners, cost, lifecycle
    RedundancyTwo or more assets providing the same capability for the same purposeThree CRMs in three divisions
    OverlapPartial duplication of functionTwo billing tools both calculate tax
    Technical debtCost of shortcuts or ageing technologyUnsupported OS, hard-coded integrations
    Shadow ITTechnology bought outside IT governanceTeam-purchased SaaS survey tool
    Application sprawlUncontrolled growth in number of applicationsAfter mergers
    RationalisationReduce, consolidate, retire or standardiseMove 3 CRMs onto 1
    DecommissioningSwitching off and removing an applicationArchive data, terminate licences
    Program vs projectProgram = group of related projects delivering combined benefitsDigital Banking Program

    🧬 Root causes of redundancy

    • Mergers and acquisitions: systems inherited and never integrated.
    • Divisional autonomy: each unit optimises locally.
    • Project-based funding: projects build the minimum for their scope and do not retire old systems.
    • Poor visibility: no reliable inventory or capability mapping.
    • Vendor-driven buying: best-of-breed per department.
    • Fear of change: "if it works, don't touch it", especially for legacy.
    • Weak governance: architecture review is optional or late.
    🏷️
    Business redundancy
    Same capability performed in different ways by divisions
    💾
    Application redundancy
    Multiple systems for the same business need
    ☁️
    Technology redundancy
    Different platforms, databases, clouds for the same job
    🗃️
    Data redundancy
    Duplicate and inconsistent master data

    Application Portfolio Management

    Building and using an application inventory: the data you need, lifecycle stages and categories of rationalisation.

    📋 Building the inventory

    You cannot rationalise what you cannot see. Application Portfolio Management (APM) starts with a complete, trustworthy inventory.

    Attribute groupExamples
    IdentityName, ID, description, vendor, version
    OwnershipBusiness owner, IT owner, division
    Business fitCapabilities supported, processes, number of users, criticality
    Technical fitTechnology stack, hosting, integration count, security posture
    LifecyclePlan, Active, Phase-out, End-of-life, vendor support end date
    CostLicence, infrastructure, support, change, total cost of ownership (TCO)
    Risk and complianceData classification, regulatory scope, DR tier
    📌
    Start with 15-25 attributes. A complete small dataset beats a perfect large one that never gets finished.

    🔄 Application lifecycle states

    Lifecycle drives timing of investment and decommission decisions.
    StateTypical action
    Plan / EmergingAssess strategic fit; avoid duplicating an existing service
    Active / StrategicInvest, enhance, make the standard
    Active / SustainedMaintain, no major new features
    Phase-outFreeze scope; plan migration
    End-of-lifeDecommission; archive data

    🧮 The TIME model (Gartner)

    TIME places each application on two axes — business value/fit and technical quality/fit:

    Low technical fitHigh technical fit
    High business fitMigrate — valuable but poor tech; modernise or moveInvest — strategic; strengthen
    Low business fitEliminate — low value and poor tech; retireTolerate — works fine; low value; keep cheap
    💡
    TIME = Tolerate, Invest, Migrate, Eliminate.

    Rationalisation Method and the 6 Rs

    A repeatable process to find, assess, decide and execute consolidation, including the cloud migration "Rs".

    🛠️ The rationalisation process

    Treat as a continuous practice, not a one-off project.
    1. Discover: collect inventory from CMDB, finance, procurement, interviews; deduplicate.
    2. Assess: score business fit, technical fit, cost, risk, redundancy.
    3. Decide: choose TIME disposition and target application per capability.
    4. Plan: sequence, business case, data migration, decommission steps.
    5. Execute: migrate users and data, cut over, switch off, terminate contracts.
    6. Sustain: governance gates prevent new redundancy; refresh data regularly.

    ♻️ The 6 Rs of disposition

    RMeaningUse when
    RetainKeep as isStrategic and healthy
    RetireSwitch offNo longer needed or duplicated
    RehostLift and shift to new infrastructureQuick infra savings, minimal change
    ReplatformMove with minor optimisationManaged DB or cloud service
    Refactor / Re-architectRedesign for cloud-nativeHigh value, long life
    RepurchaseReplace with SaaS or packageCommodity capability
    📌
    Many firms use 5, 6 or 7 Rs. The names matter less than agreeing a consistent disposition vocabulary across the enterprise.

    🏆 Scoring and choosing the target application

    Weighted scoringCapability: Customer Relationship Management (3 candidate apps)
Criteria (weight)         CRM-A   CRM-B   CRM-C
Functional fit   (30%)      4       3       5
Technical health (20%)      3       4       4
Cost / TCO       (20%)      3       4       3

    • Test the winner with real use cases before committing.
    • Document the rationale for audit and re-use.

    Capability-to-Application Mapping and Detection

    Using architecture views to detect where the redundancy actually is, and distinguishing harmful from healthy duplication.

    🧩 Application x Capability matrix

    The most direct redundancy detector: map every application to the business capabilities it supports. Rows with several applications are candidates.

    CapabilityDiv ADiv BDiv CDiv D# Apps
    Customer OnboardingApp-1App-2App-3App-23 (consolidate)
    Invoice ProcessingERP-1ERP-1ERP-22 (review)
    Fraud DetectionFD-XFD-XFD-XFD-X1 (shared service)
    PayrollPR-1PR-4PR-53 (consolidate)
    ✅
    Add a second dimension: colour each cell by TIME disposition or by annual cost to see the money at stake.

    🔎 Detecting redundancy beyond the obvious

    SignalWhat it suggests
    Same vendor, multiple instancesConsolidate instances
    Different vendors, same functionChoose a standard
    Same data entity mastered in many placesCreate master data domain
    Many point-to-point interfaces between similar appsIntegration hub / APIs
    Multiple projects requesting the same capabilityShared platform or service
    Same business process described differentlyProcess standardisation opportunity

    ⚖️ Harmful vs justified duplication

    Harmful redundancy

    • No strategic reason for difference
    • Same users or customers served
    • Duplicated cost and data inconsistencies
    • Examples: three HR systems after merger

    Justified diversity

    • Regulatory or legal separation
    • Different business model needs
    • Resilience or active-active design
    • Planned phased migration
    ⚠️
    Do not rationalise blindly. A unified system that forces one process on very different businesses can destroy value — use the operating model type (diversification, replication, coordination, unification) to decide how much to standardise.

    Cross-Program Governance and Alignment Mechanisms

    The structures, roadmaps and funding mechanisms that stop programs duplicating one another.

    🏛️ Governance structures

    MechanismPurposeWho
    Architecture Review Board (ARB)Approve solution designs against standards and target stateEnterprise and domain architects, security, ops
    Design AuthorityTechnical decisions inside a programmeProgramme architects
    Portfolio Management Office (PMO / EPMO)Prioritise and fund initiativesFinance, strategy, business leaders
    Technology Business Management (TBM)Cost transparency by serviceFinance + IT
    Technology Standards CouncilMaintain technology radar and approved listArchitects, engineering leads
    Data Governance CouncilMaster data ownership and qualityData owners, stewards
    💡
    Gate early: an ARB checkpoint at the idea / funding stage prevents duplication cheaply; at build stage it is expensive.

    🗓️ Alignment mechanisms across programs

    • Integrated roadmap: one view of all initiatives by capability, showing overlap and dependencies.
    • Dependency management: shared milestones, release trains, integration testing environments.
    • PI / quarterly planning (SAFe) or cross-team planning to align increments.
    • Shared platforms and services: identity, payments, notification, customer master as reusable services.
    • Reference architectures and patterns: approved building blocks.
    • Technology radar: Adopt, Trial, Assess, Hold list of technologies.
    • Inner-source / API catalogue: discover and reuse existing components.

    💶 Funding models and incentives

    ModelEffect on alignment
    Project fundingEncourages local solutions, ignores shared cost
    Persistent product / platform fundingEnables shared platforms and long-term ownership
    Central funding for shared servicesOvercomes "tragedy of the commons"
    Chargeback / showbackMakes the cost of duplication visible
    Decommission KPIsRewards removing systems, not just adding
    ⚠️
    If divisions are rewarded only for their own P&L, alignment will fail no matter what the architecture says. Align incentives.

    Business Case, Migration and Decommissioning

    Quantifying savings, planning data and user migration, and avoiding the "zombie application" problem.

    📈 Building the consolidation business case

    Cost / benefitExamples
    Savings (run)Licences, hosting, support, maintenance staff, vendor contracts
    Savings (change)Fewer integrations, one change backlog, shared testing
    Benefits (business)Faster onboarding, unified customer view, better reporting
    Risk reductionRetire unsupported systems, lower cyber exposure
    One-time costsMigration, data cleansing, retraining, parallel run, contract exit fees
    Ongoing costsIncreased load on retained system
    Payback and NPVScenario: Consolidate 3 CRMs to 1
Annual run saving:        $2.4M   (licences 1.1, infra 0.8, support 0.5)
One-time cost:            $3.6M   (migration, training, parallel run)
Payback: 3.6 / 2.4 = 1.5 years
3-year NPV @ 8%:          approx. $2.6M   (cash flows -3.6, +2.4, +2.4, +2.4 discounted)
Risks: data quality, user adoption, contract exit fees

    🚚 Migration planning

    Data migration is usually the riskiest part of consolidation.
    • Data: profile, deduplicate, map fields, define golden record rules, reconcile post-migration.
    • Integrations: re-point or retire interfaces; maintain a dependency inventory.
    • Users: training, support, change champions per division.
    • Roll-out: by division, region or product; pilot first.
    • Fallback: define rollback criteria before cutover.

    🧟 Decommissioning and zombie applications

    Many consolidations "go live" but never switch off the old system, so cost is paid twice. Decommissioning must be a funded, tracked deliverable.

    1. Confirm no active users or dependencies.
    2. Archive data per retention and legal hold rules.
    3. Remove integrations and access; shut down infrastructure.
    4. Terminate licences and support contracts (watch notice periods).
    5. Update CMDB, architecture repository and portfolio.
    6. Verify and report realised savings.
    ⚠️
    Include decommission as a gate for programme closure — no savings, no closure.

    💡
    Intermediate skill is choosing between options and justifying the choice, not just naming them.

    🧠 Advanced strategies

    ChallengeExpert approach
    Post-merger duplicate estatesClassify by capability; choose "best of both" per capability using objective scoring; time-box transition services
    Divisions resisting standardisationOffer a service with SLAs, allow configurable variation, show cost via showback; escalate with CFO data
    Legacy with unknown dependenciesUse discovery tools (traffic analysis, CMDB), strangler pattern to migrate piece by piece
    Vendor sprawlVendor consolidation strategy with preferred supplier list and commercial leverage
    Duplicate dataMaster data management or data product ownership; reduce copies
    Too many integrationsAPI-led / event-driven integration, enterprise service bus retirement plan
    Cloud migration + rationalisationRationalise first, then migrate; never lift and shift duplicates
    💡
    Order of operations: rationalise, then modernise, then migrate. Moving redundant applications to the cloud simply relocates the waste.

    📏 Metrics that matter

    • Number of applications (total and per capability) and trend.
    • Percentage of capabilities with a single strategic application.
    • Annual run cost and cost per user or transaction.
    • Decommissions completed vs planned; realised vs planned savings.
    • Percentage of new projects reusing platforms or approved patterns.
    • Technical debt index; share of applications past end-of-support.
    • Number of unapproved purchases (shadow IT) detected.

    🎤 Expert insights and interview prep

    PointDetail
    What it isCross-program alignment is the practice of coordinating programmes and divisions so they share capabilities, platforms and data, while rationalisation removes redundant applications and technology.
    Why it is usedIt cuts run costs, reduces risk and complexity, accelerates change and creates a coherent customer experience.
    How it worksInventory and map applications to capabilities; assess business and technical fit; decide TIME and R dispositions; govern new demand; plan data and user migration; decommission and track benefits.
    How to use itStart with a pilot capability that has visible duplication and executive support; prove savings; scale with governance gates and integrated roadmaps.
    Common mistakesRationalising by technology only; ignoring data migration; never switching off old systems; imposing one process on different businesses; counting only licence savings; no sponsor.
    Real-world usageA manufacturer finds 14 ERP instances after acquisitions, maps them to finance and supply capabilities, standardises on 3 regional instances, retires 11, and saves 18% of ERP run cost within 30 months.
    Interview pointsExplain TIME and 6 Rs; compute payback for a consolidation; justify when NOT to consolidate; describe how you would run an ARB; explain how to handle a hostile division.
    ✅
    Strong answer: lead with business capability and value, quantify with a simple business case, then discuss risk and governance.
