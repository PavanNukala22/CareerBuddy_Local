    Agile Foundations & Framework Landscape

    Values, principles, empirical process control and the difference between a method, a framework and a practice.

    🌱 What “Agile” really means

    Agile is a mindset described in the 2001 Agile Manifesto: iterative delivery, fast feedback and adaptive planning under uncertainty. It is not a process; Scrum, Kanban, XP and SAFe are ways of applying it.

    Manifesto valuePrefersOver (still has value)
    1Individuals and interactionsProcesses and tools
    2Working softwareComprehensive documentation
    3Customer collaborationContract negotiation
    4Responding to changeFollowing a plan
    📌
    The Manifesto has 12 principles — e.g. “deliver working software frequently”, “welcome changing requirements”, “simplicity — the art of maximising the amount of work not done”. Expect “which principle…” questions.

    ⚖️ Predictive vs adaptive

    Predictive (waterfall)

    • Plan the whole scope up front
    • Phases: requirements → design → build → test
    • Change is controlled and costly
    • Fits stable, well-understood problems

    Adaptive (agile)

    • Plan in short horizons; learn and adjust
    • Increments delivered every 1–4 weeks
    • Change is welcome — cost of delay mindset
    • Fits uncertain, complex problems (Cynefin: complex)

    Empirical process control (Scrum’s theory) rests on three pillars: Transparency, Inspection, Adaptation.

    🧭 Method vs framework vs practice — “what is what”

    TermMeaningExamples
    Mindset / valuesBeliefs that guide choicesAgile Manifesto, Lean thinking
    FrameworkMinimal structure you fill in; incomplete by designScrum, Kanban Method, SAFe, LeSS, Nexus
    Methodology / methodPrescriptive set of roles, steps, artefactsDSDM, FDD, RUP
    Practice / techniqueA specific way of workingPair programming, TDD, planning poker, user stories
    ToolSoftware supporting the workJira, Azure DevOps, Linear, Trello
    🏉
    Scrum
    Time-boxed sprints, 3 accountabilities, 5 events, 3 artefacts.
    📋
    Kanban
    Visualise work, limit WIP, optimise flow; evolutionary change.
    🛠️
    XP
    Engineering practices: TDD, CI, pair programming, refactoring.
    🏢

    Scaling framework for many teams: ARTs, PI Planning, portfolio.
    🧩

    🎯

    🧑‍💼 Where does the BA fit?

    FrameworkBA positioning
    ScrumNo “BA” accountability. BA works as a Developer (team member), PO proxy, or product analyst feeding refinement. Accountability for ordering stays with the PO.
    KanbanExisting roles remain; BA clarifies requirements and policies, maintains replenishment quality.
    SAFeBA appears as Product Owner, Product Management support, Business Analyst on teams, or Solution/Enterprise analyst at Large Solution level.
    💡
    Titles vary across companies. In interviews, describe outcomes (clarity, slicing, validation) rather than defending a title.

    Scrum Core: Roles, Events, Artefacts

    The 2020 Scrum Guide: accountabilities, five events, three artefacts with commitments, and the values that hold it together.

    🏉 Scrum in one page

    One Sprint: planning sets the Sprint Goal; daily inspection; review inspects the Increment; retrospective improves the process.

    Scrum is a lightweight framework helping people generate value through adaptive solutions for complex problems. Five values: Commitment, Focus, Openness, Respect, Courage.

    👥 Accountabilities (roles)

    AccountabilityKey responsibilitiesNot responsible for
    Product OwnerMaximise value; Product Goal; develop and communicate the Product Backlog; order items. One person, not a committee.Telling developers how to build
    Scrum MasterTeam effectiveness; coach in Scrum; remove impediments; ensure events happen and are positive/productive; serve PO, team and organisation.Managing people or assigning work
    DevelopersCreate any aspect of a usable Increment each sprint: plan the Sprint Backlog, adhere to the DoD, adapt daily, hold each other accountable.Waiting for task assignments; being a separate “QA team” silo
    📌
    Scrum Team = 1 PO + 1 SM + Developers, typically ≤ 10 people, no sub-teams or hierarchies. “Developers” means anyone creating the Increment — including analysts, testers and designers.

    ⏱️ Events and timeboxes

    EventPurposeMax timebox (1-month sprint)
    The SprintContainer for all other events; produces a Done Increment≤ 1 month (shorter is common)
    Sprint PlanningWhy is the sprint valuable (goal)? What can be Done? How will the work get done?8 hours
    Daily ScrumInspect progress to Sprint Goal; adapt the plan for next 24 hours15 minutes
    Sprint ReviewInspect outcome with stakeholders; adapt backlog; not just a demo4 hours
    Sprint RetrospectivePlan ways to increase quality and effectiveness3 hours

    For shorter sprints the timeboxes are usually shorter. A sprint can be cancelled only by the Product Owner if the Sprint Goal becomes obsolete.

    📦 Artefacts and their commitments

    ArtefactCommitmentMeaning
    Product BacklogProduct GoalLong-term objective the Scrum Team plans against
    Sprint BacklogSprint GoalSingle objective for the sprint; flexible about the work, fixed on the goal
    IncrementDefinition of DoneA usable, inspectable step toward the Product Goal; must meet the DoD
    ⚠️
    Scrum-but examples: “we have a Scrum Master, but he’s also the project manager who assigns tasks”; “we skip retros when busy”; “the PO is a committee”. These weaken inspection and adaptation.

    Scrum Ceremonies in Depth

    How to run each event well, what the BA prepares and contributes, and which metrics help (and which hurt).

    📝 Sprint Planning step by step

    TopicQuestionBA contribution
    1. WhyWhy is this sprint valuable? → Sprint GoalLink goal to outcome/KR; challenge “goal = list of tickets”
    2. WhatWhich Product Backlog items can we complete?Ensure ready items; clarify AC; surface dependencies
    3. HowHow will we do it? → plan/tasksBe available for questions; data/UX rules; test data
    sprint-goalWeak goal:  "Complete tickets PAY-101 to PAY-114"
Good goal:  "Let returning customers pay with a saved card in under 3 clicks,
             so we can measure uplift in repeat checkout conversion."
    💡
    Capacity-based planning: Capacity = people × available days × focus factor. Example: 6 people × 9 days × 0.7 = 37.8 person-days; use yesterday’s weather (recent velocity) as the forecast, not as a target.

    🔄 Daily Scrum, Review and Retrospective

    EventHealthy practiceAnti-pattern
    Daily ScrumDevelopers plan the next 24 h around the Sprint Goal; any format; walk the board right-to-leftStatus report to the manager; “yesterday/today/blockers” read from Jira
    Sprint ReviewWorking Increment, discuss market/user feedback, revise backlog & forecastsSlide-show demo with no stakeholder input; sign-off ceremony
    RetrospectivePick 1–2 improvement actions and put them in the next Sprint BacklogVenting list with no owner; same actions repeated every sprint
    Retro formatHow it works
    Start / Stop / ContinueThree columns; vote; pick actions
    4LsLiked, Learned, Lacked, Longed for
    SailboatWind (helps), anchors (slows), rocks (risks), island (goal)
    Mad / Sad / GladEmotion-based, good for team health
    TimelinePlot events and mood over the sprint

    📅 A two-week sprint calendar

    DayEvent / activityTypical BA tasks
    Mon wk1Sprint Planning (≤ 4 h)Walk through top stories; confirm AC; capture decisions
    DailyDaily Scrum (15 min)Answer clarification questions same day; update AC changes
    Wed wk1Refinement #1 (1 h)Present next 2 sprints’ candidates; split; spikes
    Mon wk2Refinement #2 (1 h)Review readiness; resolve red cards
    Thu wk2Acceptance testing / UAT previewValidate AC with PO; log defects
    Fri wk2Sprint Review (≤ 2 h) → Retrospective (≤ 1.5 h)Capture feedback as backlog items; record decisions

    📏 Metrics: use carefully

    MetricUseMisuse
    VelocityForecast the next sprint’s capacity for one teamComparing teams; setting velocity targets
    Burndown (sprint)See if scope is on track for the sprintUsing it as a surveillance tool
    Burnup (release)Show scope change vs progress (scope line + done line)Ignoring scope growth
    Commitment reliability% of planned stories finishedPunishing misses instead of learning
    Cycle time / escaped defectsQuality and flow signalSingle-number targets without context
    📌
    A burndown can look perfect while delivering nothing valuable. The Sprint Goal and Increment are the real tests.

    Kanban & Flow Metrics

    Visualise, limit WIP and manage flow — with Little’s Law, aging WIP, classes of service and how Kanban differs from Scrum.

    📋 Kanban Method fundamentals

    Kanban Method (David J. Anderson)
    Foundational principlesStart with what you do now · Agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change · Initially respect current roles and responsibilities · Encourage acts of leadership at every level
    Six core practicesVisualise · Limit WIP · Manage flow · Make policies explicit · Implement feedback loops · Improve collaboratively, evolve experimentally

    A Kanban board shows workflow states as columns, items as cards. A WIP limit caps how many items may be in a column/state at once, creating a pull system — new work starts only when capacity frees up.

    boardBacklog | Analysis (WIP 3) | Dev (WIP 4) | Review (WIP 2) | Test (WIP 3) | Done
 ...    | [A][B][C]        | [D][E][F][G]| [H]           | [I][J]       | [K]
        (at limit)         (at limit)    (1 free)        (1 free)
-> Analysis and Dev are full: stop starting; swarm on Review/Test to finish.
    💡
    Policy examples: “A card is Ready only when AC are written and UX is attached”; “Expedite items max 1 at a time”; “Blocked cards get a red flag and are discussed daily”.

    🌊 Flow metrics & Little’s Law

    MetricDefinition
    WIPItems started but not finished
    ThroughputItems finished per unit time
    Cycle timeTime from start to finish of an item
    Lead timeRequest received → delivered
    Work item age (aging WIP)Time since an in-progress item started — your early warning
    Service Level Expectation (SLE)“85% of items finish in ≤ 8 days” — a percentile forecast
    littles-lawLittle's Law:   Avg Cycle Time = Avg WIP / Avg Throughput

Example:  WIP = 24 items, Throughput = 6 items/week
          Cycle time = 24 / 6 = 4 weeks
Cut WIP to 12 (same throughput)  ->  12 / 6 = 2 weeks
    📌
    Little’s Law holds for stable systems (arrivals ≈ departures, no aging WIP explosion). It explains why starting less finishes more.

    Cumulative Flow Diagram (CFD): the vertical gap between bands = WIP; horizontal gap = approximate cycle time; widening bands = bottleneck forming; flat “Done” line = nothing is being delivered.

    🚦 Classes of service & cadences

    Class of serviceMeaningPolicy example
    ExpediteCost of delay is extreme and immediate (outage, legal)Max 1 in flight; may break WIP limits
    Fixed dateValue drops sharply after a date (regulatory deadline)Start early enough using SLE; track risk
    StandardNormal value; FIFO or value-based pullDefault
    IntangibleValue later (refactoring, learning)Allocate ~10–20% capacity
    Kanban cadencePurpose
    ReplenishmentChoose what enters the system next
    Kanban (stand-up) meetingWalk the board, unblock work
    Delivery planningCoordinate release/delivery
    Service delivery reviewInspect fitness for purpose (SLE, metrics)
    Risk / operations / strategy reviewHigher-level feedback loops

    🥊 Scrum vs Kanban vs Scrumban

    AspectScrumKanbanScrumban
    CadenceFixed-length sprintsContinuous flowHybrid: planning on demand
    RolesPO, SM, DevelopersNo prescribed rolesTeam decides
    CommitmentSprint Goal + forecastWIP limits and SLEWIP limits + optional goals
    Change mid-cycleDiscouraged during sprintAllowed if WIP permitsAllowed with policy

    Scaled Agile Framework (SAFe)

    Configurations, the seven core competencies, the Agile Release Train, PI Planning agenda, ROAM risks and Inspect & Adapt.

    🏢 SAFe at a glance

    SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework, version 6.0 at time of writing — always check scaledagileframework.com for newer releases) gives large organisations a structure to align many teams around business agility.

    ConfigurationForAdds
    Essential SAFeOne ART (50–125 people)Team & Technical Agility, Agile Product Delivery; PI Planning; Agile Release Train
    Large Solution SAFeMultiple ARTs building one large solutionSolution Train, Solution Intent, Capabilities, Enterprise Solution Delivery
    Portfolio SAFeFunding & strategy alignmentLean Portfolio Management, Strategic Themes, Epics, Lean budgets, Portfolio Kanban
    Full SAFeEnterprise with all levelsEverything above combined
    Lean-Agile LeadershipTeam & Technical AgilityAgile Product DeliveryEnterprise Solution DeliveryLean Portfolio ManagementOrganizational AgilityContinuous Learning Culture

    Above: the seven core competencies.

    🧱 Work item hierarchy and roles

    Work itemLevelNotes
    Strategic ThemePortfolioDifferentiator that connects strategy to portfolio
    Epic (Business / Enabler)PortfolioNeeds Lean Business Case if large; MVP defined with hypothesis
    CapabilityLarge SolutionSolution-level behaviour split into Features
    FeatureProgram (ART)Fits one PI; has benefit hypothesis + acceptance criteria
    StoryTeamFits one iteration
    Enabler (Architectural / Infrastructure / Compliance / Exploration)AllSupports future business functionality
    RoleResponsibility
    Release Train Engineer (RTE)Chief Scrum Master of the ART; facilitates PI Planning, ART Sync, I&A
    Product ManagementOwns Program Backlog, vision and roadmap; prioritises Features
    System Architect / EngineerTechnical direction, architectural runway, NFRs
    Business OwnersKey stakeholders; assign business value to PI Objectives
    Product Owner (team)Team backlog, stories, acceptance
    Business Analyst (when present)Elicits/clarifies requirements for features and stories, supports PO, models, validates

    🗓️ PI Planning — the heartbeat

    Cadence-based planning: a PI contains iterations plus an IP iteration; PI Planning kicks off and I&A closes the PI.
    Day 1Day 2
    Business context · Product/solution vision · Architecture vision & development practices · Planning context & lunch · Team breakout #1 (draft plans) · Draft plan review · Management review & problem solvingPlanning adjustments · Team breakout #2 (final plans, PI Objectives with business value) · Final plan review & lunch · Program risks (ROAM) · Confidence vote · Plan rework if needed · Planning retrospective & moving forward
    ROAMMeaning
    ResolvedEliminated; no longer a concern
    OwnedSomeone takes ownership and action
    AcceptedCannot be mitigated; accept the consequence
    MitigatedPlan reduces impact or probability
    ✅
    Confidence vote: fist of five. Average ≥ 3 → commit; any individual vote of 1–2 should voice concerns. Teams create PI Objectives with planned business value (1–10) and stretch objectives that are not committed.

    🔍 Inspect & Adapt, WSJF and the Program Board

    ConceptDetail
    Inspect & Adapt (I&A)End-of-PI event: PI System Demo → quantitative & qualitative measurement → retrospective & problem-solving workshop (root cause, backlog of improvement items)
    System DemoIntegrated view of the Increment at end of each iteration
    Program BoardVisual: features, milestones, dependencies (red strings) between teams
    Innovation & Planning (IP) iterationBuffer for innovation, learning, planning, I&A; improves predictability
    WSJFCost of Delay ÷ Job Size. CoD = User/Business Value + Time Criticality + Risk Reduction/Opportunity Enablement (relative Fibonacci scores)
    Architectural runwayExisting technical foundation that lets features be added without major redesign
    ⚠️
    SAFe misuse: PI Planning as top-down task allocation; teams not co-creating plans; treating stretch objectives as commitments; IP iteration filled with spill-over work.

    Release Planning Workflows

    Forecasting and release planning in Scrum, Kanban and SAFe — capacity maths, release burnups, probabilistic forecasting and release readiness.

    🚀 Release planning in Scrum

    Scrum has no “release plan” event. Teams use release forecasts based on velocity range and a ranked backlog. A release can happen at any time once the PO decides the Increment is valuable.

    StepActionOutput
    1Define release goal & constraints (date or scope)Fixed-date vs fixed-scope decision
    2Story-map and order backlogSlices; must-have vs nice-to-have
    3Estimate sizeTotal points (with ranges)
    4Use velocity range (low/avg/high)Date range or scope range
    5Track with release burnupScope line vs completed line
    6Reforecast every sprintAdjust scope/date with stakeholders
    forecastRemaining backlog for release: 240 points
Velocity last 6 sprints: 28, 31, 24, 30, 26, 29  -> low 24 / avg 28 / high 31
Sprints needed:  240/31 = 7.7 -> 8   |   240/28 = 8.6 -> 9   |   240/24 = 10
Forecast: 8-10 sprints (a RANGE, not a date). 2-week sprints -> 16-20 weeks.
    💡
    Never present a single date from average velocity. Use ranges, then narrow them as you learn. Show the release burnup so stakeholders see scope change separately from progress.

    🎲 Probabilistic forecasting (Kanban)

    Instead of points, Kanban teams use throughput history and Monte Carlo simulation (random sampling of historical weekly throughput) to answer: “When will these 40 items be done?” with a confidence level.

    QuestionMethodAnswer shape
    How long will one item take?Cycle-time percentiles (SLE)“85% within 8 days”
    When will N items finish?Monte Carlo on weekly throughput“85% chance by week 11”
    How many items by a date?Monte Carlo (how many)“85% chance of ≥ 32 items by 30 Nov”
    📌
    Probabilistic forecasts require reasonably stable work item sizes and a policy to keep items similarly small (“right-sizing”).

    🧮 PI planning workflow and capacity maths

    StepDetails
    Pre-planningProduct Management prepares vision/roadmap; Program Backlog top ~10 features WSJF-ordered; teams’ backlogs readied; logistics (RTE)
    Capacity per iterationPeople × days − holidays/leave − ceremonies; then focus factor
    Load storiesTeams pull features, split into stories, place into iterations; keep load ≤ capacity; reserve 10–20% for unplanned
    DependenciesRed strings on Program Board; negotiate order; use ROAM
    CommitConfidence vote; commit to PI Objectives, not to every story
    capacityTeam of 7 (5 dev, 1 QA, 1 BA), 10-day iteration, 1 person on leave 3 days
Available person-days = 7*10 - 3 = 67
Ceremonies/admin ~ 10%  -> 60.3
Focus factor 0.8 (support, meetings) -> ~48 person-days
Velocity baseline 36 pts for ~54 person-days -> 0.67 pts/person-day
Planned points = 48 * 0.67 = ~32  (plan to 32, not 36)

    Scaling, Choosing & Expert Playbook

    LeSS, Nexus, Scrum@Scale, Spotify-style models, choosing a framework, and diagnosing common failures.

    🧩 Scaling frameworks compared

    FrameworkCore ideaKey structures
    SAFeAlignment through cadence & Lean portfolioART, PI Planning, RTE, Solution Train
    LeSSScrum scaled with minimal added process; one PO, one Product Backlog, one sprint, one IncrementFeature teams, Area POs in LeSS Huge
    Nexus3–9 Scrum teams with an integration focusNexus Integration Team, Nexus Sprint Planning, Nexus Sprint Goal
    Scrum@ScaleScale-free architecture; Scrum of ScrumsSoS, MetaScrum, Executive Action Team
    Disciplined Agile (DA)Toolkit of choices; context-sensitiveProcess goals, lifecycles
    Spotify modelCultural pattern, not a framework (squads, tribes, chapters, guilds)Autonomous squads
    ⚠️
    The “Spotify model” was a snapshot of how one company worked at one time; copying titles without the culture rarely works.

    🧭 Choosing a framework — decision guide

    SituationLean towardWhy
    One product team, unclear requirementsScrumShort feedback loops and goal-driven sprints
    Support / operations, unpredictable arrivalsKanbanFlow, SLE, no sprint planning waste
    5–12 teams, shared roadmap, regulated enterpriseSAFe (or Nexus/LeSS if mature)Cadence, dependency management, portfolio alignment
    Strong Scrum teams, want minimal overheadLeSSSimplicity and feature teams
    Mixed project + BAU workScrumbanSprint-like goals with flow policies

    Rule: fix the problem, then pick the framework — start by identifying constraint (dependencies? unclear priorities? slow QA?).

    🩺 Diagnosing failure patterns

    SymptomLikely root causeBA-level action
    Stories carry over each sprintLarge stories, hidden dependencies, too much WIPRe-slice; dependency map; limit WIP
    Constant mid-sprint changesWeak PO prioritisation or Sprint Goal absentStrengthen goal; evidence for value; decide what pauses
    Demo = no feedbackWrong stakeholders; review as presentationInvite real users; collect hypotheses; track outcomes
    PI plan “fantasy”No capacity maths; dependencies hiddenCapacity per team; Program Board; ROAM
    Velocity inflatingTargets or comparisonRemove targets; focus on cycle time and outcomes
    Teams blocked on each otherComponent teams; shared servicesFeature teams; API contracts; reduce coupling (Conway)

    🏦 Expert case: 5-team bank onboarding programme

    Context: A retail bank must launch digital onboarding in 9 months. Five teams: Mobile, Web, KYC Services, Core Banking Integration, Fraud. Past releases slipped due to dependencies.

    DecisionReasoning
    One ART with a 10-week PI, 2-week iterationsShared roadmap; common cadence; regulatory milestones
    Feature teams for Mobile/Web onboarding; platform teams for KYC & Core integrationMix due to legacy constraints; explicit APIs
    Program Board with red-string dependenciesMake cross-team ordering visible; ROAM the risks
    Enabler Features for integration runway (Exploration spikes first)Reduces PI 1 risk on unknown APIs
    Cycle time and predictability (PI Objective %)Replace velocity comparisons
    BA acts as Feature owner for Onboarding; writes Feature benefit hypothesis and acceptance criteriaAligns business outcome with delivery detail
    ✅
    Interview angle: describe what you would measure: PI predictability, cycle time, dependency resolution time, defect escape rate, onboarding conversion.
