    Lean & Value Foundations

    What “value” means, the eight wastes, flow vs batch, push vs pull — the vocabulary behind every value stream map.

    💎 Value, value stream and flow

    A value stream is the end-to-end sequence of activities needed to take a customer request from trigger to delivered value, including information and material flow. Value is what the customer would be willing to pay for or what achieves a business outcome.

    Activity classMeaningSoftware example
    Value-adding (VA)Transforms the product in a way the customer valuesDesigning, coding, testing a feature
    Necessary non-value-adding (NNVA)Doesn’t add value but required now (law, risk, current tech)Regulatory review, security scan
    Non-value-adding (waste / muda)Consumes resources, no valueWaiting for approval, rework, hand-offs
    📌
    Lean’s five principles (Womack & Jones): Specify value → Map the value stream → Create flow → Establish pull → Pursue perfection.

    🗑️ The eight wastes (DOWNTIME) and software equivalents

    WasteManufacturingSoftware / BA examples
    DefectsScrap, reworkBugs, misunderstood requirements, escaped defects
    OverproductionMaking more than neededBuilding features nobody uses; gold-plated scope
    WaitingIdle machines/peopleWaiting for approvals, environments, answers to questions
    Non-utilised talentSkills unusedAnalysts doing data entry; devs excluded from discovery
    TransportationMoving materialHandoffs between teams/tools; copy-pasting between systems
    InventoryStock waitingLarge backlog; partially done work; unmerged branches
    MotionUnnecessary movementContext switching; searching for information
    Extra processingDoing more than requiredGold-plating; redundant approvals; duplicate documentation
    💡
    Poppendieck’s 7 wastes of software development: partially done work, extra features, relearning, handoffs, delays, task switching, defects. Useful to quote in interviews.

    🔀 Push vs pull, batch vs flow

    Push / batch

    • Work is assigned/started regardless of downstream capacity
    • Large batches (big releases, big requirement documents)
    • High WIP → long queues, late feedback
    • Local optimisation (each team busy)

    Pull / flow

    • Work starts when downstream has capacity (WIP limits)
    • Small batches (small stories, frequent releases)
    • Low WIP → short lead time, early feedback
    • System optimisation (whole value stream)
    ConceptDefinition
    Lead timeElapsed time from request to delivery
    Process (touch) timeTime actually worked on the item
    Wait (queue) timeTime spent idle between steps
    Takt timeAvailable time ÷ customer demand — the rate demand requires
    Batch sizeAmount of work moved together
    Flow efficiencyProcess time ÷ lead time

    VSM Basics: Symbols, Types & Steps

    What a VSM is, which kinds exist, how it differs from process mapping, and how to run a mapping workshop.

    🗺️ What is a VSM and which types exist

    TypePurposeTypical use
    Current-state mapShows how work really flows today, with times and queuesBaseline and bottleneck discovery
    Future-state mapDesigned improved flow achievable in 3–12 monthsImprovement roadmap
    Ideal-state mapPerfect flow with no waste (a direction, not a plan)Vision / stretch thinking
    Process-level VSMSingle team/process (e.g. “code to production”)Team improvement
    Enterprise / extended VSMSpans departments (e.g. “concept to cash”)Transformation, portfolio decisions
    Information-flow VSMFocus on information handoffs and decisionsKnowledge work, BA processes
    Value Stream MapProcess map (BPMN/flowchart)
    FocusTime, queues, flow, wasteLogic, decisions, roles
    Key measuresLead time, process time, %C&A, WIPRarely quantitative
    ScopeEnd-to-end customer flowSingle process or sub-process
    OutcomeWhere to improve flowHow the process works

    🔣 Standard symbols (what is what)

    SymbolMeaning
    Process boxA step performed by a team/function (verb + noun, e.g. “Code feature”)
    Data boxMetrics under the process: Process Time (PT), Lead Time (LT), %C&A, WIP, people, batch size
    Inventory / queue triangleWork waiting between steps (count and wait time)
    Push arrowWork pushed to next step without pull signal
    Supermarket / pull / kanban signalPull system between steps
    Information flow arrowsOrders, schedules, approvals, tickets
    Customer / supplier iconsStart/end of stream
    Kaizen burst (lightning)Improvement opportunity marked on the map
    Timeline ladderBottom line alternating wait (up) and process (down) times, summing to lead time
    📌
    %C&A (percent complete and accurate): the % of items arriving at a step that the next step can use without correction or clarification. Rolled %C&A = product of each step’s %C&A.

    🧑‍🤝‍🧑 How to run a mapping workshop

    1. Define scope and trigger/end: e.g. “customer request accepted” → “feature live in production”.
    2. Bring the right people who actually do the work: BA, dev, QA, ops, product, support, compliance — 6–10 people.
    3. Walk the process backward from delivery (“go to the gemba”) using real tickets, not ideal flows.
    4. Capture data per step: PT, wait, WIP, %C&A, handoffs. Use ranges (median and 85th percentile) if variable.
    5. Draw the timeline ladder; calculate lead time, total process time and flow efficiency.
    6. Mark kaizen bursts: top bottlenecks and waste.
    7. Design the future state and an improvement plan with owners and metrics.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes: mapping the “should be” process, mapping only your own team, using averages without variance, and ending with a poster but no improvement backlog.

    Current-State Mapping & Metrics

    Build a data-driven current-state map for a software delivery stream and compute lead time, flow efficiency and rolled %C&A.

    📐 A worked current-state map

    Stream: “Approved idea → feature live” in a retail-banking app team.

    Current-state VSM: yellow triangles are queues; the largest waits are before Refinement (20 d) and before Release (7 d).
    StepProcess time (PT)Wait before (W)%C&A
    Refine (BA/PO)1 d20 d100%
    Develop3 d5 d95%
    Code review0.5 d2 d90%
    QA2 d4 d80%
    UAT / approval1 d6 d85%
    Release0.5 d7 d98%
    Total8 d44 d—

    🧮 Key calculations

    formulasLead Time (LT)       = sum(PT) + sum(Wait) = 8 + 44 = 52 days
Flow Efficiency (FE) = PT / LT = 8 / 52 = 15.4%
Rolled %C&A          = 1.00 x 0.95 x 0.90 x 0.80 x 0.85 x 0.98 = 0.57  (57%)
                       -> only 57 of every 100 items pass through with no rework/clarification

Takt time            = available time / demand
                       e.g. 20 working days / 10 features demanded = 2 days per feature
                       Process step with PT > takt cannot keep up unless parallelised

Little's Law check   : WIP = Throughput x LT. If 5 features/month (0.25/day), LT 52 d
                       -> expected WIP ~ 13 features in the stream
    MetricFormulaHow a BA uses it
    Lead timeΣ PT + Σ WCustomer-facing speed; baseline for improvement
    Flow efficiencyPT ÷ LTHow much of the time is “real work”; typical software 5–15%
    Rolled %C&AΠ %C&AQuality of information/work handed on; highlights BA-related rework
    Takt timeTime available ÷ demandPace needed to meet demand
    Cycle time (per step)End − start of the stepLocate slow steps
    💡
    BA lever: low %C&A at Develop and QA often means ambiguous requirements. Improving acceptance criteria quality directly increases rolled %C&A and reduces rework queues.

    🔎 Data collection techniques

    TechniqueHowCaution
    Ticket analysis (Jira/ADO)Export status transitions; compute time in each statusStatuses must reflect reality; fix data first
    Pipeline dataCI/CD timestamps: commit → deployMisses pre-dev wait time
    Gemba walk / interviewsAsk the people who did last month’s 5 itemsMemory bias; verify with data
    SamplingTrace 10–20 recent real items end-to-endPick representative sizes/types
    PercentilesUse 50th/85th/95th instead of meanSkewed data hides in averages
    ⚠️
    Avoid “clean” data: if all items show 1-day waits, your statuses were probably edited retroactively. Look for items that “teleport” through multiple statuses.

    Identifying Bottlenecks in Delivery Pipelines

    Theory of Constraints, queueing intuition, CFDs, DORA and Flow Framework metrics — find the real constraint, not the loudest complaint.

    ⛓️ Theory of Constraints (TOC)

    Every system has at least one constraint limiting throughput. Improving a non-constraint step does not improve the system — it only creates more WIP in front of the constraint.

    TOC stepQuestionPipeline example
    1. IdentifyWhich step limits throughput?QA completes 4 items/week; Dev 8/week
    2. ExploitSqueeze the most from the constraint without spendingQA works only ready items; remove meetings; test data ready
    3. SubordinateEverything else supports the constraintDev starts only what QA can absorb (WIP limit)
    4. ElevateInvest to increase the constraint capacityAutomate regression; add testers; shift-left testing
    5. RepeatConstraint moves — find the next oneAfter QA is fixed, UAT becomes the constraint
    📌
    “An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole system.” An hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage.

    📉 Queueing intuition: why high utilisation hurts

    For a simple single-server queue (M/M/1), queue wait relative to service time grows as ρ / (1 − ρ), where ρ = utilisation.

    Utilisation ρWait ÷ service timeMeaning
    50%1.0×Wait equals work time
    70%2.3×
    80%4.0×Wait is 4 times the work time
    90%9.0×Wait is 9× the work time
    95%19×System nearly unusable
    ✅
    Takeaway for BAs: running everybody at 100% (“keep the analysts busy”) increases lead time. Slack is the price of flow, and variability (unclear requirements) makes it worse.

    📡 Signals of a bottleneck

    SignalWhere to lookTypical cause
    Queue growing in front of a stepBoard columns, CFD band wideningCapacity < arrival rate
    Downstream starvationIdle devs/QA waiting for inputUpstream (e.g. analysis) too slow or uneven
    Work item age risingAging WIP chartBlocked work; waiting for decisions
    Frequent “expedite”Class-of-service mixPoor ordering or hidden constraint
    Rework loopItems moving backwardsLow %C&A; unclear AC; late testing
    Heroics / overtime in one teamTeam loadSpecialist bottleneck (single expert)
    Bottleneck typeExampleTypical remedy
    People/skillOne security reviewerCross-train; pairing; delegate to automation
    Process / policyCAB meets weeklyStandard changes pre-approved; risk-based approvals
    HandoffRequirements → dev → QA silosCross-functional team; Three Amigos
    EnvironmentOne shared test envEphemeral environments; service virtualisation
    Tool / integrationManual deploymentCI/CD automation
    InformationWaiting for business answersEmbed product expert; decision SLAs

    📊 Measuring the pipeline: DORA and the Flow Framework

    DORA metricMeasuresNotes
    Deployment frequencyHow often you deploy to productionThroughput
    Lead time for changesCommit → running in productionSpeed (code to prod; not idea to prod)
    Change failure rate% deployments causing failureStability
    Failed-deployment recovery time (formerly MTTR)Time to restore service after a failed changeStability; recent DORA reports also track rework rate
    Flow Framework (Mik Kersten)Definition
    Flow TimeStart-to-finish elapsed time of an item (like lead time)
    Flow VelocityNumber of flow items completed per time period
    Flow EfficiencyActive time ÷ total flow time
    Flow LoadNumber of flow items in progress (WIP)
    Flow DistributionMix of Features / Defects / Risks / Debts (reveals capacity allocation)
    Flow PredictabilityPlanned vs actual delivery
    ⚠️
    DORA lead time starts at commit. A BA’s biggest waste is often before commit (idea → ready). Map the entire stream, not just the pipeline.

    Future-State Design & Continuous Improvement

    Kaizen, A3, 5 Whys, improvement kata and a worked before/after example showing how smaller batches and WIP limits change lead time.

    🛣️ Designing the future-state map

    The future state is not a dream; it is the next achievable condition, 3–6 months away, that attacks the biggest wastes first. Use these questions (adapted from Rother & Shook):

    • What does the customer really need and how fast (takt/demand)?
    • Where can we create continuous flow (remove handoffs and queues)?
    • Where do we need pull (WIP limits) because flow isn’t possible?
    • Which step is the pacemaker (schedule only here)?
    • Where can we shift left (test earlier, involve QA and ops in refinement)?
    • What small improvements (kaizen bursts) can start next week?
    Improvement leverEffect on the mapExample
    Reduce batch sizeShorter queues; faster feedbackStories of ≤ 3 days; release weekly
    Limit WIPShorter cycle time (Little’s Law)WIP = team size + 1
    Remove handoffsFewer waits; higher %C&ACross-functional squad; Three Amigos
    AutomateCuts PT and waitRegression suite; CI/CD
    ParalleliseShorter elapsed timeSecurity review begins during development
    Eliminate stepRemoves NNVAReplace CAB with automated policy checks

    🧰 Improvement toolkit

    ToolWhat it isWhen to use
    5 WhysAsk “why?” until a systemic root cause emergesSingle recurring defect or delay
    Ishikawa (fishbone)Categorised causes (people, process, tools, environment…)Multiple possible causes
    A3 problem solvingOne-page structured story: background, current state, goal, root cause, countermeasures, follow-upCommunicating improvement proposals
    PDCA / DemingPlan–Do–Check–Act cycleIterative experiments
    Improvement KataUnderstand direction → grasp current condition → next target → experimentBuilding a habit of improvement
    Kaizen eventShort focused workshop to fix a specific flow problemQuick wins
    Pareto analysis80/20: rank causes by impactChoose what to fix first
    5whysProblem: Features wait ~6 days for UAT sign-off.
Why? -> The business reviewer is available only on Thursdays.
Why? -> UAT is scheduled as a weekly session by the change manager.
Why? -> Policy requires sign-off in a single meeting for audit evidence.
Why? -> Auditors want recorded approval; nobody checked if automated evidence works.
Why? -> No-one owns the evidence requirement.
Countermeasure: pre-agree standard change categories + automated evidence (tickets/test reports)
                with auditors; sign-off asynchronously within 1 business day.

    📈 Worked before / after

    MetricCurrentAfter 3 months of kaizenHow
    Wait time44 d14 dWIP limits; right-sized stories; async approvals
    Process time8 d7 dAutomated regression
    Lead time52 d21 d—
    Flow efficiency15.4%33.3%7 ÷ 21
    Rolled %C&A57%78%Gherkin AC + Three Amigos
    Deployment frequencyMonthlyWeeklyPipeline automation
    ✅

    ⚠️
    Beware sub-optimisation: speeding up Dev without enough QA/UAT capacity will increase WIP and rework — apply TOC first.

    ROI & Business Value

    Quantify value: ROI, payback, NPV, Cost of Delay, value types and benefit realisation — and the traps to avoid.

    💰 Business value types and measures

    Value typeExample metrics
    Revenue growthConversion rate, ARPU, upsell, new customers
    Cost reductionHandling time, FTE hours, infrastructure cost, rework cost
    Risk reductionExpected loss avoided (probability × impact), audit findings
    CompliancePenalty avoided; licence to operate
    Customer valueNPS, CSAT, churn, task success
    Strategic / option valueCapability enabling future bets
    📌
    Use leading indicators (activation, adoption) to learn early and lagging indicators (revenue, churn) to confirm value. Define the metric before building.

    🧮 ROI, payback, NPV — formulas and examples

    financeROI (%)           = (Net benefit / Cost) x 100        where Net benefit = Benefit - Cost
Payback period   = Initial cost / Net annual benefit
NPV              = sum( CashFlow_t / (1 + r)^t ) - Initial investment
Benefit-Cost Ratio (BCR) = PV(benefits) / PV(costs)

Example (first-year view)
 Feature 1: cost 100k, annual benefit 250k -> ROI = (250-100)/100 = 150%, payback = 100/250 = 0.4 yr (4.8 months)
 Feature 2: cost 300k, annual benefit 450k -> ROI = 50%,  payback = 300/450 = 0.67 yr (8 months)
 Feature 3: cost  50k, annual benefit  60k -> ROI = 20%,  payback = 50/60  = 0.83 yr (10 months)

NPV (Feature 1, 3 years, r = 10%, benefit 250k/yr):
 annuity factor = 2.4869 -> PV benefits = 621.7k -> NPV = 621.7 - 100 = 521.7k
    MetricStrengthWeakness
    ROISimple, comparableIgnores time value and scale (50k at 20% vs 300k at 50%)
    PaybackEasy risk view (cash recovery)Ignores benefits after payback and time value
    NPVAccounts for time value of moneyNeeds discount rate and forecasts
    IRRRate that makes NPV = 0Misleading with multiple sign changes
    TCOIncludes run costs and maintenanceEffort to estimate

    ⏳ Cost of Delay (CoD) and CD3

    Cost of Delay = value lost per unit time by not having the feature (e.g. £ per week). CD3 = Cost of Delay ÷ Duration. Sequencing by highest CD3 minimises total delay cost.

    FeatureCoD (k/week)Duration (weeks)CD3
    A2045.0
    B10110.0
    C40104.0
    sequenceOrder B -> A -> C : B ends wk1, A wk5, C wk15
  delay cost = 10*1 + 20*5 + 40*15 = 10 + 100 + 600 = 710k
Order A -> B -> C : 20*4 + 10*5 + 40*15 = 80 + 50 + 600 = 730k
Order C -> A -> B : 40*10 + 20*14 + 10*15 = 400 + 280 + 150 = 830k
=> Highest CD3 first (B, A, C) is cheapest. WSJF is the relative-score version of CD3.
    CoD profileShapeExample
    StandardLinear loss over timeRevenue feature
    ExpediteSteep immediate lossProduction outage
    Fixed dateZero until deadline, then cliffRegulation effective 1 Jan
    IntangibleSlow, grows laterTech debt, security hardening

    🕳️ Pitfalls

    PitfallFix
    Vanity metrics (page views, tickets closed)Tie to outcomes (conversion, cost per case)
    Double counting benefits across featuresAttribute once; use experiment baselines
    False precisionUse ranges and confidence levels
    Ignoring cost of running (TCO)Include support/maintenance
    Never verifying (benefits realisation)Plan measurement and a post-release review

    Prioritising Features with Flow Data & Expert Case

    Combine VSM findings, ROI and CoD to prioritise improvement work and features; run an enterprise VSM; and work through a loan-approval case.

    🔗 From map to prioritised backlog

    StepOutput
    1. MapCurrent-state VSM with wait/PT/%C&A
    2. Find constraintsTop 3 bottlenecks and wastes
    3. Hypothesise“If we cut UAT wait from 6 d to 1 d, lead time falls 10% and CoD recovered = X”
    4. Size benefitsROI / CoD per improvement & feature
    5. RankCD3 / WSJF; consider risk and dependencies
    6. ExperimentSmallest testable change; measure flow metrics
    7. RepeatNew constraint, new map
    Flow Framework: Flow DistributionHealthy signalWarning
    Features50–70% of capacity (typical target range varies)< 30% → value work starved
    DefectsStable or fallingGrowing → quality problem in the stream
    RisksPlanned share (security/compliance)Zero → hidden risk
    Debts10–20% allocatedZero → future slowdown
    💡
    Flow distribution is a capacity-allocation argument: “we spend 55% of capacity on defects; reducing defects by half returns capacity worth a team of 3.”

    🏢 Value Streams in SAFe: operational vs development

    Value streamDefinitionExample
    Operational value streamSteps that deliver value to the customer using the solutionLoan origination: apply → verify → decide → disburse
    Development value streamSteps to build/maintain the systems that enable the operational streamIdea → code → test → deploy loan platform

    A SAFe Value Stream Identification workshop defines operational streams first, then the systems supporting them, then the people/teams — the basis for Agile Release Trains. This is VSM used for organisational design.

    🏦 Expert case: loan approval

    Context: A bank’s personal-loan approval takes 14 days median; competitors approve in 1 day. Applications drop out at 38%.

    StepPTWait%C&AObservation
    Online application0.2 d070%30% need manual correction
    Document verification0.5 d2 d75%Manual PDF checks; one team
    Credit scoring0.1 d1 d95%Automated but batch at night
    Underwriter review0.5 d6 d85%Queue of 220 cases; 4 underwriters
    Approval & offer0.2 d3 d95%Weekly committee for > 10k
    Disbursement0.1 d1 d99%Overnight payments
    analysisTotal PT = 0.2+0.5+0.1+0.5+0.2+0.1 = 1.6 d
Total W  = 0+2+1+6+3+1             = 13 d
Lead time = 14.6 d  (matches 14 d median)   Flow efficiency = 1.6/14.6 = 11%
Rolled %C&A = .70 x .75 x .95 x .85 x .95 x .99 = 0.399 (40%)
Top constraints: Underwriting queue (6 d) and committee (3 d)
CoD estimate: 38% drop-out x 5,000 applications/month x margin 900 = 1.71M/month at risk
    ImprovementExpected gainEffortPriority
    Real-time document validation (OCR + checks)%C&A up, −1.5 d waitMedium1
    Risk-based auto-decision for low-risk (< 10k) loans−6 d on 55% of casesMedium1
    Replace weekly committee with delegated authority + daily slots−2.5 d for high-valueLow2
    Real-time credit scoring−1 dLow3
    ✅
    BA deliverables: current/future maps, benefit case (ROI + CoD), prioritised improvement backlog with hypotheses, and AC/Gherkin for decision rules (eligibility, thresholds).

    🏁 Expert checklist

    • Map real work, not policy; use sampled tickets and percentiles.
    • Show lead time, flow efficiency and rolled %C&A together.
    • Find the constraint (TOC) before proposing fixes.
    • Quantify with ROI/NPV and Cost of Delay; show assumptions and ranges.
    • Prefer experiments with leading indicators; plan benefit realisation.
    • Re-map after each major change — the constraint moves.
