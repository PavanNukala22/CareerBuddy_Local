Why classification, and not just a component library Foundation

Most design systems do not fail because someone drew the button badly. They fail because two people looked at the same button and disagreed about what it meant — and nothing written down could settle it. Component libraries answer "what does it look like". Classification answers the harder questions: what is this for, when is it allowed, what would make it wrong, and what breaks if you change it.

A system needs three artefacts, and most teams only build the first two:

Fig 1 — The three artefacts of a design system. This document is the third, and it is the one most teams skip.

The four-level hierarchy Foundation

Every entry in this document sits at one of four depths. Getting the depth right is most of the skill — put a value too high and it becomes unfindable, too low and the document becomes an unreadable list.

The most common structural mistake. Writing "Colors: #ff385c, #222222, #f7f7f7, #dddddd" as a flat list. That is a palette, not a classification — it tells you the values exist but not which one to reach for. Every leaf value needs a role attached, and a role is a sentence about where it is allowed and where it is forbidden.

Descriptive vs prescriptive documentation Intermediate

There are two reasons to write a document like this, and they produce different content. Know which one you are doing before you start.

  • Descriptive — you are reverse-engineering an existing product (yours or a competitor's) to understand what it actually does. Language is observational: "buttons are 8px radius; the search pill is 32px." You are allowed to record inconsistencies, because they are facts.
  • Prescriptive — you are defining what the product should do going forward. Language is normative: "buttons use radius.md (8px); the search pill is the single exception at radius.pill." Inconsistencies are now bugs.

Most real work starts descriptive and ends prescriptive. The transition point is where the value comes from: the moment you turn "there are six greys in the codebase" into "there are five named greys, and the sixth is a defect", the document stops being an audit and starts being a standard.

Visual Theme
& Atmosphere

This section is prose, and people skip it because prose feels soft next to hex codes. That is a mistake. Atmosphere is the layer that decides which correct answer you pick when several are technically correct — and without it a team produces work that passes every rule and still looks like four different products stitched together.

What an atmosphere statement actually does Foundation

A good atmosphere paragraph is a constraint generator. You should be able to hold a design decision up against it and get a verdict. "Warm, editorial, photography-first" immediately rules out: dense data tables as a hero, hard drop shadows, competing accent colours, and any layout where the image is a thumbnail. That is the test — if your paragraph rules nothing out, it is marketing copy, not documentation.

Constrains — usable
  • "Quiet confidence — a travel magazine that happens to be an app. Photography leads; the interface gets out of the way."
  • "Institutional and monumental. Type does the shouting; colour is held in reserve for one red band per page."
  • "Dense and technical. The user is an expert. Information per square inch is a virtue, not a flaw."
Adjective soup — useless
  • "Modern, clean, intuitive, user-friendly." (Names no trade-off. Every system claims this.)
  • "Bold yet minimal, playful yet professional." (Contradictory; permits anything.)
  • "Inspired by nature and human connection." (Poetic; produces no decision.)

The three paragraphs, and what each is for Intermediate

  • Opening paragraph — the mood. What does this system feel like, and at what pace does it move? Written for a designer who has never seen the product.
  • Brand-defining moves — the fingerprint. The 2–3 choices you would still recognise in a screenshot with the logo cropped out. If you cannot name them, you do not yet have a system; you have a stylesheet.
  • Newest surface — the growth edge. The most recently shipped area, what it inherited, and what it introduced. This paragraph is where you find out whether your system is still coherent or has quietly forked.

The logo-crop test Intermediate

Take a screenshot of your product. Crop out the logo and the product name. Show it to someone who knows your industry. If they cannot tell it apart from two competitors, your signature moves are not signature — they are defaults. This is the fastest diagnostic in the whole document, and it takes ninety seconds.

Worked example — three systems, same test read-only reference

A · Marketplace, photography-led. Mood: quiet confidence; a travel magazine that happens to be an app. Signature moves: (1) a single coral accent used only on primary CTAs and the active-tab underline; (2) full-bleed 4:3 photography at 14–20px rounding as the primary visual vocabulary; (3) circular icon buttons at 50% radius with a white separator ring when they sit over a photograph. What it rules out: second accent hues, text overlaid on images, drop shadows on content cards.

B · Telecom, institutional. Mood: monumental and civic; type does the shouting. Signature moves: (1) a full-width red divider band between every hero and the content below; (2) display type at 144px uppercase with 0.79 line-height against calm 16px body; (3) no italics anywhere — emphasis is weight 700, never slant. What it rules out: decorative gradients, photographic heroes without the band, italic emphasis.

C · Developer tool, technical. Mood: dense and expert; the interface assumes you know what you are doing. Signature moves: (1) monospace for every value, never for prose; (2) 4px base grid with deliberately tight 4–8px internal row gaps; (3) colour reserved almost entirely for semantic state — the interface is otherwise near-monochrome. What it rules out: generous marketing whitespace, illustrative empty states, brand colour as decoration.

Notice that each example's value is in the last sentence. A signature move you cannot invert into a prohibition is not yet specific enough.

Advanced — atmosphere as a dispute-resolution tool. Advanced The real return on this section arrives eighteen months later, in a design review where two senior people disagree and neither can win on evidence. A written atmosphere statement converts "I don't like it" into "this violates our stated move — we said photography leads and the interface recedes, and this hero puts a gradient scrim and three chips over the image." Write it while everyone still agrees; you are creating a tiebreaker for a future argument you cannot yet predict.

Color Palette
& Roles

The single most important idea in this section: a design system does not have colours, it has roles that colours are assigned to. The moment you write down "our blue is #2f80ed" instead of "our primary action colour is #2f80ed", you have made theming, dark mode and rebranding into refactors instead of configuration changes.

Role-based naming Foundation

Name by job, not by appearance. When a designer renames "brand-blue" to purple next quarter, every reference to --brand-blue becomes a lie, but --color-action-primary stays true forever.

Appearance-named — breaks
  • --blue, --light-blue, --blue-2
  • --red-button (couples colour to one component)
  • --grey-that-alex-picked
  • In dark mode, --white-bg is no longer white
Role-named — survives
  • --color-action-primary
  • --color-feedback-danger
  • --color-surface-canvas / -raised / -sunken
  • --color-text-primary / -secondary / -disabled

The three-tier token model Intermediate

Flat token lists are the reason most teams cannot ship dark mode without a month of work. Three tiers fix it, and the middle tier is where the value is.

Fig 2 — Three-tier tokens. Components reference tier 2 only; a theme is a redefinition of tier 2 against different primitives.
The same structure in CSS
/* Tier 1 — primitives. A closed set. Nothing outside a component may use these. */ --coral-500: #ff385c; --coral-700: #e00b41; --grey-900: #222222; --grey-600: #6a6a6a; --grey-300: #dddddd; --grey-050: #f7f7f7; /* Tier 2 — semantic. This is the theming surface. */ --color-action-primary: var(--coral-500); --color-action-pressed: var(--coral-700); --color-text-primary: var(--grey-900); --color-text-secondary: var(--grey-600); --color-border-hairline: var(--grey-300); --color-surface-canvas: #ffffff; --color-surface-sunken: var(--grey-050); /* A whole theme is now this — nine lines, zero component edits. */ [data-theme="dark"] { --color-text-primary: #ebebeb; --color-surface-canvas: #141414; --color-surface-sunken: #1e1e1e; /* note: LIGHTER, not darker */ }
Why tier 3 should stay small. Every component token is a permanent exception you now have to maintain. Add one only when a component genuinely diverges for a reason you can state — for example, an input focus ring that must differ from the general focus ring because it sits inside a coloured container. If you cannot state the reason, use tier 2.

Building a ramp, not picking swatches Intermediate

A hue needs a ladder, typically 50 (lightest) through 900 (darkest), because you will need tints for backgrounds, mid tones for borders, and dark tones for text-on-tint. Picking three greys by eye guarantees you run out at the worst moment.

  • Keep hue drift deliberate. Naive interpolation in sRGB makes mid-tones muddy — blues turn purple, oranges turn brown. Building the ramp in a perceptual space (OKLCH or LCH) keeps lightness steps even and hue stable.
  • Lightness steps should be perceptually even, not numerically even. In OKLCH you can literally step L by a fixed amount; in hex you cannot.
  • Every ramp needs at least one accessible text pair. For a coloured background at step 100, the matching text is usually step 700–900 — verify it, don't assume it.
Perceptual ramp — even lightness, stable hue
/* OKLCH: L (lightness 0–1) · C (chroma) · H (hue angle) */ --coral-050: oklch(0.97 0.02 15); /* tint background */ --coral-100: oklch(0.93 0.05 15); /* hover tint */ --coral-300: oklch(0.80 0.13 15); /* borders, disabled */ --coral-500: oklch(0.65 0.22 15); /* the brand colour */ --coral-700: oklch(0.52 0.20 15); /* pressed / active */ --coral-900: oklch(0.35 0.14 15); /* text on coral tint */ /* Hue (15) never moves. Lightness steps are visually even. The same six lines in hex would require hand-tuning each step. */

Accent scarcity — the arithmetic Intermediate

An accent colour works by being rare. Attention is zero-sum: if three things on a screen are coral, none of them is the primary action any more. The practical rule most mature systems converge on is one accent element per viewport, occupying single-digit percentages of the visible pixels.

This is why the "roles it owns" field matters more than the hex value. Write the allowed list, then write the forbidden list — the forbidden list is what actually holds the line in review.

Semantic colour: state, never decoration Foundation

Error, success, warning and info are the only four that most products need. The rule that keeps them meaningful: if a colour communicates state, it may not also be used decoratively. A green that means "success" cannot also be the marketing brand green, because then a green badge is ambiguous.

Two obligations attach to every semantic colour, and both are routinely missed:

  • Never colour-alone. Roughly 1 in 12 men has a colour-vision deficiency. Every semantic signal needs a second channel — an icon, a word, or a position. A red border with no message is not an error state, it is a decoration that some users cannot see.
  • Contrast on both surfaces. Your error red must pass 4.5:1 as text on white and read correctly as a 3:1 border. These are usually two different values from the same ramp, and a single hex almost never satisfies both.

The disabled-state trap. WCAG exempts disabled controls from contrast requirements, so an unreadable grey button passes every automated audit. It is still a usability failure — the user cannot tell whether the control is broken or merely unavailable. Prefer keeping the control enabled and explaining the blocker on activation; if you must disable it, put the reason in visible text, not in a hover tooltip.

Dark mode is a second design, not an inversion Advanced

  • Elevation inverts. On light backgrounds, higher elements cast darker shadows. On dark backgrounds shadows barely register, so higher surfaces get lighter. Your elevation tokens must therefore be paired with surface tokens, not defined independently.
  • Pure white on pure black is wrong. Maximum contrast causes halation — light bleeding visually into the dark ground — which many readers experience as blur, and which is harder for people with astigmatism. Off-white (#ebebeb) on near-black (#141414) is the standard compromise.
  • Saturated accents glow. A coral that reads as confident on white appears to vibrate on near-black. Reduce chroma by roughly 15–25% in the dark theme; in OKLCH this is a one-number change per token.
  • Semantic hues shift meaning. A dark red on a dark ground can read as brown. Semantic colours usually need a distinct dark-theme value, not a tinted version of the light one.

Gradients: document the prohibition Intermediate

Most systems should record "gradients: none" — and that is a valuable entry, not an empty one. An explicit "none" prevents a future contributor from adding one and calling it consistent. If you do have a gradient, three fields are mandatory: the exact CSS string, where it is allowed, and where it is forbidden. A gradient without a forbidden list will end up as a page background within a year.

Advanced — perceptual colour spaces and wide-gamut display. Advanced Hand-picked hex ramps drift in perceived lightness because sRGB hex is not perceptually uniform — two steps that look evenly spaced on a colour wheel can differ wildly in contrast against the same background. Generating ramps in OKLCH (or OKLab) lets you hold lightness (L) and chroma (C) constant while sweeping hue, so every step-500 across every hue family hits the same WCAG contrast against your surface tokens automatically, instead of by manual retesting. On P3-capable displays, author the same role in both srgb and display-p3 via CSS color() or a @media (color-gamut: p3) override, and treat the P3 value as an enhancement, never the source of truth — a system that only ships P3 breaks predictably on older hardware and print exports.
03

Typography
Rules

Typography carries more of a brand's character than colour does, and it is where undocumented systems decay fastest — because a designer can invent a new size in two seconds and nobody notices for six months. The goal of this section is a closed set: a finite list of roles, each with a complete spec, and no legal way to use anything else.

Build a scale, never pick sizes Foundation

Choose a base (16px is the web default and the accessibility floor for body text) and a ratio, then generate every step. Common ratios: 1.2 (minor third — calm, good for dense UI), 1.25 (major third — the safe default), 1.333 (perfect fourth — editorial, more dramatic).

The reason to generate rather than choose is not aesthetics, it is enforcement. "18px isn't on the scale" is a fact anyone can check. "18px feels slightly off" is an opinion you will lose.

Tracking follows size, inversely. Type is drawn to look correct at a text size. Blown up to 48px the letters look loose, so you tighten (negative tracking). Shrunk to 11px they look cramped, so you open up (positive tracking). A system that applies 0 tracking everywhere will have display type that looks unresolved and micro-type that looks clogged. This is one of the clearest tells of an undocumented type system.

Weight strategy Intermediate

Decide the body weight explicitly and state it, because it is a genuine brand fork. Some systems set body at 400 (classic, quieter, more text-like); others at 500 (denser, more confident, common in modern product UI). Neither is wrong; the failure is having both in the same product because nobody wrote it down.

Then define what emphasis means. If body is 500, then bold has to be 700 to read as a step; if body is 400, 600 is enough. And decide whether italics exist at all — a number of institutional systems ban italic emphasis outright and use weight instead, which is a legitimate and enforceable rule.

Measure, and why ch beats pixels Foundation

Line length ("measure") should sit between 45 and 75 characters for continuous reading. Past roughly 90, the eye loses its place on the return sweep and re-reads lines. Set it in ch units so it adapts to the font actually rendering, rather than to a pixel guess made in one typeface.

A scale that responds without media queries
/* Fluid display: 28px floor, 44px ceiling, scales with viewport between. */ h1 { font-size: clamp(1.75rem, 1.2rem + 2.2vw, 2.75rem); line-height: 1.15; } /* Measure controlled by the font, not by a pixel guess. */ p { max-width: 65ch; line-height: 1.6; } /* Fallback metric matching — prevents layout shift while the webfont loads. Without size-adjust, the fallback renders at a different height and the page jumps when the real font arrives. This is a CLS problem caused by type. */ @font-face { font-family: "Brand Sans Fallback"; src: local("Arial"); size-adjust: 107%; ascent-override: 92%; }
Worked example — two opposite type systems read-only reference

A · Product-first (marketplace). One family, six weights, no second typeface. Body is 16px at weight 500 — the system's "regular" is heavier than the web default, which gives every paragraph a slightly confident texture. Negative tracking applies only at 20px and above. All-caps appears exactly once, at an 8px superscript for price footnotes. No italics for emphasis anywhere. Effect: extremely uniform, quietly branded, very hard to get wrong.

B · Institutional (telecom). Display type at 144px, weight 800, uppercase, line-height 0.79, tracking −1px — the headline is the brand asset. Body then drops to a deliberately calm 16px/400. There is no mid-scale: nothing lives between 48px and 20px, and that gap is intentional so the display never has competition. Effect: dramatic, editorial, and it fails badly if someone adds a 32px heading "to bridge the gap".

What to copy from both: each system states the thing it will not do — no italics; no mid-scale. A type section without prohibitions is a suggestion.

Fallback stacks and substitution Intermediate

If your brand face is proprietary, the fallback stack is part of the system, not an afterthought — most external partners and every AI-generated mock will render the fallback. Document three things: the closest commercial substitute, the closest free substitute (usually a Google Font), and the fidelity caveat that tells someone what will look wrong.

  • Match metrics, not vibes. Two faces at the same nominal size can differ by 10% in x-height, which changes perceived size and line height. size-adjust in @font-face corrects this and prevents the font-swap layout jump.
  • Always end with a generic. sans-serif as the last entry, always. And put system-ui before it if platform-native rendering is acceptable.
  • Variable fonts change the accounting. One variable file can replace six static weights, which usually reduces total bytes and lets you use intermediate weights — but only document weights you actually permit, or the closed set reopens.

Component
Stylings

A component entry that lists only the resting appearance is worth very little — the resting state is the easy 20%. The value is in states, variants, content rules and accessibility, because those are what an engineer has to guess at otherwise, under deadline, differently each time.

The state matrix is where systems are won or lost. Seven states × four variants × three sizes is 84 combinations, and you obviously do not draw 84 artboards. You define the state rules once — "hover darkens the background one ramp step; focus-visible adds a 2px ring at --color-focus with 2px offset; disabled drops to --color-surface-sunken with --color-text-disabled" — and then every variant inherits. Worked example — a complete primary button spec read-only reference

Anatomy: container → [optional leading icon 16px] → label → [optional trailing icon 16px]. Gap between slots: space-2 (8px).

Variants: primary (filled accent), secondary (1px hairline border, canvas fill), tertiary (text only, no border), danger (filled error). Exactly four. A fifth requires an RFC.

Sizes: sm 32px tall / 8×14px padding / 14px label · md 44px / 12×20px / 16px label · lg 52px / 16×24px / 16px label. Minimum target on touch is md.

States: default --color-action-primary · hover one ramp step darker · focus-visible 2px ring --color-focus at 2px offset, ring never removed · active scale(0.97), 100ms · disabled sunken surface + disabled text, cursor not-allowed, reason stated in adjacent text · loading label stays in place, spinner replaces leading icon, width does not change, control is aria-busy and non-submitting.

Content: 1–3 words, sentence case, verb-first ("Place order", not "Submit"). Max 24 characters before the size must increase — no truncation on buttons, ever. Icon-only permitted at sm and md only, and then an accessible name is mandatory.

Accessibility: native <button>; name from visible label, or aria-label when icon-only; Enter and Space both activate; ≥44×44px touch target including invisible padding; never outline: none without a replacement of at least 3:1.

Don't: two primary buttons in one view · a primary button for a destructive action (use danger) · a disabled primary with no explanation · a button that is really a link (use an anchor so it opens in a new tab correctly).

Variant explosion, and how to stop it Advanced

Every design system trends toward more variants, because every request looks reasonable in isolation. Three controls actually work:

  • A closed variant matrix, published. Not "here are the buttons we have" but "here are the buttons that exist; anything else is a defect".
  • Evidence threshold for additions. A new variant requires three independent teams having needed it. One team's need is a one-off; three is a gap in the system.
  • Documented escape hatches. A small, named set of override slots is far healthier than either total rigidity — which drives teams to fork the component — or unrestricted className injection, which means the system no longer describes what ships.

Watch for the tell: when engineers start building parallel components, the system is not being ignored, it is failing to meet a real requirement. Treat a fork as a bug report about the system, not as a discipline problem.

Every distinctive product has two or three components that are not generic — an award lockup, a red divider band, a tri-tab category picker, a sticky booking panel. These deserve the most detailed entries in the whole document, because they are the parts an outsider will reproduce badly and the parts your users recognise instantly. Specify them to the pixel, and state where they appear so nobody scatters them.

Layout
Principles

Spacing is the least glamorous section and the one that most determines whether a product feels professionally made. The reason is perceptual, not aesthetic: distance encodes relationship. Two elements close together are read as one thing before the viewer has consciously registered either of them.

The proximity law that governs everything here Foundation

Space within a group must be visibly tighter than space between groups. If it is not, the eye groups the wrong things, and no amount of correct colour or type will fix it. This single rule explains most "it looks a bit off but I can't say why" feedback.

Fig 3 — Inner spacing must be visibly tighter than outer spacing. A ratio of roughly 1:3 or 1:4 is safe; anything under 1:2 is ambiguous.

Base unit: 4px or 8px? Intermediate

The honest answer is both, in a defined division of labour. An 8px rhythm carries page-level spacing — section padding, gutters, gaps between components. A 4px step exists for the tight internal spacing that 8px is too coarse for: icon-to-label, badge padding, the gap between a label and its input.

Declaring "8px grid" and then using 4, 6, 12 and 20px in practice is worse than declaring a 4px base honestly, because the stated rule no longer describes the system and people stop trusting the document.

Advanced — optical vs mathematical spacing. Advanced A mathematically equal gap is not always an optically equal one. A circular avatar next to square text needs slightly more space than two squares, because the circle's visual mass recedes at its edges. Capital letters, punctuation and icons all sit differently inside their bounding boxes. The system rule should be "use the scale", with a named exemption: "optical adjustments up to ±2px are permitted inside a component and must be committed to the component, never applied ad hoc in a layout."

Radius as a semantic scale Intermediate

Radius is not decoration — it is a category signal. Users learn, without being told, that in your product a 50% radius means "this is a control you press" and a 14px radius means "this is content". A radius scale is only useful if each step owns a class of surfaces.

  • 2–4px — utility and form chrome; reads as functional and sharp
  • 6–8px — standard cards, dropdowns, text buttons
  • 12–16px — content surfaces, photography, generous containers
  • 20–32px — pill buttons, search bars, large hero containers
  • 50% / 999px — circular controls and avatars only

One detail that separates careful work: nested radii must be adjusted. A 12px-radius card containing a 12px-radius image at 8px padding will look wrong, because the inner curve should be outer radius minus padding — here 4px. Getting this right is invisible; getting it wrong reads as sloppy without anyone identifying why.

Grid, container and density Intermediate

Two numbers matter more than the column count: the maximum content width (past which line lengths become unreadable and the eye has to travel too far) and the minimum comfortable card width (which determines how many columns are actually possible at each breakpoint). Derive your columns from those, not from a fixed 12-column habit.

If your product has density modes — a comfortable default and a compact mode for power users — document what changes. Usually it is only vertical padding and row height; type size and touch targets should not shrink, or compact mode becomes an accessibility failure.

Layout primitives — reusable, intrinsic, no magic numbers
/* A "stack" — consistent vertical rhythm without margin collapse bugs. */ .stack > * + * { margin-block-start: var(--space-4); } /* A "cluster" — wrapping row of items with even gaps. */ .cluster { display: flex; flex-wrap: wrap; gap: var(--space-2); } /* An intrinsic grid — columns are a consequence of available space, not of a breakpoint you had to guess. */ .grid { display: grid; gap: var(--space-6); grid-template-columns: repeat(auto-fill, minmax(280px, 1fr)); } /* Nested radius: inner = outer − padding. */ .card { border-radius: 12px; padding: 8px; } .card img { border-radius: 4px; } /* not 12px */

Depth &
Elevation

Elevation is a semantic ladder, not a decorative effect. Each level should mean something specific and consistent: this is the page, this is attached to the page, this floats above it, this is modal and blocks everything below. When elevation is applied by feel, users lose the ability to predict what is dismissible and what is not.

Layered shadows and why single shadows look cheap Intermediate

Real shadows have two components: a tight, darker contact shadow immediately under the object, and a soft, wide ambient shadow further out. A single 0 4px 12px rgba(0,0,0,.15) tries to be both and reads as a grey smudge. Stacking two or three shadows at different offsets and opacities is what produces the subtle, expensive look — and it costs nothing extra to render.

Fig 4 — One blur trying to do two jobs, versus a contact shadow plus an ambient shadow
An elevation ladder with meaning attached
/* Each level owns a category of surface. Do not add a level without a category. */ --elev-0: none; /* ↳ page content, listing cards. Separation comes from whitespace. */ --elev-1: 0 1px 2px rgba(14,12,10,.06), 0 1px 3px rgba(14,12,10,.10); /* ↳ attached to the page: pressed icon buttons, hovered rows. */ --elev-2: 0 0 0 1px rgba(14,12,10,.02), 0 2px 6px rgba(14,12,10,.04), 0 4px 8px rgba(14,12,10,.10); /* ↳ floating above: dropdowns, sticky panels, popovers. */ --elev-3: 0 4px 8px rgba(14,12,10,.07), 0 12px 24px rgba(14,12,10,.12); /* ↳ modal layer: dialogs, sheets. Always paired with a scrim. */ --ring-focus: 0 0 0 2px var(--color-focus); /* not elevation */ --ring-separator: 0 0 0 4px #ffffff; /* controls over photos */
Focus rings are not elevation. They live in the same CSS property, which is why they get muddled, but they answer a different question — "where is the keyboard" rather than "how high is this". Keep them in a separate token group so that a change to card elevation can never accidentally weaken a focus indicator, and so the ring can meet its own 3:1 contrast requirement independently.

Depth without shadow Intermediate

Shadow is one of four ways to signal layering, and often not the best. Document which your system uses, because a system that mixes all four inconsistently looks unresolved.

  • Shadow — literal depth. Reads well on light grounds, poorly on dark.
  • Surface lightness — a lighter panel reads as nearer. This is the primary elevation cue in dark themes.
  • Border / hairline — flat separation with no implied height. Quiet, precise, very robust in both themes.
  • Scrim / backdrop blur — pushes everything behind it away. Reserve for modal layers; overuse destroys the meaning.

Dark-mode elevation inverts Advanced

This is the detail most teams discover in production. On light backgrounds, elevation adds shadow. On dark backgrounds shadow is nearly invisible, so elevation must add light — each level raises the surface lightness a step. Practically this means your elevation tokens cannot be theme-independent: --elev-2 in dark mode is a surface colour change plus a much subtler shadow, not the same shadow string.

Performance and the z-index contract Advanced

  • Large blur radii are expensive, particularly when animated or applied to many elements in a scrolling list. Prefer smaller blurs with layered opacity over one 40px blur.
  • Never animate box-shadow directly on hover for list items; animate the opacity of a pseudo-element carrying the shadow instead, which stays on the compositor.
  • Publish a z-index scale as named tokens — base 0, dropdown 100, sticky 200, overlay 300, modal 400, toast 500. Ad-hoc z-index: 9999 is a symptom of a missing contract, and it is why the cookie banner ends up above the modal.
  • Elevation and focus must not conflict. WCAG 2.2 (2.4.11) requires the focused element not be obscured by sticky chrome — an elevation decision with an accessibility consequence.

Advanced A numeric z-index scale (10, 20, 30…) only works within a single stacking context; the moment a component creates its own context — via transform, filter, will-change, or an ancestor with isolation: isolate — its children's z-index values become meaningless relative to siblings outside that context. Rules that carry an exception Intermediate

Real systems have exceptions. The failure is leaving them undocumented, because an undocumented exception looks identical to a violation, and reviewers learn to stop enforcing anything. Write the exception into the rule with its own scope:

  • "No all-caps. Exception: the 8px price-footnote superscript, which is the single uppercase role in the system."

One named exception is a system. Five unnamed exceptions is not.

Responsive
Behavior

Breakpoints should come from your content, not from a list of device widths. Device widths change every year; the width at which your product card stops being readable does not. Resize the browser slowly and note where the layout actually breaks — those are your breakpoints, and they will rarely land exactly on 768 or 1024.

Mobile-first is a code strategy, not a slogan Foundation

Write the small layout as your base styles, then add complexity upward with min-width queries. This produces less CSS and far fewer overrides than starting wide and stripping things away — and it means the smallest, most constrained experience gets designed rather than derived.

Container queries change the unit of responsiveness Advanced

Media queries ask "how wide is the viewport?" — but a card does not care about the viewport, it cares how much room it has. The same card in a wide main column and in a narrow sidebar needs different layouts at the same viewport width. Container queries let a component respond to its own container, which is the correct model for a design system: components become genuinely portable instead of carrying assumptions about where they will be placed.

Component-level responsiveness
/* The component adapts to ITS space, not the window's. */ .card-wrap { container-type: inline-size; } .card { display: grid; gap: var(--space-3); } @container (min-width: 420px) { .card { grid-template-columns: 140px 1fr; } /* image beside text */ } /* Now the same card is correct in a 1200px main column AND in a 320px sidebar, with no viewport breakpoints and no variant prop. */

Touch targets and the two numbers that matter Foundation

  • 44×44pt (Apple) / 48×48dp (Material) — the usability target. This is what you design to.
  • 24×24 CSS px (WCAG 2.2, SC 2.5.8 AA) — the legal floor, with a spacing alternative. This is what you must not go below.
  • Hit area ≠ visual size. A 20px icon can carry invisible padding out to 44px. Do that rather than making every icon visually enormous.
  • Spacing between targets counts. Two adjacent 48px buttons with a 0px gap still produce mis-taps; keep at least space-2 (8px) between interactive elements, and more when one of them is destructive.

Every multi-part component needs a documented answer to "what happens when there isn't room". Leaving this to implementation is how you end up with four different mobile navigation patterns in one product.

Agent
Prompt Guide

This section is newer than the rest of the discipline, and it is quickly becoming the most valuable. When an AI agent generates a screen, it has no access to your Figma library, your taste, or the conversation where you decided the accent must stay scarce. It has only the text you gave it. Documentation quality now converts directly into output quality, which is a genuinely new incentive.

Describing vs specifying Foundation

Human documentation can rely on shared context. Agent documentation cannot. The same rule has to be written twice, differently, for the two audiences.

Describing — a human can act on this
  • "Our buttons feel confident but not loud."
  • "Use the standard card elevation."
  • "Follow the spacing scale."
  • "Match the brand's editorial tone."
Specifying — an agent can act on this
  • "Background #ff385c, white label at 16px weight 500, padding 14px×24px, radius 8px, no shadow."
  • "box-shadow: 0 0 0 1px rgba(0,0,0,.02), 0 2px 6px rgba(0,0,0,.04), 0 4px 8px rgba(0,0,0,.1)"
  • "Spacing values permitted: 4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32, 48, 64px. No others."
  • "Sentence case, verb-first, 1–3 words, max 24 characters."

Anatomy of a prompt that produces the right screen Intermediate

Five parts, in this order. Skipping the last two is why generated output usually looks generic.

  1. What to build — the component and its role in one clause.
  2. Exact values inline — never a reference to "the primary colour". Say #ff385c and its token name.
  3. States — hover, focus, active, disabled, loading. Unstated states get invented.
  4. Prohibitions — "no gradient, no drop shadow, no second accent". Models default to embellishment; the prohibition is what removes it.
  5. The atmosphere sentence — one clause of feel, which resolves the hundred micro-decisions you did not specify.
Worked example — the same request, three quality levels read-only reference

Weak. "Make a nice product card for an e-commerce site." → Generic bootstrap-looking output, arbitrary shadow, random radius, a gradient nobody asked for.

Better. "Make a product card with a 4:3 image, title, price and rating. Use #ff385c for the price and 8px rounded corners." → Correct shape, but the accent has been misapplied to the price (it is reserved for actions), there are no states, and there is nothing stopping a drop shadow.

Strong. "Build a product card. Container: white, no shadow, separation from whitespace only. Image: 4:3, object-fit: cover, 14px radius, always with width and height declared. Below the image, stacked at 4px: title 16px/600 #222222 clamped to 2 lines; subtitle 14px/500 #6a6a6a, 1 line; price 16px/600 #222222 with a 12px/400 #6a6a6a 'per night' suffix; rating row 14px/400. Whole card is a single link with one accessible name. States: hover raises nothing and only underlines the title; focus-visible shows a 2px #222222 ring at 2px offset. Do not use the accent colour #ff385c anywhere in this component — it is reserved for primary actions. No gradients, no drop shadow, no overlaid text on the image. Overall feel: editorial and photography-led; the interface recedes."

The strong version is longer, and that is the point — every extra sentence removes a decision the model would otherwise have made for you. Note especially the explicit negative instruction about the accent: stating where a colour must not appear is often more effective than stating where it should.

Machine-readable exports Advanced

Prose is for judgement; structured data is for values. Mature systems ship both from one source: tokens in a standard format (the W3C Design Tokens Community Group format is the emerging convention) that can be transformed into CSS variables, iOS and Android resources, and a JSON blob you paste into a system prompt. The Export JSON button in this document's footer produces exactly that shape for whatever you have filled in.

The rule of thumb: if a value can be enumerated, it belongs in the data export; if it requires judgement, it belongs in the prose. Colours, spacing steps and type roles are data. "The accent must feel scarce" is prose, and no export format will ever carry it.

Iteration protocol Intermediate

  • One component at a time. Asking for a whole page produces output that is 70% right everywhere and correctable nowhere.
  • Name tokens, not appearances. "Ink Black #222222" beats "dark grey" — the second one drifts every regeneration.
  • Correct with rules, not with adjectives. "Increase the gap to 24px" is repeatable; "give it more breathing room" is not.
  • Add prohibitions when output drifts. If shadows keep appearing, the fix is a sentence saying no shadows — not another attempt at describing flatness.
  • Keep the atmosphere clause in every prompt. It is short and it does a disproportionate amount of work.

Known gaps are first-class content Advanced

Writing them down converts an unknown unknown into a known one — and it stops a reader from confidently generating something in an area where you have no standard at all.

A colour entry reads: #ff385c. What is the minimum that must be added before it counts as a classification entry rather than a palette swatch?
  • AIts RGB and HSL equivalents
  • BThe name of the designer who chose it
  • CA role — where it is allowed and where it is forbidden
  • DA tint and shade ramp
Answer: C. A value without a role tells you the colour exists but not when to reach for it, which is exactly the ambiguity classification is meant to remove. The forbidden list matters as much as the allowed list — it is what actually holds the line in design review. A ramp (D) is useful but secondary; format conversions (A) add nothing.
2
Which token name will still be correct after the brand changes its primary colour from blue to purple?
  • A--brand-blue
  • B--color-action-primary
  • C--blue-500
  • D--primary-blue-button
Answer: B. Role-based names describe the job, so they survive appearance changes. A, C and D all encode the current hue and become actively misleading after a rebrand — and D additionally couples a colour to one component. Note that --blue-500 is legitimate as a tier-1 primitive; it is only wrong when a component references it directly.
3
In the three-tier token model, at which tier do you apply a theme?
  • ATier 1 — redefine the primitives
  • BTier 3 — redefine each component token
  • CAll three tiers simultaneously
  • DTier 2 — redefine the semantic tokens
Answer: D. The semantic tier is the indirection layer that makes theming a data change. Components reference meaning, so redefining meaning re-themes everything at once. Redefining primitives (A) would work only if every theme used the same hues; touching tier 3 (B) means editing every component, which is the problem the model exists to prevent.
4
Why build a colour ramp in OKLCH rather than by hand-picking hex values?
  • ALightness steps come out perceptually even and the hue stays stable, so mid-tones don't go muddy
  • BOKLCH values are smaller to store
  • CHex cannot represent enough colours
  • DOKLCH guarantees WCAG compliance automatically
Answer: A. Interpolating in sRGB shifts hue and bunches lightness — blues drift purple, oranges drift brown. A perceptual space lets you step lightness by a fixed amount and keep the hue angle fixed. D is a common misconception: OKLCH makes contrast easier to reason about because L is perceptual, but you must still measure the ratio.
5
Your accent is used on the primary CTA, the active tab, section headings, three icons and a promotional banner. What is the problem?
  • ANone — consistent use of the brand colour is good
  • BThe accent will fail contrast in at least one of those uses
  • CAttention is zero-sum: with six accent elements on screen, none reads as the primary action any more
  • DSix uses exceeds the recommended token count
Answer: C. An accent works by scarcity. Once it is everywhere it stops being a signal and becomes a texture, and the user loses the ability to find the primary action by colour. B may also be true in practice but it is not the structural problem. The fix is the "roles it owns" plus "where forbidden" pair, enforced in review.
6
Which statement about semantic colours (error / success / warning / info) is correct?
  • AA single hex per state is sufficient
  • BEach state usually needs distinct text, border and tint values, and must always carry a second non-colour channel
  • CThey can double as brand accents when the palette is small
  • DWarning yellow only needs 3:1 because it is a warning
Answer: B. One hex almost never satisfies both 4.5:1 as text and 3:1 as a border on a tint, so a state needs several values from the same ramp. The second channel — an icon or the word itself — is required because colour alone excludes people with colour-vision deficiency. C destroys the meaning: a green that is both "success" and "brand" makes every green badge ambiguous.
7
In a dark theme, how should a raised surface differ from the page background?
  • AIt gets lighter, because shadows barely register on dark grounds
  • BIt gets darker, mirroring the light theme
  • CIt stays identical; only the border changes
  • DIt uses a stronger version of the same shadow token
Answer: A. Elevation inverts in dark mode: on light grounds height adds shadow, on dark grounds height adds light. This is why elevation tokens cannot be theme-independent — --elev-2 in dark mode is primarily a surface-lightness change with a much subtler shadow, not the same shadow string turned up.
8
Your system has no gradients. What should the gradient entry contain?
  • ANothing — delete the section
  • BA placeholder gradient for future use
  • CA note saying "TBD"
  • DAn explicit "none", which is itself a rule that prevents someone adding one later and calling it consistent
Answer: D. An explicit prohibition is one of the most valuable entries in a classification document. An empty or missing section reads as an unanswered question and invites a contributor to fill the gap; "none" closes it. "TBD" (C) is the worst option — it actively signals that adding one would be welcome.
9
Why generate a type scale from a base and a ratio rather than choosing sizes as you go?
  • AGenerated scales are more attractive
  • BIt makes the set closed and enforceable — "18px isn't on the scale" is a fact, not an opinion
  • CBrowsers render mathematical ratios more sharply
  • DIt reduces the number of font files needed
Answer: B. The point is enforcement, not aesthetics. A closed set turns a taste argument into a checkable statement, which is what stops a type system decaying into fourteen sizes over two years.
10
Why does display type usually need negative letter-spacing while 11px labels need positive?
  • AIt's a stylistic convention with no underlying reason
  • BNegative tracking saves horizontal space on headlines
  • CType is drawn to look correct at text sizes; scaled up the spacing looks loose, scaled down it looks cramped
  • DScreen rendering adds a pixel of spacing at large sizes
Answer: C. Letterforms and their sidebearings are optimised for a reading size. Enlarge them and the relative gaps appear to grow; shrink them and the counters and gaps close up. Uniform 0 tracking across a scale is one of the clearest signs of an undocumented type system.
11
What does size-adjust in an @font-face fallback declaration prevent?
  • ALayout shift when the web font loads and replaces a fallback with different metrics
  • BUsers overriding the font in their browser
  • CThe font from loading at the wrong weight
  • DLicensing violations when substituting a commercial face
Answer: A. Two faces at the same nominal size can differ substantially in x-height and line box, so text reflows the moment the real font arrives. size-adjust and the ascent/descent overrides match the fallback's metrics to the web font, which removes the jump. Worth knowing that this is a typography decision with a measurable performance consequence.
12
A component spec lists background, text colour, padding and radius. What is most likely missing that will cause real problems?
  • AThe hex values in RGB form
  • BA Figma link
  • CThe designer's rationale
  • DStates, content limits and accessibility — hover, focus-visible, disabled, loading, max length, accessible name
Answer: D. The resting appearance is the easy fraction of a spec. Unstated states get invented per screen under deadline, which is how focus rings vanish and every team builds a different loading treatment. Content limits are what stop the component breaking on real data and on translated strings.

Seven states × four variants × three sizes is 84 combinations. How should a spec handle that?
  • ADraw all 84 artboards for completeness
  • BDefine the state rules once so every variant and size derives from them
  • CDocument only the primary variant and let others follow by analogy
  • DReduce to one size and one variant
Answer: B. State behaviour should be expressed as rules — "hover darkens one ramp step; focus-visible adds a 2px ring at 2px offset; disabled uses sunken surface plus disabled text" — which every variant inherits. If your rules cannot generate all 84 combinations, the spec is incomplete. C is how variants silently diverge.

Engineers on three teams have each built their own table component instead of using the system's. What is the correct first interpretation?
  • AA bug report about the system — the component probably doesn't meet a real, repeated requirement
  • BA discipline problem to be resolved in code review
  • CEvidence the design system should be deprecated
  • DNormal and not worth investigating
Answer: A. One fork is a one-off; three independent forks is a gap. Repeated divergence almost always means the API is too rigid, the component is undiscoverable, or the contribution path is slower than rebuilding. Treating it as indiscipline (B) makes teams hide their forks rather than report the gap.

In a form, the gap between a label and its input is 16px, and between one field and the next label is 20px. What happens?
  • ANothing — the for/id association is what matters
  • BScreen readers will announce the wrong label
  • CSighted users will bind labels to the wrong fields, because the gaps are too close to equal to disambiguate
  • DThe form will fail WCAG automatically
Answer: C. Proximity is read pre-consciously and overrides your intent. Inner spacing must be visibly tighter than outer — roughly 1:3 or 1:4. B is wrong, and that is exactly why this bug survives automated testing: the markup is correct, so only sighted users are affected.

A card has a 12px radius and 8px padding. What radius should the image inside it have?
  • A12px, to match the card
  • B0 — inner elements should be square
  • C20px — outer plus padding
  • D4px — outer minus padding
Answer: D. Concentric curves need inner = outer − padding to stay visually parallel. Matching the outer radius (A) makes the inner curve look too round and the gap uneven at the corners. This is invisible when correct and reads as sloppiness when wrong, without most people being able to name why.

Why do three stacked low-opacity shadows look better than one 0 4px 12px rgba(0,0,0,.18)?
  • AThree shadows render faster
  • BReal shadows have a tight contact component and a soft ambient one; a single blur tries to be both and reads as a grey smudge
  • CBrowsers cap single shadows at low opacity
  • DIt is required for WCAG compliance
Answer: B. Layering separates the two jobs: a small tight shadow defines the crisp perimeter, a wide soft one gives ambient falloff. Rendering cost is essentially the same, which is why this is one of the cheapest available quality upgrades in a design system.

Why should focus-ring tokens be kept separate from elevation tokens?

  • DThere is no reason; they belong together
Answer: A. They share a CSS property, which is why they get muddled, but "how high is this" and "where is the keyboard" are unrelated questions. Coupling them means a purely visual elevation tweak can silently break an accessibility requirement.
