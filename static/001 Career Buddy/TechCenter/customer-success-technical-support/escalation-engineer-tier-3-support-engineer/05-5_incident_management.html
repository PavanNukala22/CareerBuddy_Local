    Module 02 · Lifecycle

    The Incident Lifecycle: From Alert to Review

    Every Sev-1 follows the same six phases. Knowing them lets you stay calm and always know what the next step is.

    🔄 The Six Phases

    An incident is an unplanned event that disrupts or degrades a service. Handling it is a process, not heroics: detect, declare, mobilise, mitigate, resolve, review. Each phase has a goal and a clear output, so the team is never guessing what comes next.

    🧴
    Analogy: a fire brigade. First the alarm (detect), then the chief takes charge (declare), crews arrive and take positions (mobilise), the fire is contained (mitigate), the building is declared safe (resolve), and investigators learn how it started (review).
    PhaseGoalOutput
    DetectNotice the problemAlert or customer report with a timestamp
    DeclareMake it official and set severityIncident ID, Incident Commander named
    MobiliseAssemble people and a channelWar room open, roles assigned
    MitigateStop the customer impact, even if the cause is unknownService recovering
    ResolveConfirm full recovery and monitorMetrics normal, all-clear message
    ReviewLearn and preventRCA, post-mortem, tracked action items

    📣 When to Declare an Incident

    declare if ANY is true[ ] Multiple customers affected, or one critical customer fully blocked
[ ] A core function is down or severely degraded (login, checkout, API)
[ ] Possible data loss, corruption or security exposure
[ ] Error budget burning at more than 10x normal rate
[ ] You cannot tell yet how bad it is but it could be bad
    💡
    When in doubt, declare. Opening an incident that turns out minor costs a few minutes; delaying a real one costs customers, trust and money. You can always downgrade the severity later.

    ⏱️ Anatomy of a Typical Sev-1

    timeline (UTC)14:02  Release 4.2.1 reaches 100% traffic; checkout errors begin (impact start)
14:05  Alert: checkout 5xx above 5% for 3 min
14:08  On-call acknowledges the page
14:12  Declared Sev-1; Incident Commander assigned
14:15  War room open; Comms and Scribe named
14:25  Hypothesis: release 4.2.1 (errors started within 3 min of deploy)
14:32  Decision: roll back 4.2.1
14:41  Error rate back under 0.5%
15:10  Monitoring stable; resolved

Time to detect   = 14:05 - 14:02 = 3 min
Time to mitigate = 14:41 - 14:02 = 39 min

    Read the timeline for decisions: the team formed a hypothesis from what changed, chose the lowest-risk mitigation (rollback) before understanding the root cause, then monitored before closing. Those measurements feed MTTD and MTTR trends.

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.Why declare a Sev-1 early even when you are not sure of the scale?
    Declaring mobilises people and structure immediately. A false alarm costs minutes, while delay lets the impact grow. Severity can be lowered once the facts are known.
    Q1.Which phase focuses on stopping customer impact even if the cause is unknown?
    • A Detect
    • B Mitigate
    • C Review
    • D Declare

    Module 03 · War Room

    Running the War Room

    How to set up, staff and run a Sev-1 call so the team acts as one calm unit instead of twenty people talking at once.

    🏗️ Opening the War Room

    1. Create one dedicated channel using a standard name, for example #inc-2026-10-06-checkout.
    2. Start a video bridge and pin the link in the channel.
    3. Name the Incident Commander (IC), Tech Lead, Comms Lead and Scribe.
    4. Post the opening message below.
    5. Invite only the people needed; others follow the status page or read-only channel.
    opening messageINCIDENT SEV-1  Checkout errors  (INC-2291)
Impact: about 40% of checkout attempts fail since 14:02 UTC
IC: Priya   Tech lead: Arun   Comms: Dana   Scribe: Imran
Bridge: <link>   Next sync: 14:30 UTC
Rules: one conversation at a time; findings in this channel, not in DMs.

    🎭 Roles in Detail

    RoleResponsible forMust not
    Incident CommanderCoordinates, sets priorities, makes decisions, keeps the clockDebug personally or go down rabbit holes
    Tech Lead / OpsDirects the technical investigation and changesMake changes without telling the IC
    Subject-matter expertsInvestigate their own service and reportDebate for long without an owner
    Comms LeadWrites status page, executive and customer updatesSpeculate about causes publicly
    ScribeKeeps a timestamped log of actions and decisionsSkip entries because 'it is obvious'

    🎼 The Incident Commander's Playbook

    The IC is the conductor, not a musician: they do not play an instrument, they make sure everyone plays together. Their power is clarity.

    🧴
    Analogy: an orchestra conductor. If the conductor starts playing the violin, no one is keeping time and the music falls apart.
    phrases an IC uses"Status check in 10 minutes. What do we know, what are we trying?"
"Arun, you own the rollback. Report back by 14:35."
"We have two hypotheses. Time-box each to 10 minutes, then we decide."
"Proposal: roll back 4.2.1. Any objections? ... None. Decision made, go."
"Dana, post an update: impact, action, next update at 14:50."

    Notice the pattern: assign an owner, set a deadline, ask for objections, decide. Silence is not agreement, so the IC explicitly asks. Hypotheses are time-boxed so the team never spends an hour on one idea.

    🧘 Keeping the Room Healthy

    Long incidents drain people and tired engineers make mistakes. Rotate roles every 2 to 4 hours, hand over explicitly, and keep the call small.

    handoff script"IC handoff at 17:00 UTC. Summary: checkout recovered at 14:41 after rollback; secondary issue with delayed emails remains. Open items: Arun investigating queue backlog, Dana to post 17:30 update. Next decision: whether to re-enable the new release flag. New IC: Meera. Meera, do you confirm?"
"Confirmed. I am IC from now."
    📌
    The outgoing IC stays reachable for questions, but there is only ever one IC at a time, and the handoff is announced in the channel.

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.During a Sev-1 the IC starts reading logs to find the bug. Why is that a problem and what should they do instead?
    The IC loses the overview: no one is prioritising, tracking time or coordinating communication. They should delegate the investigation to the tech lead, keep time-boxing hypotheses and make decisions.
    Q1.Why must IC handoffs be explicit?
    • A It is tradition
    • B So there is exactly one clear decision-maker at any time
    • C To slow things down
    • D To impress executives

    Module 04 · Detection

    Alerting, SLOs & Triage

    You can only respond to what you detect. Good alerts are specific, actionable and rare.

    📏 SLI, SLO & Error Budget

    TermMeaningExample
    SLIA measured indicatorShare of requests under 500 ms
    SLOThe target for the SLI99.9% over 30 days
    Error budgetAllowed failure0.1% = about 43 minutes/month

    The error budget turns reliability into a number. While budget remains, teams can ship faster; when it is nearly spent, they slow down and fix reliability.

    🧴
    Analogy: a monthly phone data plan. You can spend it freely early on, but once it is nearly gone you must be careful.

    🔔 Good Alerts vs Alert Fatigue

    Good alertBad alert
    Tied to user impact (error rate, latency)CPU > 70% with no user effect
    Includes a runbook link'Something is wrong'
    Pages only when action is needed nowFires 50 times a day, ignored
    ⚠️
    Alert fatigue is dangerous: when everything pages, real incidents get ignored. Delete or downgrade alerts that nobody acts on.

    🧭 Triage in Five Questions

    1. What is the user impact and how many users?
    2. When did it start? What changed (deploy, config, traffic)?
    3. Is it getting worse or stable?
    4. Is there a safe mitigation (rollback, flag, failover)?
    5. Which severity and who needs to be paged?
    runbook# Runbook: Checkout 5xx spike
1. Check dashboard 'checkout-health'
2. Last deploy in 30 min? -> rollback via deploy tool
3. DB connections > 90%? -> scale pool / kill long queries
4. Still failing after 15 min -> page payments on-call

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.A new 99.9% monthly SLO has used 80% of its error budget by day 10. What should the team do?
    Slow or freeze risky releases, prioritise reliability fixes, review the incidents that consumed budget, and tighten alerting and rollback practices until the budget recovers.
    Q1.Alert fatigue is caused by…
    • A Too many low-value alerts
    • B Too few alerts
    • C Fast servers
    • D Good runbooks

    Module 05 · Mitigation

    Mitigation: Stop the Bleeding First

    Restoring service comes before finding the root cause. Learn the standard tools and the discipline for using them safely.

    🩹 Common Mitigations

    Mitigation reduces or removes customer impact quickly, even if the underlying bug still exists. The root cause is fixed later, calmly.

    🧴
    Analogy: a paramedic at an accident. They stop the bleeding and stabilise the patient first; surgery to repair the cause comes after.
    TechniqueWhen it fitsRisk
    Roll back the releaseProblem began right after a deployNeeds DB/schema compatibility
    Turn off a feature flagA new feature is the suspectFlag must exist and be tested
    Fail over to standby/regionPrimary node or region is unhealthyReplica lag, data loss window
    Scale outOverload or capacity shortageCan overload dependencies further
    Load shedding / rate limitingTraffic spike or abuseSome users get errors on purpose
    Circuit breaker / degrade gracefullyA dependency is failingReduced functionality
    RestartMemory leak, stuck processTemporary; hides the cause, loses evidence

    ⌨️ Commands You Should Know

    bashkubectl rollout undo deployment/checkout            # roll back to previous version
kubectl rollout status deployment/checkout          # watch the rollback finish
kubectl scale deployment/checkout --replicas=12     # scale out
kubectl logs deploy/checkout --since=15m | grep ERROR | tail -50
# Capture evidence BEFORE restarting a suspect pod:
kubectl exec checkout-7d9 -- jcmd 1 Thread.print > threads.txt

    The last two lines show an important habit: preserve evidence (logs, thread dump, heap dump) before restarting or deleting anything, otherwise the review has nothing to analyse.

    🧭 Choosing a Mitigation

    1. Ask what changed in the last hours (release, config, flag, traffic, dependency).
    2. If a change correlates with the start time, reverse it (rollback or flag off).
    3. If it is load-related, scale out or shed load.
    4. If a dependency is failing, use a breaker, fallback or failover.
    5. If still unknown, mitigate the symptom (restart or reroute) while a separate team keeps investigating.
    ⚠️
    Rules for changes during an incident: one change at a time, announce it in the channel before you do it, get the IC's approval, record the time, then watch the metric. Parallel uncoordinated changes make it impossible to know what helped or hurt.

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.Errors began 3 minutes after a deploy. List your first two mitigation options and what you must check before using them.
    Roll back the release or turn off the new feature flag. Before rolling back, confirm database and message schemas remain compatible with the old version and capture logs/dumps as evidence.
    Q1.Why capture a thread dump before restarting a hung service?
    • A To make the restart faster
    • B Restarting destroys the evidence needed to find the root cause
    • C Dumps fix the bug
    • D It is a legal rule

    Module 06 · Communication

    Status Pages, Updates & Metrics

    How you communicate during an incident is judged as strongly as how fast you fix it.

    📢 Internal vs External Updates

    ChannelAudienceContentCadence
    War-room chatRespondersTechnical details, hypothesesContinuous
    Exec channelLeadershipImpact, ETA, decisions neededEvery 30 min
    Status pageAll customersPlain impact and workaroundEvery 30–60 min
    Direct emailKey accountsSpecific impact and contactOn change
    🧴
    Analogy: an airline delay. Pilots talk technical details to each other, while passengers need to know the new departure time and what the airline will do for them.

    🗒️ Status Page Template

    templateINVESTIGATING  14:20 UTC  We are investigating errors during checkout.
IDENTIFIED     14:45 UTC  Cause found in a recent release; rolling back.
MONITORING     15:05 UTC  Rollback complete; monitoring recovery.
RESOLVED       15:30 UTC  Service restored. A full report will follow within 3 days.

    Use the standard four states: Investigating → Identified → Monitoring → Resolved. Do not promise a fix time you cannot keep and never publish speculation about cause.

    ⏱️ Incident Metrics

    MetricStands forShows
    MTTDMean time to detectHow quickly you notice
    MTTAMean time to acknowledgeHow quickly someone responds
    MTTRMean time to restoreHow quickly users are back
    MTBFMean time between failuresHow often things break
    💡
    Track the trend of these over months. A falling MTTD usually shows monitoring is improving; a rising MTBF shows reliability work is paying off.

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.Write the 'Identified' status-page message for a failed database failover.
    'We have identified the cause as a failed database failover. Our team is restoring the primary database. Some users may see errors when saving. Next update in 30 minutes.'
    Q1.Standard status page states in order:
    • A Investigating, Identified, Monitoring, Resolved
    • B Resolved, Investigating
    • C Monitoring only

    Root Cause Analysis & Post-mortems

    Turn an outage into lasting improvement with blameless analysis.

    🔍 5 Whys Example

    5 whysOutage: checkout failed 42 min
1 Why? Payment service timed out.
2 Why? DB connections exhausted.
3 Why? Query held connections for 30 s.
4 Why? Missing index after schema change.
5 Why? No performance test on migrations.
Root cause: migration process lacks performance checks.

    📝 Post-mortem Template

    post-mortemSummary & impact (users, duration, revenue)
Timeline (detect, respond, mitigate, resolve)
Root cause & contributing factors
What went well / what went poorly
Action items (owner, due date, priority)
Prevention & detection improvements
    📌
    Blameless means focusing on systems and process, not individuals.

    Module 08 · RCA

    Root Cause Analysis: Method and Reasoning

    How to move from 'what broke' to 'why it was possible', using structured techniques and avoiding the traps of blame.

    🔥 Trigger, Root Cause, Contributing Factors

    An incident rarely has one cause. Separate three things:

    TermMeaningExample
    TriggerThe event that set it offRelease 4.2.1 was deployed
    Root causeThe underlying weakness that made the trigger harmfulMigrations ran queries with no performance test, so a missing index reached production
    Contributing factorsThings that made it worse or longerAlert threshold too high (10 min late), outdated runbook, no canary stage
    🧴
    Analogy: a forest fire. The spark is the trigger, the dry undergrowth is the root cause, and strong wind is a contributing factor. Removing sparks forever is impossible; fixing the dry forest and wind exposure is achievable.

    🧰 RCA Techniques

    TechniqueHow it worksBest for
    5 WhysKeep asking why until you reach a process or system causeSingle clear causal chain
    Fishbone (Ishikawa)Group causes into branches: Process, Technology, Monitoring, Communication, EnvironmentSeveral interacting causes
    Timeline analysisLine up events and spot the gaps and delaysLong, multi-step incidents
    Fault treeBoolean logic of what must be true for failureSafety-critical systems
    5 whys with a branchCheckout failed 41 min
1 Why? Payment service timed out.
   2a Why? DB connections exhausted.        2b Why? Alert fired 10 min late.
   3a Why? Query held connections 30 s.      3b Why? Threshold set at 15% errors.
   4a Why? Missing index after migration.    4b Why? Never revisited after traffic grew.
   5a Why? No performance check on migrations.
Root cause: migration process lacks a performance gate.
Contributing: alerting threshold not reviewed.

    ✍️ Good and Bad Cause Statements

    Weak (blames a person or stops early)Strong (a system or process)
    The engineer forgot to add an indexThe release process has no automated check for missing indexes, so a slow query reached production
    Human errorManual configuration steps with no peer review allowed an invalid value to be applied
    The database was slowThe query scanned 80M rows because the index was missing; capacity was fine
    Monitoring failedThe alert required 15 minutes of sustained errors, delaying detection by 10 minutes
    💡
    'Human error' is a symptom, not a root cause. Ask what in the system made that error easy to make and hard to catch. Stop asking why when you reach something you can change with a process, tool or design.

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.Turn 'the on-call engineer was too slow to respond' into a system-focused cause statement.
    'The paging policy did not escalate unacknowledged alerts after 5 minutes, so the page sat unread for 18 minutes; the on-call rotation also lacked a secondary responder.'
    Q1.A root cause statement should…
    • A Name the person responsible
    • B Describe a fixable system or process weakness
    • C Say 'human error'
    • D Be as short as possible

    Module 09 · Post-mortem

    Writing the Post-mortem

    The document that turns an outage into lasting improvement: structure, blameless language and action items that actually get done.

    📄 The Template

    post-mortemTITLE: INC-2291 Checkout outage, 6 Oct 2026   Severity: Sev-1   Status: Final
AUTHORS / REVIEWERS / DATE

1. SUMMARY        Two to three sentences: what, how long, who was affected
2. IMPACT         Users, % of requests, revenue, SLA/credits, support tickets
3. TIMELINE       UTC timestamps: detect, declare, mitigate, resolve (decisions included)
4. ROOT CAUSE     Trigger, root cause, contributing factors (with evidence)
5. DETECTION      How we found out; MTTD; what should have caught it sooner
6. RESPONSE       What went well / what went poorly / where we got lucky
7. ACTION ITEMS   Prevent, detect, mitigate; owner, due date, priority, ticket link
8. LESSONS        What we learned that applies elsewhere
9. APPENDIX       Graphs, logs, links to channel and dashboards

    A reader who was not in the room should understand the incident from sections 1 to 3 alone. 'Where we got lucky' is valuable: it exposes near-misses that did not hurt this time.

    🕊️ Blameless Language

    BlamefulBlameless
    John deployed a bad releaseRelease 4.2.1 was deployed and contained a regression that tests did not catch
    The team ignored the alertsThe alert was routed to a channel with no on-call owner
    Ops failed to scaleThe autoscaling limit was set below peak demand
    Careless config changeThe config change process permitted an unvalidated value

    Blameless does not mean no accountability. It means assuming people acted reasonably with the information they had, so the investigation can focus on fixing conditions instead of hiding mistakes. If people fear punishment they stop reporting problems and you lose the evidence.

    ✅ Writing Action Items That Get Done

    WeakStrong
    Be more careful with migrationsAdd a CI check that fails migrations with sequential scans over 1M rows (Owner: Platform, due 20 Oct, DEV-882)
    Improve monitoringAlert when checkout 5xx exceeds 2% for 2 minutes and link runbook (Owner: SRE, due 14 Oct, OPS-415)
    Train the teamRun a quarterly game day for DB failover (Owner: IC rotation, first on 15 Nov)
    action item classificationPREVENT  Stop it happening     -> performance gate for migrations
DETECT   Notice sooner         -> lower, faster alert threshold
MITIGATE Recover faster        -> one-click rollback documented in runbook
    💡
    Every action needs an owner, due date, priority and ticket link. Track the completion rate: post-mortems without tracked actions are just stories, and the same incident will return.

    🗣️ The Review Meeting

    1. Circulate the draft at least 24 hours before so people can correct facts.
    2. Use a facilitator who was not the IC; walk the timeline together.
    3. Ask 'what surprised us?' and 'where did we get lucky?'.
    4. Agree on actions and owners in the meeting, not afterwards.
    5. Publish the final post-mortem (internally, and a summary to customers if appropriate).
    6. Review open actions in a regular meeting until all are closed.

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.Rewrite this action item to be strong: 'Improve alerting for the database'.
    'Add an alert when DB connection pool usage exceeds 85% for 2 minutes, paging the database on-call with a link to the runbook. Owner: SRE team, due 14 Oct, ticket OPS-415.'
    Q1.A blameless post-mortem still requires…
    • A Accountability through tracked, owned action items
    • B No action items
    • C Hiding the timeline

    Module 10 · Scenarios

    Incident Scenarios from Production

    Four realistic incidents showing decisions, trade-offs and what the post-mortem should capture.

    🗄️ Scenario: Database failover that did not work

    • Primary DB node fails at 03:10; automatic failover promotes a replica that is 40 s behind.
    • The application starts reading stale data and some writes conflict.
    • Mitigation: set the app to read-only mode, wait for the replica to catch up, then re-enable writes. IC decides to accept 12 minutes of read-only over data inconsistency.
    • Post-mortem actions: replica lag alert, tested failover runbook, quarterly failover game day.

    📜 Scenario: Expired TLS certificate

    • All API calls fail at 09:00 with certificate errors. Impact is global and immediate.
    • Root cause: auto-renewal job had been failing silently for a month; the only notification went to a former employee's mailbox.
    • Mitigation: issue and deploy a new certificate (25 min).
    • Actions: expiry monitoring at 30/14/7 days, alerts to a team alias, remove personal ownership of shared automation.

    💳 Scenario: Third-party payment provider outage

    • The provider is down; we cannot fix it. Your product is affected for 2 hours.
    • Mitigation: switch to the secondary provider through a flag, queue failed payments for retry, show an honest banner to users.
    • Comms: status page "Identified: third-party provider issue", updates every 30 minutes.
    • Learning: dependencies need fallbacks and timeouts; a post-mortem is still needed even when the cause is external.

    🔁 Scenario: The same incident twice

    • A queue backlog caused an outage in March and again in September.
    • The March post-mortem had 6 action items; only 2 were completed and none tracked.
    • Lesson: action completion is part of incident management. Introduce a monthly review, escalate overdue Sev-1 actions to management, and block related risky releases until they are done.

    🏋️ Practice & Quick Check

    Practice
    P1.A third-party provider is down and you cannot fix it. What do you still do during and after the incident?
    During: mitigate (fallback provider, queueing, degrade gracefully), communicate clearly, and track the provider's status. After: run a post-mortem on your detection, resilience and fallback gaps, and add timeouts, circuit breakers and a secondary provider.
    Q1.Why can a post-mortem be needed even when the cause was an external vendor?
    • A It is never needed
    • B To find gaps in your own resilience, detection and communication
    • C To blame the vendor publicly
    • D Only for paperwork
