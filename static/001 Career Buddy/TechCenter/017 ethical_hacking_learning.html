    Ethical Hacking Foundations

    Build the professional mindset first: authorization, scope, risk, testing objectives, and how ethical hackers turn uncertainty into evidence.

    🛡️Why Ethical Hacking Exists

    Ethical hacking is authorized security testing performed to discover weaknesses before they are abused. The value is not in breaking systems for its own sake; it is in producing evidence that helps an owner reduce measurable risk. Modern testing programs connect technical findings to business processes, data, identities, and resilience.

    i
    Practice lens: Think in four questions: What is exposed? What trust boundary is crossed? What could happen? What evidence would convince the owner?

    🛡️Hacker Roles & Security Teams

    Black-hat, gray-hat, and white-hat are shorthand for intent and authorization. In professional environments, the more useful distinction is between roles: penetration testers validate controls, red teams simulate adversaries, blue teams detect and respond, and purple teams connect attack simulation with detection engineering.

    i
    Practice lens: A penetration test usually has a bounded technical objective. A red-team exercise is broader and is designed to test people, processes, technology, and detection together.

    🛡️Penetration Testing vs Vulnerability Assessment

    A vulnerability assessment inventories weaknesses and prioritizes them at scale. A penetration test goes further by validating selected weaknesses, following attack paths, and demonstrating realistic impact within an agreed scope. Scanners are useful inputs, not substitutes for analysis.

    i
    Practice lens: A high scanner count does not automatically mean high risk. Context, exploitability, exposure, business value, and compensating controls matter.

    🛡️Threat, Vulnerability, Exploit & Risk

    A threat is a potential cause of harm; a vulnerability is a weakness; an exploit is a method that takes advantage of a weakness; and risk combines likelihood, impact, and context. Separating these terms makes reports more precise and prevents severity from becoming guesswork.

    i
    Practice lens: Use vulnerability IDs such as CVEs when applicable, but do not confuse a CVE's existence or CVSS score with the complete business risk of a finding.

    🛡️Rules of Engagement & Stop Conditions

    Before testing, define targets, dates, accounts, acceptable techniques, communication paths, data-handling rules, and explicit stop conditions. NIST SP 800-115 frames technical testing around planning, conducting, analyzing, and reporting rather than treating testing as a free-form attack exercise. [NIST SP 800-115]

    i
    Practice lens: When a system behaves unexpectedly, the professional response is to pause, preserve evidence, and follow the escalation path rather than improvising.

    🛡️Professional Evidence Standards

    Good evidence is minimal, reproducible, contextual, and safe to share. Capture timestamps, affected assets, relevant request or system observations, impact, and remediation clues. Sanitize secrets and personal data before including screenshots or samples in a report.

    i
    Practice lens: The strongest finding tells a reader what happened, why it matters, how to reproduce it safely, and how to verify the fix.

    Module 02 · Networking Fundamentals for Ethical Hackers

    Networking Fundamentals for Ethical Hackers

    Understand the protocols, addressing, traffic flows, and trust boundaries that make modern attack surfaces possible.

    🌐OSI & TCP/IP Models

    The OSI model is a conceptual way to reason about networking layers; TCP/IP is the model used by real Internet protocols. Ethical hackers use both as mental maps: when traffic fails, ask whether the problem is link, network, transport, application, or policy behavior.

    i
    Practice lens: Application-layer issues such as HTTP authorization can exist even when lower layers are configured perfectly.

    🌐Ethernet, MAC, ARP & Switching

    Ethernet moves frames on local networks using MAC addresses. ARP resolves IPv4 addresses to local MAC addresses. Switches build forwarding tables from observed source addresses. These mechanisms create local trust relationships that matter during network security assessments.

    i
    Practice lens: When documenting a lab, identify which systems share a broadcast domain and which traffic is routed or filtered.

    🌐IPv4, IPv6 & Subnetting

    IP addressing defines how hosts and networks are identified and routed. Subnetting determines broadcast boundaries and available address space. IPv6 introduces a larger address space and different discovery and configuration mechanisms, so testers should not assume an IPv4-only attack surface.

    i
    Practice lens: Practice subnetting until you can quickly identify network, host, and broadcast concepts without relying on a calculator.

    🌐TCP, UDP, Ports & Sockets

    TCP provides ordered, connection-oriented delivery; UDP provides lightweight datagrams. A port identifies an application endpoint within an IP address. Scanning helps characterize reachable services, but a port number alone never proves what software or function is actually present.

    i
    Practice lens: Interpret scan results as hypotheses to validate rather than facts to blindly trust.

    🌐DNS, DHCP & NAT

    DNS maps names to addresses and other records. DHCP supplies configuration such as addresses and gateways. NAT translates addresses at network boundaries. Together they shape how assets are discovered and how internal services differ from public exposure.

    i
    Practice lens: A security review should distinguish public DNS records, internal DNS, split-horizon behavior, and cloud-managed name resolution where relevant.

    🌐HTTP, SSH, SMB, LDAP & Other Protocols

    Common protocols expose recognizable workflows and security controls. HTTP carries web traffic; SSH provides secure remote administration; SMB supports Windows file and service operations; LDAP supports directory queries and identity data. Testers should understand normal protocol behavior before looking for weaknesses.

    i
    Practice lens: Protocol familiarity makes logs, packet captures, and application traces far easier to interpret.

    🌐Segmentation & Trust Boundaries

    Segmentation limits how far a compromise can spread. VLANs, firewalls, security groups, routing policy, and identity controls can all form boundaries. A good assessment checks whether intended boundaries are actually enforced.

    i
    Practice lens: The key question is not 'Is there a firewall?' but 'What traffic is allowed between these trust zones, and why?'

    Module 03 · Linux for Ethical Hackers

    Linux for Ethical Hackers

    Master the operating-system concepts needed to inspect systems, understand permissions, interpret evidence, and work safely in security labs.

    🐧Filesystem & Processes

    Linux exposes a unified filesystem, standard device interfaces, processes, services, and permissions. Ethical hackers need to navigate these structures to understand where applications store data, how services start, and which processes hold privileges.

    i
    Practice lens: A useful lab habit is to map application files, service definitions, logs, and running processes before changing anything.

    🐧Users, Groups & Permissions

    Unix permissions separate owner, group, and other access and combine read, write, and execute capabilities. Least privilege means accounts and services should receive only the access required for their purpose.

    i
    Practice lens: When investigating a finding, distinguish intended permissions from accidental inheritance or overly broad group membership.

    🐧Services, Packages & Configuration

    Package managers provide repeatable software installation and updates. Services define long-running application behavior. Configuration files often determine exposure, authentication, logging, and privilege boundaries.

    i
    Practice lens: Security testing should consider both the software itself and the configuration choices that surround it.

    🐧Shell & Automation Fundamentals

    Shell skills help testers repeat safe checks, transform outputs, and collect evidence consistently. Variables, pipes, redirection, conditionals, loops, and exit codes are enough to automate many routine lab tasks without turning every exercise into a custom tool.

    i
    Practice lens: Prefer small, auditable scripts with clear inputs and outputs over opaque one-liners that are difficult to review.

    🐧Logs & Host Evidence

    System and application logs can reveal authentication failures, service errors, configuration problems, and security events. A tester should know which logs are relevant before attempting a validation so observations can be correlated with timestamps.

    i
    Practice lens: In a professional engagement, preserve relevant evidence rather than modifying logs or disabling controls.

    🐧Linux Hardening as the Counterpoint

    Ethical hackers learn faster when they understand the fix. Hardening includes timely updates, minimal services, least privilege, strong SSH configuration, controlled file permissions, centralized logging, and secure secret handling.

    i
    Practice lens: For every offensive observation, ask what preventive or detective control would have made the path harder or more visible.

    Module 04 · Windows & Active Directory Fundamentals

    Windows & Active Directory Fundamentals

    Learn the identity, directory, authentication, and policy concepts that dominate enterprise Windows environments.

    🪟Windows Architecture & Security Boundaries

    Windows hosts combine user accounts, services, registry settings, file permissions, security principals, and policy. Ethical hackers map these components to understand where trust is established and where privilege changes.

    i
    Practice lens: Separate local security boundaries from domain-level identity and policy boundaries.

    🪟Domains, Domain Controllers & Active Directory

    Active Directory stores identities, groups, computers, policies, and relationships used to manage Windows environments. A domain controller is a critical identity service, making directory security a central penetration-testing concern.

    i
    Practice lens: Model the environment as an identity graph: users, groups, machines, policies, and trust relationships.

    🪟Kerberos & NTLM

    Kerberos provides ticket-based authentication in domain environments, while NTLM is an older challenge-response protocol still present for compatibility. Understanding these protocols explains why identity configuration, time synchronization, service principals, and legacy dependencies matter.

    i
    Practice lens: Do not assume that 'password authentication' is one mechanism; identity protocols create different logging and attack surfaces.

    🪟LDAP, SMB & Windows Management

    LDAP exposes directory information and search operations. SMB supports file sharing and Windows service interactions. Management technologies such as PowerShell are powerful administrative interfaces and therefore deserve strong authorization and auditing controls.

    i
    Practice lens: The security question is which identities can query or administer which resources, not merely whether a protocol is enabled.

    🪟Group Policy & Identity Security

    Group Policy distributes security and configuration settings across domain-joined systems. Mis-scoped or overly permissive policies can weaken many endpoints simultaneously.

    i
    Practice lens: Review policy inheritance, privileged groups, service accounts, and delegated administration as a connected system.

    🪟AD Attack Surface & Defensive Controls

    Common risk areas include excessive privilege, stale accounts, weak delegation, exposed management paths, insecure service configurations, and incomplete monitoring. Defenses include least privilege, strong identity governance, tiering, privileged access controls, patching, and high-quality audit data.

    i
    Practice lens: Treat an Active Directory review as an identity and trust analysis, not merely a list of Windows commands.

    Module 05 · Reconnaissance & Attack-Surface Discovery

    Reconnaissance & Attack-Surface Discovery

    Turn a defined scope into a documented map of assets, identities, applications, and exposed functionality without overreaching.

    🧭Passive vs Active Reconnaissance

    Passive reconnaissance observes information already available through authorized sources; active reconnaissance interacts with target systems. The distinction matters because active activity can create operational impact or cross a boundary more quickly.

    i
    Practice lens: Choose the least intrusive technique that answers the current question.

    🧭Asset Discovery

    Start with a source-of-truth inventory, then reconcile discovered hosts, domains, cloud assets, applications, APIs, and third parties. A useful attack-surface map records ownership, environment, exposure, and confidence.

    i
    Practice lens: Unknown assets are a governance problem as much as a technical problem.

    🧭DNS & Subdomain Discovery

    DNS records can reveal application names, service boundaries, mail infrastructure, and public endpoints. Subdomain discovery is valuable when it remains within agreed scope and results are validated before deeper testing.

    i
    Practice lens: A discovered hostname is not automatically in scope; scope still controls what you may test.

    🧭Certificate Transparency & Public Metadata

    Public certificates can reveal hostnames and organizational relationships. Search-engine results, documentation, public code, and metadata can also provide clues about technology and deployment patterns.

    i
    Practice lens: Publicly visible information is not automatically permission to interact with the underlying systems.

    🧭Technology Fingerprinting

    Headers, page structure, certificate details, response patterns, and application behavior can suggest frameworks or server technologies. Fingerprints are probabilistic and should be validated before being used to infer a vulnerability.

    i
    Practice lens: Record confidence levels instead of presenting guesses as facts.

    🧭Recon Notes & Evidence

    Maintain an asset register with source, discovery time, scope status, IP/hostname, technology clues, and next validation step. This makes recon reproducible and gives later testers a clean handoff.

    i
    Practice lens: A good recon notebook prevents duplicated work and reduces accidental testing of out-of-scope assets.

    Module 06 · Scanning & Enumeration

    Scanning & Enumeration

    Move from asset discovery to disciplined service characterization and vulnerability validation.

    🔎Host Discovery

    Host discovery determines which systems respond to permitted probes. Results can be incomplete because of filtering, proxies, rate limits, sleeping systems, or different network paths.

    i
    Practice lens: Treat non-response as 'unknown' rather than 'does not exist.'

    🔎Ports & Services

    Port scanning identifies reachable transport endpoints. Enumeration then asks what service is behind each endpoint and how that service behaves. Nmap is widely used for controlled network assessment because it combines discovery, scanning, and service identification capabilities.

    i
    Practice lens: Always validate service identity using more than the port number when the distinction matters.

    🔎Version Detection & Fingerprinting

    Version information can help identify known vulnerabilities and support remediation decisions, but banners are often incomplete, customized, or misleading. Application behavior and package evidence are stronger when available.

    i
    Practice lens: Record the evidence source for every version claim.

    🔎Authenticated vs Unauthenticated Scanning

    Unauthenticated scans show an external perspective; authenticated scanning can inspect configuration and patch state more accurately. The two views answer different questions and should not be treated as interchangeable.

    i
    Practice lens: Use the least-privileged scan account necessary and define exactly what data the scanner may access.

    🔎Vulnerability Scanner Output

    Tools such as Nessus and Greenbone can find large numbers of candidate issues efficiently. Their output needs human validation to remove false positives, understand compensating controls, and prioritize business impact.

    i
    Practice lens: Scanner output is triage data; a professional finding is a validated conclusion.

    🔎Safe Scanning Practice

    Keep rates conservative, avoid disruptive checks unless explicitly authorized, monitor target health, and stop when observed behavior differs from the test assumptions.

    i
    Practice lens: For production environments, coordinate scanning with owners and record time windows and source addresses.

    Module 07 · Vulnerability Assessment & Risk

    Vulnerability Assessment & Risk

    Learn how to validate, score, prioritize, remediate, and retest vulnerabilities in a way that matters to system owners.

    🧪CVE, CWE & Vulnerability Taxonomy

    CVE identifiers describe publicly tracked vulnerabilities. CWE describes recurring weakness patterns. These taxonomies help standardize communication, but neither replaces context-specific risk analysis.

    i
    Practice lens: Use the most specific evidence-supported classification; avoid forcing a finding into a category that does not fit.

    🧪CVSS 4.0

    CVSS v4.0 is an open framework for communicating vulnerability characteristics and severity. It separates Base, Threat, Environmental, and Supplemental metric groups and produces transparent vectors alongside scores. [FIRST CVSS v4.0]

    i
    Practice lens: CVSS is a severity language, not a complete business-risk decision. Environmental and threat context can materially change prioritization.

    🧪Severity vs Business Risk

    A technical severity score is only one input. Business impact depends on data value, exposure, compensating controls, user populations, exploit maturity, and operational consequences.

    i
    Practice lens: A medium technical weakness on a crown-jewel system can deserve urgent attention, while a critical weakness in an isolated lab asset may not.

    🧪False Positives & Validation

    Validation means proving that the reported condition actually exists and is relevant to the affected asset. Correlate scanner evidence with application behavior, configuration, package state, logs, or safe proof.

    i
    Practice lens: A smaller list of accurate findings creates more value than a large list of uncertain alerts.

    🧪Remediation Quality

    A good remediation statement addresses the root cause, not just the visible symptom. It may involve code changes, configuration, architectural changes, identity controls, monitoring, or process improvements.

    i
    Practice lens: Write remediation so an engineer can understand what control needs to change and why.

    🧪Retesting & Closure

    Retesting repeats the meaningful part of the original validation after remediation. Confirm that the intended weakness is gone and that the fix did not create a new control gap.

    i
    Practice lens: Capture before/after evidence and clearly state whether the issue is remediated, partially remediated, or still open.
    Intermediate

    Module 08 · Web Application Security Testing

    Web Application Security Testing

    Study the largest modern application attack surface using OWASP methodology and a control-focused testing mindset.

    🌐HTTP Request/Response Lifecycle

    Web testing starts with understanding requests, methods, parameters, headers, cookies, status codes, redirects, caching, and content types. The same business action can have different security consequences depending on where state is stored and enforced.

    i
    Practice lens: Use browser developer tools or an intercepting proxy in a lab to observe normal application behavior before testing edge cases.

    🌐Authentication & Session Management

    Authentication establishes identity; session management maintains that identity across requests. Review password recovery, MFA flows, session rotation, expiry, logout, cookie attributes, and token handling.

    i
    Practice lens: A secure login page does not guarantee a secure session lifecycle.

    🌐Authorization & Broken Access Control

    Authorization decides which user can perform which action on which resource. Broken access control occurs when those decisions are missing, inconsistent, or enforced only on the client. OWASP's current Top 10 places Broken Access Control at A01:2025. [OWASP Top 10 2025]

    i
    Practice lens: Test the same action across roles, ownership states, and resource IDs using synthetic accounts.

    🌐Injection & SQL Injection

    Injection occurs when untrusted data changes the meaning of an interpreter or query. Parameterized queries keep data separate from SQL structure. OWASP's 2025 list keeps Injection as A05:2025. [OWASP Top 10 2025]

    i
    Practice lens: In a lab, focus first on understanding the boundary and then on the defensive fix rather than memorizing payloads.

    🌐Cross-Site Scripting

    XSS arises when untrusted content reaches a browser execution context without appropriate output encoding or safe handling. Correct defenses depend on the HTML, attribute, JavaScript, CSS, or URL context involved.

    i
    Practice lens: Validate the browser context and verify that the application treats user-controlled data as data rather than executable code.

    🌐CSRF, Cookies & Browser Trust

    CSRF abuses a victim's authenticated browser context to trigger unwanted state-changing requests. Strong anti-CSRF controls, appropriate SameSite cookie settings, origin checks, and re-authentication for sensitive actions can reduce risk.

    i
    Practice lens: A request is not trustworthy merely because it contains a valid session cookie.

    🌐SSRF & Server-Side Fetching

    Server-side request forgery occurs when an application fetches attacker-influenced destinations without sufficiently restricting where it may connect. Testing should focus on the server's network trust boundary and the business function that requires outbound requests.

    i
    Practice lens: The safest validation uses a controlled lab endpoint and observes whether the application reaches an unexpected destination.

    🌐File Upload & Path Traversal

    File handling becomes risky when names, paths, or content are trusted too much. Review storage location, filename normalization, content validation, permissions, retrieval paths, and whether uploaded content becomes executable.

    i
    Practice lens: Separate upload storage from executable application directories whenever the architecture permits.

    🌐Security Misconfiguration & Headers

    Default credentials, exposed debug interfaces, verbose errors, unsafe CORS, missing security headers, permissive storage, and weak TLS settings can create avoidable exposure. OWASP's 2025 list places Security Misconfiguration at A02:2025. [OWASP Top 10 2025]

    i
    Practice lens: Configuration reviews are often the fastest way to turn a vague security concern into a concrete control improvement.

    🌐Business Logic & Race Conditions

    Business logic flaws violate assumptions about workflow, order, state, quantity, ownership, or timing. Race conditions occur when concurrent operations interact in an unsafe way.

    i
    Practice lens: Model the intended state machine first, then look for transitions the server fails to constrain.

    🌐Web Testing Methodology

    OWASP's current Web Security Testing Guide organizes testing around an application-testing framework, web security testing categories, reporting, tools, fuzzing, encoded injection, and developer-tool use. [OWASP WSTG Latest]

    i
    Practice lens: A methodology keeps testing complete and makes findings reproducible across testers and projects.
    Intermediate

    Module 09 · API Security Testing

    API Security Testing

    Treat APIs as first-class security boundaries covering identity, authorization, data exposure, business logic, and operational limits.

    🔗REST & API Architecture

    REST APIs expose resources and actions through HTTP. Modern applications may also use event APIs, GraphQL, gRPC, or internal service interfaces. Map endpoints to business functions rather than focusing only on URLs.

    i
    Practice lens: Inventory methods, parameters, authentication requirements, roles, and data returned by each important endpoint.

    🔗API Authentication & Tokens

    APIs commonly use session cookies, OAuth access tokens, JWTs, API keys, or service identities. Review token scope, lifetime, storage, audience, issuer, and revocation behavior.

    i
    Practice lens: A valid token proves very little unless the API validates the token for the exact resource and action requested.

    🔗BOLA & Authorization

    OWASP API Security Top 10 2023 identifies Broken Object Level Authorization as API1:2023. APIs frequently receive object identifiers, making object-level authorization a critical control. [OWASP API Security Top 10]

    i
    Practice lens: Use two synthetic users in a lab and verify that changing an identifier cannot cross an ownership boundary.

    🔗Property & Function-Level Authorization

    APIs can fail when users are allowed to change properties they should not control or call functions meant for more privileged roles. Modern API testing therefore examines both object ownership and field/function permissions.

    i
    Practice lens: Create an explicit role-action-resource matrix before testing complex APIs.

    🔗Rate Limits & Resource Consumption

    APIs can be abused through excessive requests, expensive queries, oversized inputs, or repeated workflows. Rate limiting is only one control; server-side resource caps, pagination, queueing, and cost-aware design may also be needed.

    i
    Practice lens: Test conservatively and avoid load-testing production systems unless the rules of engagement explicitly authorize it.

    🔗GraphQL & API Documentation

    GraphQL exposes flexible queries against a schema. Security concerns include authorization at the resolver/object level, excessive query complexity, introspection exposure, and inconsistent validation. API documentation can also reveal hidden functionality when it is exposed unintentionally.

    i
    Practice lens: Treat documentation as an attack-surface artifact: useful to testers, but sensitive when it exposes internal functions or data models.

    🔗API Error Handling & Logging

    Errors should reveal enough to support legitimate debugging without disclosing secrets, stack traces, internal paths, or unnecessary data. Logs should capture security-relevant events while protecting sensitive values.

    i
    Practice lens: A good API security design makes failures observable to defenders but unhelpful to unauthorized callers.
    Intermediate

    Module 10 · Authentication, Authorization & Identity Security

    Authentication, Authorization & Identity Security

    Connect application identity, enterprise access control, sessions, OAuth, OIDC, and privilege boundaries into one security model.

    🪪Identity Lifecycle

    Identity security begins with provisioning and ends with timely deprovisioning. Review joiner-mover-leaver processes, dormant accounts, service identities, ownership, recovery paths, and privileged access.

    i
    Practice lens: An account that no longer has a business owner is a security finding even if nobody has exploited it yet.

    🪪Password Security & Credential Handling

    Passwords should be protected with modern password-hashing schemes, strong rate controls, secure recovery, and careful secret handling. Fast, outdated hashes such as MD5 are unsuitable for password storage.

    i
    Practice lens: Do not use real credentials in labs. Use synthetic accounts and test secrets designed for the exercise.

    🪪MFA & Session Security

    MFA adds an independent factor, reducing the impact of password compromise. Testing should consider enrollment, recovery, step-up authentication, session lifetime, device trust, and logout behavior.

    i
    Practice lens: The strongest MFA feature can still be undermined by a weak recovery process.

    🪪RBAC, ABAC & Least Privilege

    RBAC assigns permissions through roles; ABAC evaluates attributes and context. Both should enforce least privilege: users receive only the access required for their work.

    i
    Practice lens: Test effective permissions, not only policy documentation. The real control is what the server actually allows.

    🪪OAuth 2.0 & OpenID Connect

    OAuth 2.0 enables delegated access; OpenID Connect adds an identity layer using standardized claims and flows. Security depends on correct redirect URI validation, state/nonce protections, token audience, scope, and client configuration.

    i
    Practice lens: Always distinguish an authorization token from an identity assertion and document which component validates each one.

    🪪Kerberos & Enterprise Identity

    Kerberos relies on tickets and a trusted Key Distribution Center. Identity attacks often target the relationships among accounts, services, delegation, tickets, and privileges rather than a single login page.

    i
    Practice lens: Focus on the directory's trust model and administrative boundaries before studying individual techniques.
    Intermediate

    Module 11 · Network & Infrastructure Security Testing

    Network & Infrastructure Security Testing

    Assess network controls, remote services, segmentation, wireless exposure, and the resilience of infrastructure security boundaries.

    🛰️Firewall & Segmentation Review

    Review allowed traffic paths, source/destination constraints, administrative access, and logging. Segmentation should reduce unnecessary communication between trust zones.

    i
    Practice lens: A rule that is technically correct but far broader than necessary can still create meaningful risk.

    🛰️IDS/IPS & Security Monitoring

    IDS detects suspicious activity; IPS can block traffic inline. Ethical hacking should consider whether important test actions generate expected alerts and whether defenders can distinguish them from routine activity.

    i
    Practice lens: Red-team and purple-team exercises are especially useful for validating detection coverage.

    🛰️VPN & Remote Access

    Remote-access systems concentrate identity and network trust. Review authentication, MFA, device posture, session management, logging, split-tunnel policy, and administrative exposure.

    i
    Practice lens: Remote access should receive the same scrutiny as an Internet-facing application because it is a gateway into trusted zones.

    🛰️Wireless Security

    Wireless assessments examine authentication, encryption, network segmentation, rogue access points, guest isolation, and management interfaces. WPA2 and WPA3 security depends on both protocol configuration and credential management.

    i
    Practice lens: Use owned access points and isolated lab devices; never test nearby networks without explicit authorization.

    🛰️Insecure Services & Remote Administration

    Legacy protocols, default configurations, exposed management interfaces, and unnecessary services expand the attack surface. Assess whether each service is needed, patched, authenticated, encrypted, and restricted.

    i
    Practice lens: The safest infrastructure is usually the one with fewer unnecessary services and narrower management paths.

    🛰️Infrastructure Resilience

    Availability is part of security. Testers should understand maintenance windows, redundancy, backup assumptions, and recovery procedures before performing potentially disruptive validation.

    i
    Practice lens: A test that proves a point by causing an outage is often professionally worse than a lower-impact proof.
    Advanced

    Module 12 · Privilege Escalation & Post-Compromise Concepts

    Privilege Escalation & Post-Compromise Concepts

    Understand how local configuration and identity weaknesses can transform limited access into broader control, while keeping practice confined to labs.

    ⬆️Privilege Boundaries

    Privilege escalation means moving from a lower-privileged context to a higher-privileged one. On Linux and Windows, boundaries can be created by OS permissions, services, tasks, tokens, security policies, and application design.

    i
    Practice lens: Start by mapping the current identity, allowed actions, and trust boundaries rather than jumping to exploit hunting.

    ⬆️Linux Escalation Concepts

    Common categories include weak file permissions, unsafe service configurations, excessive sudo rights, insecure scheduled tasks, exposed secrets, and vulnerable software. The learning objective is to recognize why these conditions matter and how defenders remove them.

    i
    Practice lens: Use deliberately vulnerable hosts and synthetic credentials; record each step so the lab can be reset and the finding retested.

    ⬆️Windows Escalation Concepts

    Windows privilege boundaries can involve services, scheduled tasks, access-control lists, tokens, policies, registry permissions, and software configuration. Misconfigured privileges can expose powerful execution paths.

    i
    Practice lens: A reliable assessment distinguishes a genuine privilege boundary violation from a capability the current user is intentionally supposed to have.

    ⬆️Credential Exposure

    Credentials and secrets can appear in configuration files, logs, environment variables, scripts, browser stores, CI systems, and service settings. Ethical testing validates exposure and then protects the data encountered.

    i
    Practice lens: Never paste real secrets into chat, notes, screenshots, or lab exercises.

    ⬆️Post-Compromise Decision Making

    After confirming access, a professional tester asks whether further activity is necessary to demonstrate impact. The objective is to prove risk with the least additional exposure and to preserve the option for safe cleanup.

    i
    Practice lens: Stop when you have enough evidence; 'more access' is not automatically 'more valuable evidence.'

    ⬆️Containment & Cleanup

    Cleanup means removing test artifacts, restoring configuration when required, closing temporary accounts, and verifying that systems return to an agreed state. The engagement report should disclose any changes made.

    i
    Practice lens: A clean handoff is part of testing quality, not an optional courtesy.
    Advanced

    Module 13 · Wireless & Physical Security

    Wireless & Physical Security

    Extend ethical hacking beyond servers to radios, facilities, devices, and human trust relationships.

    📶Wi-Fi Architecture

    Wireless networks use access points, clients, authentication mechanisms, encryption, and management traffic. Security reviews examine segmentation, guest access, management exposure, and credential practices.

    i
    Practice lens: Document what a user can reach after connecting to each SSID; that is often more important than the SSID name itself.

    📶Rogue Access Points & Evil-Twin Concepts

    A rogue access point appears where an unauthorized wireless device provides connectivity. Evil-twin scenarios mimic a legitimate network to influence client behavior. Testing these scenarios requires an explicitly authorized lab and controlled devices.

    i
    Practice lens: The learning goal is to understand trust and detection, not to collect credentials from real users.

    📶Physical Security Testing

    Physical assessments consider doors, visitor processes, badge controls, exposed devices, removable media, and secure areas. Physical findings can bypass strong digital controls when attackers gain direct access to systems or information.

    i
    Practice lens: Define exactly what physical actions are allowed and ensure stakeholders understand the exercise before it begins.

    📶Social Engineering Concepts

    Phishing, vishing, pretexting, tailgating, and related techniques exploit human trust. Professional simulations require written authorization, safety boundaries, target selection criteria, and careful handling of results.

    i
    Practice lens: Measure behaviors and control effectiveness; never shame individual employees in reports.

    📶Removable Media & Device Security

    USB devices, laptops, printers, cameras, and embedded systems can expose data or trusted network paths. Review device inventories, access controls, encryption, update processes, and endpoint monitoring.

    i
    Practice lens: A device's physical placement can change its security exposure even when its software configuration is strong.
    Advanced

    Module 14 · Cloud, Containers & Kubernetes Security Testing

    Cloud, Containers & Kubernetes Security Testing

    Apply ethical-hacking methodology to dynamic cloud infrastructure, identity systems, storage, workloads, APIs, and container platforms.

    ☁️Shared Responsibility

    Cloud providers secure the underlying service, while customers retain responsibilities that vary by service model. NIST CSF 2.0 provides a broader risk-management frame for governance and security outcomes. [NIST CSF 2.0]

    i
    Practice lens: Always identify which party controls the tested layer before attributing a finding.

    ☁️Cloud Attack Surface

    Cloud environments expose APIs, identity policies, storage, network controls, secrets, CI/CD pipelines, serverless functions, and managed services. Asset inventory is therefore partly an identity and configuration problem.

    i
    Practice lens: Track resources by account, region, environment, owner, data class, and public/private exposure.

    ☁️IAM & Over-Privileged Roles

    Cloud IAM policies can grant actions across resources and often use reusable identities. Overly broad permissions create larger blast radii when credentials or workloads are compromised.

    i
    Practice lens: Review effective permissions and trust relationships, not only the written policy statements.

    ☁️Storage, Secrets & Logging

    Common cloud risks include public or unintended storage exposure, secrets embedded in code or pipelines, missing logs, and weak retention. Testing should validate access boundaries and auditability without copying sensitive production data.

    i
    Practice lens: The safest proof is often a synthetic object or controlled test record.

    ☁️Containers & Images

    Container security includes image provenance, dependency vulnerabilities, secrets, runtime privileges, network exposure, and host isolation. Scanning images before deployment is useful, but scanners still require triage and contextual validation.

    i
    Practice lens: Treat containers as workloads that inherit risks from both application dependencies and the underlying platform.

    ☁️Kubernetes Security

    Kubernetes introduces APIs, service accounts, namespaces, network policies, admission controls, secrets, and workload identities. Misconfiguration can create broad privilege or network reach.

    i
    Practice lens: Start with a cluster architecture map and namespace/service-account inventory before evaluating individual workloads.
    Advanced

    Module 15 · Red Teaming, Adversary Simulation & Purple Teaming

    Red Teaming, Adversary Simulation & Purple Teaming

    Move from isolated vulnerabilities to controlled adversary emulation and measurable defensive validation.

    🎯What a Red Team Tests

    A red team evaluates whether a realistic adversary can achieve defined objectives while defenders operate normally. The scope may include technology, people, physical access, cloud, identity, and business processes.

    i
    Practice lens: Unlike a checklist pentest, the exercise is organized around objectives and attack paths, not simply vulnerability counts.

    🎯MITRE ATT&CK as a Map

    MITRE ATT&CK describes adversary tactics as the 'why' and techniques as the 'how'. The Enterprise matrix spans reconnaissance, initial access, execution, persistence, privilege escalation, credential access, discovery, lateral movement, collection, command and control, exfiltration, and impact. [MITRE ATT&CK]

    i
    Practice lens: Use ATT&CK to describe behavior and detection coverage, not as a shopping list of attack commands.

    🎯Adversary Emulation

    Adversary emulation maps a controlled exercise to documented adversary behaviors and success criteria. The best plans specify objective, permitted technique families, safety limits, expected detection, and cleanup.

    i
    Practice lens: Predefine what constitutes sufficient evidence so the team does not escalate activity merely because a technique is available.

    🎯Detection Validation

    A purple team connects offensive activity to defensive telemetry. The question becomes: did the expected signal appear, was it correlated correctly, and could an analyst make a useful decision from it?

    i
    Practice lens: This creates a direct feedback loop between penetration testing and security operations.

    🎯Lateral Movement & Trust Analysis

    Lateral movement explores how access to one asset may expose additional systems or identities. Ethical testing should use narrow, evidence-driven steps and stop once the agreed attack path is demonstrated.

    i
    Practice lens: Map trust relationships and identity paths before interacting with additional hosts.

    🎯Measuring Coverage

    Useful measures include objectives achieved, controls bypassed, detections generated, time to triage, time to contain, and remediation or detection improvements. Counts alone can be misleading.

    i
    Practice lens: The purpose of an advanced exercise is learning and risk reduction, not maximizing technical spectacle.
    Advanced

    Module 16 · Reporting, Risk, Remediation & Professional Practice

    Reporting, Risk, Remediation & Professional Practice

    Turn technical observations into decisions that engineers, managers, and risk owners can act on.

    📝Finding Structure

    A professional finding normally includes title, affected asset, description, preconditions, evidence, impact, severity, root cause, remediation, references, and retest guidance. Structure makes findings comparable and reviewable.

    i
    Practice lens: Write the conclusion first, then provide only the evidence needed to support it.

    📝Executive Summary

    Executives need the story of business exposure, not a command transcript. Summaries should explain what was tested, the most material risks, themes, and recommended priorities.

    i
    Practice lens: Avoid technical detail that does not change the decision the reader needs to make.

    📝Evidence & Reproducibility

    Evidence should be sufficient for an independent reviewer to understand the observation. Include timestamps, sanitized requests/responses, relevant screenshots, affected assets, and environmental context.

    i
    Practice lens: Reproducibility is a quality signal; ambiguity creates avoidable remediation delays.

    📝Risk Prioritization

    Combine severity, exposure, business criticality, exploit maturity, population affected, and compensating controls. CVSS can standardize vulnerability severity, while business risk requires context. [FIRST CVSS v4.0]

    i
    Practice lens: Prioritize the fixes that reduce the most meaningful risk, not simply the findings with the biggest numerical score.

    📝Remediation & Retesting

    A remediation plan should identify owners, actions, dependencies, and validation criteria. Retesting verifies that the original weakness is fixed and that the fix does not create a new gap.

    i
    Practice lens: Close the loop by linking each original finding to concrete verification evidence.

    📝Responsible Disclosure & Ethics

    Professional practice includes protecting confidential information, respecting engagement boundaries, reporting responsibly, and documenting unexpected events. Ethical hackers are trusted because they can find serious weaknesses without becoming the source of harm.

    i
    Practice lens: The simplest ethical rule remains the strongest: only test systems you own or are explicitly authorized to assess.
