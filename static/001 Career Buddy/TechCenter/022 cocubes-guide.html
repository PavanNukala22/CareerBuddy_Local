    What is CoCubes? Who is it for? Quick Facts The Recruitment Connection

    CoCubes

    An assessment and campus-hiring platform that turns your aptitude and skills into a profile recruiters shortlist.

    What is CoCubes?

    CoCubes is one of India's largest assessment and campus-hiring platforms, now part of Aon. Companies use it to evaluate final-year students and fresh graduates on aptitude, communication, and domain skills — and to run the online tests that decide who moves to the interview stage.

    Think of CoCubes less as a pass/fail exam and more as a standardized skill scorecard. Instead of every company running its own aptitude round, many rely on your CoCubes result to decide who gets shortlisted.

    Who is it for?

    Mainly final-year engineering and MBA students who want to appear in campus placement drives. You can register either through a partner college (usually free) or on your own with an individual subscription. Once your profile is tested, it sits in a pool that recruiting companies can search and call for interviews.

    The Recruitment Connection

    Your CoCubes score isn't graded by one company — it's shared. Once you clear the aptitude gate, your profile becomes visible to every recruiter using the platform. A strong score can put you in front of companies you never directly applied to, which is why treating this as a one-time formality is a mistake.

    Test Structure

    One sitting, several timed modules — and you can't go back once a section closes.

    The Five Modules

    ModuleWhat it checks
    Aptitude (EAT)Quantitative, Logical Reasoning and Verbal Ability, roughly 20 questions each
    Computer FundamentalsCore CS concepts for technical roles — OS, DBMS, networking basics
    Domain / SpecialistBranch-specific questions (CSE, ECE, Mechanical, Civil, etc.), around 30 questions
    CodingProgramming problems in C, C++, Java or Python for technical profiles
    Written English & PersonalityShort writing task plus a psychometric profile — not scored right/wrong

    How the Test Flows

    The test is delivered as a proctored, computer-based exam. Sections run one after another with a fixed timer; once a section's time is up or you move on, you cannot return to it. Not every candidate sees every module — which ones you get depends on the job profile and branch you're registered under.

    Rules Worth Knowing

    • There is no negative marking anywhere in the aptitude or domain sections — an unattempted question and a wrong one cost you the same.
    • The personality section has no right answers; consistency in how you respond matters more than trying to guess what recruiters want.
    • You can retake the test roughly every 14 days if slots are open, and your best profile is what companies see.

    Core Sections

    The syllabus mirrors most fresher aptitude platforms — prep for one transfers almost directly to CoCubes.

    Quantitative Aptitude

    This is the section that decides your speed reputation more than any other. Every topic below moves from the plain concept, to the shortcut a fresher should know, to the trap that separates an intermediate solver from an advanced one.

    PercentagesRatio & ProportionTime & Work Time, Speed & DistanceProfit & LossNumber Systems Permutations & CombinationsProbability

    Percentages Beginner

    A percentage is just a fraction out of 100 — "20%" literally means 20 out of every 100. Almost every other quant topic (profit/loss, interest, data interpretation) is percentages wearing a costume, so this is the one topic worth over-learning first.

    Core formulaPercentage = (Part / Whole) × 100

    Beginner move: memorise fraction-to-percentage equivalents (1/2=50%, 1/3≈33.3%, 1/4=25%, 1/5=20%, 1/8=12.5%) so you can convert instantly instead of doing long division.

    Intermediate move: successive percentage changes don't add up simply. A 20% increase followed by a 20% decrease does not return you to the original value.

    Successive changeNet % change = a + b + (ab / 100), using negative values for decreases
    Worked example

    A price rises 20%, then falls 20%. Net change = 20 + (−20) + (20 × −20)/100 = 0 − 4 = −4%.

    The final price is 4% lower than the original — not the same.

    Advanced move: in "population growth/depreciation" style questions, treat each year as a separate multiplying factor: value × (1 ± r/100) repeated for each year, rather than trying to add percentages across years.

    Ratio & Proportion Beginner

    A ratio compares two quantities of the same kind (a:b), while a proportion says two ratios are equal (a:b = c:d). The whole topic is about keeping relative sizes fixed while the absolute numbers scale up or down.

    Core ruleIf a:b = c:d, then a×d = b×c (cross-multiplication)

    Beginner move: always simplify a ratio to its lowest terms first (45:60 → 3:4) — CoCubes options are usually written in simplest form, so an unsimplified answer won't match.

    Intermediate move: when a total is split in a given ratio, divide the total by the sum of the ratio parts to get the value of "one part," then multiply.

    Worked example

    ₹720 is split between A and B in the ratio 5:4. One part = 720 / 9 = 80. A gets 5 × 80 = ₹400, B gets 4 × 80 = ₹320.

    Advanced move: for problems mixing two ratios on a shared quantity (e.g. A:B = 2:3 and B:C = 4:5), make the common term (B) equal by scaling both ratios to the LCM of its two values before combining into A:B:C.

    Time & Work Intermediate

    The trick that unlocks this entire topic: don't think in "days," think in "work done per day." If someone finishes a job in 10 days, they complete 1/10 of the job every single day — that fraction is their rate, and rates simply add when people work together.

    Core formulaCombined rate = 1/A + 1/B (jobs per day) → Time together = 1 / (combined rate)
    Worked example

    A finishes a job in 12 days, B in 18 days. Combined rate = 1/12 + 1/18 = 3/36 + 2/36 = 5/36 per day.

    Time together = 36/5 = 7.2 days.

    Intermediate move: "efficiency" questions just mean unequal rates — if A is twice as efficient as B, A's rate is twice B's rate; set B's time as a variable and A's as half of it.

    Advanced move: in "pipes and cisterns" variants, an outlet pipe that empties a tank is a negative rate. Add it into the combined-rate equation with a minus sign instead of treating it as a separate case.

    Time, Speed & Distance Intermediate

    Everything here rests on one relationship, applied in whichever direction the question needs.

    Core formulaSpeed = Distance / Time  ·  1 km/h = 5/18 m/s  ·  1 m/s = 18/5 km/h

    Beginner move: keep units consistent before you calculate — mixing km/h with metres and seconds is the single most common error in this topic.

    Worked example

    A train covers 60 km in 1.5 hours. Speed = 60 / 1.5 = 40 km/h.

    Intermediate move — relative speed: when two bodies move toward each other, add their speeds; when moving in the same direction, subtract them.

    Advanced move — trains crossing objects: when a train crosses another train, a platform, or a pole, the "distance covered" is the sum of both lengths (train + train, or train + platform); a pole/person is treated as a point, so only the train's own length counts.

    Worked example

    Two trains, 150 m and 100 m long, run toward each other at 54 km/h and 36 km/h. Relative speed = 90 km/h = 25 m/s. Total length to cross = 250 m.

    Time to cross = 250 / 25 = 10 seconds.

    Profit & Loss Beginner

    Built entirely on comparing Cost Price (CP), Selling Price (SP), and sometimes a Marked Price (MP) that gets discounted before it becomes SP.

    Core formulasProfit% = ((SP − CP) / CP) × 100  ·  SP = MP × (1 − discount%/100)
    Worked example

    A shirt priced ₹800 is sold at a 25% discount. SP = 800 × 0.75 = ₹600.

    Intermediate move: when a question gives "marked up X% then discounted Y%," assume CP = 100 and work the two percentage moves in sequence rather than juggling variables — it turns algebra into arithmetic.

    Worked example

    Goods marked 40% above cost, then a 25% discount is given. Let CP = 100 → Marked price = 140 → SP = 140 × 0.75 = 105.

    Net profit = 5% (SP 105 vs CP 100).

    Advanced move: in "false weight" or "dishonest dealer" problems, the effective profit% comes from comparing the goods actually given to the goods claimed, using the ratio Profit% = (Error / (True value − Error)) × 100.

    Number Systems Intermediate

    Covers divisibility, remainders, LCM/HCF, and properties of primes, factors and multiples — the "pure logic" corner of quant that rewards knowing rules over calculating.

    Useful rulesDivisible by 3 → digit sum divisible by 3  ·  Divisible by 9 → digit sum divisible by 9  ·  LCM × HCF = product of the two numbers

    Beginner move: learn the divisibility rules for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11 cold — they turn "is this divisible by X" into a five-second check instead of long division.

    Intermediate move: for HCF/LCM word problems ("find the largest number that divides X and Y leaving remainder R"), first subtract the remainder from each number, then take the HCF of the results.

    Advanced move: remainder theorems — to find the remainder of a large power like 7^45 divided by a number, look for a repeating cycle in the remainders of 7^1, 7^2, 7^3… and use modular arithmetic instead of computing the full power.

    Permutations & Combinations Advanced

    The one distinction this topic hinges on: permutation cares about order, combination doesn't. "In how many ways can 3 people be seated" is a permutation; "in how many ways can 3 people be chosen" is a combination.

    Core formulasnPr = n! / (n−r)!  ·  nCr = n! / (r! × (n−r)!)
    Worked example

    Choosing 3 books from 8 different books (order doesn't matter): 8C3 = 8! / (3! × 5!) = 56.

    Intermediate move: for words with repeated letters, divide the total arrangements by the factorial of each letter's repeat count.

    Worked example

    Arranging the letters of "LEADER" (6 letters, E repeats twice): 6! / 2! = 360.

    Advanced move: "at least one" or "none of a group" conditions are usually easier solved as (total ways) − (ways that violate the condition) rather than trying to directly count the valid cases.

    Probability Advanced

    Probability builds directly on combinations — the denominator is almost always "total ways something can happen," and the numerator is "ways the favourable outcome happens."

    Core formulaP(event) = Favourable outcomes / Total outcomes, always between 0 and 1
    Worked example

    Probability of drawing a king from a standard 52-card deck: 4 kings / 52 cards = 1/13.

    Intermediate move: for "at least one" probability questions, it's almost always faster to compute 1 − P(none of it happens) than to add up every individual favourable case.

    Advanced move: for dependent events (drawing without replacement), the probability of the second event changes because the total pool shrinks — multiply the probabilities of each stage using the updated pool size at each step, not the original one.

    Logical Reasoning

    This section rewards structure over speed-math — draw it out. A quick diagram for family trees, directions, or a truth table for series almost always beats trying to hold everything in your head.

    Number & Letter SeriesCoding-DecodingBlood Relations Direction SenseSyllogismsData Sufficiency

    Number & Letter Series Beginner

    You're given a sequence and asked to find the next term (or the odd one out). The skill is spotting the pattern of change between consecutive terms, not the terms themselves.

    Beginner move: first check the simplest patterns in order — constant difference, constant ratio (doubling/halving), then differences that themselves form a pattern.

    Worked example

    Series: 2, 4, 8, 16, __. Each term doubles the one before it.

    Next term = 16 × 2 = 32.

    Intermediate move: when the plain differences aren't constant, look at the differences of the differences — a second layer of pattern.

    Worked example

    Series: 3, 8, 15, 24, 35, __. Differences: 5, 7, 9, 11, 13 — each jumps by 2 (odd numbers).

    Next difference is 13, so next term = 35 + 13 = 48.

    Advanced move: for letter series, convert letters to their alphabet position (A=1…Z=26) mentally, apply the numeric pattern, then convert back — this avoids fumbling the alphabet under time pressure.

    Coding-Decoding Intermediate

    A word is encoded using a hidden rule (usually a letter shift), and you must apply the same rule to a new word.

    Beginner move: compare the given word and its code letter by letter, and note the shift for each position — most CoCubes questions use a simple fixed shift across every letter.

    Worked example

    CODING → DPEJOH. Check: C→D (+1), O→P (+1), D→E (+1), I→J (+1), N→O (+1), G→H (+1). Every letter shifts forward by 1.

    Apply the same +1 shift to FLOWER: F→G, L→M, O→P, W→X, E→F, R→S.

    FLOWER → GMPXFS.

    Advanced move: some codes use different shifts for different letter positions, or reverse the word before shifting — if a uniform shift doesn't fit, check odd/even positions separately before concluding the pattern is inconsistent.

    Blood Relations Intermediate

    These read like riddles but are really just family-tree puzzles. The fix for almost every mistake here is the same: draw a quick tree with arrows instead of tracking relationships in your head.

    Beginner move: resolve phrases like "only son of my mother" first — that's just the speaker themselves — before working outward to the person being asked about.

    Worked example

    "She is the daughter of my mother's only son." My mother's only son is me (the speaker, Raj). So "she" is Raj's daughter.

    The girl is Raj's daughter.

    Intermediate move: for two-person puzzles ("pointing to X, Y said…"), draw two small trees — one from each speaker's perspective — and merge them once both are resolved.

    Worked example

    "His mother is the only daughter of my mother," a woman says about a man. "Only daughter of my mother" = the woman herself. So the man's mother is the woman.

    The woman is the man's mother.

    Advanced move: when multiple relations stack up (e.g. across three generations with siblings and in-laws), assign short labels (M1, M2, F1…) as you read, rather than full names — it keeps the tree readable under time pressure.

    Direction Sense Beginner

    Someone walks in a sequence of directions and turns, and you must find their final position or distance from the start. Always sketch a simple compass (N/E/S/W) and trace the path.

    Beginner move: remember "left turn" and "right turn" are relative to the direction you're currently facing, not fixed compass directions — redraw your heading after every turn.

    Worked example

    A man walks 5 km East, then 3 km North, then 5 km West. The 5 km East and 5 km West cancel out.

    He ends up 3 km North of the start — a straight-line distance of 3 km.

    Advanced move: when the path doesn't cancel out neatly, treat East/West as one axis and North/South as the other, sum each axis separately, and use the Pythagorean theorem (√(x² + y²)) to find the final straight-line distance.

    Syllogisms Advanced

    You're given two or more statements treated as universally true, and must decide which conclusions logically follow — using only what's stated, never outside knowledge or "common sense."

    Beginner move: draw Venn diagrams for each statement. "All A are B" is a small circle A fully inside a larger circle B; "Some A are B" is two overlapping circles.

    Worked example

    Statements: All pens are books. All books are tables.
    Conclusion I: All pens are tables. Conclusion II: Some tables are pens.

    Chaining the two "All" statements gives Conclusion I directly. Since pens exist and all of them are tables, at least some tables must be pens — Conclusion II also follows.

    Both I and II follow.

    Advanced move: when conclusions involve "Some," check both directions — "Some A are B" always implies "Some B are A," but "All A are B" does not imply "All B are A." This asymmetry is where most syllogism traps hide.

    Data Sufficiency Advanced

    You're given a question plus two statements, and must judge whether each statement — alone or combined — gives enough information to answer, without actually solving for the final numeric answer.

    Beginner move: always test Statement I completely alone first, then Statement II completely alone, before ever considering them together — combining too early is the most common way marks are lost here.

    Intermediate move: the goal is only to determine whether a unique answer can be derived — if a statement leads to two or more possible answers, it's insufficient, even if it feels "close."

    Advanced move: watch for statements that look identical in form but differ in what they lock down — e.g. "the average of two numbers is 10" is not sufficient to find both numbers, but combined with "their difference is 4," it becomes sufficient. Sufficiency is about combined constraints, not information volume.

    Verbal / English

    This section tests whether you can read for meaning quickly and write/recognise correct, natural English — not literary analysis. Precision beats vocabulary size.

    Reading ComprehensionSynonyms & AntonymsSentence Correction Para JumblesGrammar

    Reading Comprehension Beginner

    A short passage followed by 3–5 questions testing whether you understood the main idea, specific details, tone, or what a word means in that context.

    Beginner move: skim the passage first for structure (what's the topic, how many paragraphs, roughly what each one argues) before reading questions — don't try to memorise details on the first pass.

    Intermediate move: for "which of these is implied" questions, the correct answer is a restatement of something the passage actually says, not something merely plausible or true in the real world.

    Advanced move: when two options both seem correct, check for absolute words like "always," "never," or "only" — comprehension answers usually avoid extreme claims unless the passage itself is extreme.

    Synonyms & Antonyms Beginner

    Straightforward vocabulary recall — pick the word closest in meaning (synonym) or opposite in meaning (antonym) to the given word.

    Worked example

    Synonym of "Rapid": Slow, Quick, Careful, Silent. "Rapid" means happening quickly.

    Answer: Quick.

    Intermediate move: when unsure of the exact word, eliminate options with the opposite connotation (positive vs negative tone) first — this alone often narrows four options to two.

    Advanced move: build vocabulary through word roots (e.g. "bene-" = good, "mal-" = bad) rather than isolated word lists — one root unlocks a dozen related words at once.

    Sentence Correction Intermediate

    You're shown a sentence and must pick the grammatically correct version, or identify which part contains an error.

    Worked example

    Identify the error: "Neither of the answers are correct."

    "Neither" is a singular subject, so it needs a singular verb.

    Error is in "are" — it should read "Neither of the answers is correct."

    Beginner move: check subject-verb agreement first — it's the single most tested error type in this format.

    Advanced move: read the sentence back with each option substituted in, rather than analysing grammar rules abstractly — a sentence that "sounds wrong" out loud usually has an identifiable rule broken.

    Para Jumbles Intermediate

    Several sentences are given out of order, and you must arrange them into a coherent paragraph.

    Beginner move: find the sentence that introduces the topic in general terms — it's almost always the opening line — and the sentence that clearly concludes or summarises, which anchors the end.

    Intermediate move: look for linking words (however, therefore, this, these, such) — a sentence starting with "this" or "such" must follow the sentence that introduces the noun it refers to.

    Advanced move: once you've fixed the first and last sentences, test the middle order by reading pairs consecutively — if a pair doesn't flow logically, swap and re-test rather than guessing the full order at once.

    Grammar Beginner

    Covers tenses, articles (a/an/the), prepositions, and common confusions (its/it's, their/there/they're). This is the most "learnable" part of verbal — the rules are finite and consistent.

    Worked example

    "Despite the heavy rain, the match ____ on schedule." A simple past action fits the context.

    Answer: "continued" (simple past), not "was continued" or "has continued."

    Intermediate move: for tense questions, identify the time-marker word first (yesterday, since, by the time, despite) — each tense has a small set of words that almost always signal it.

    Advanced move: for paraphrase/restatement questions ("choose the option that best restates…"), the correct option preserves the original meaning and tone even when the vocabulary changes completely — don't pick the option that merely reuses the most words from the original.

    Computer Fundamentals

    Relevant mainly for technical and CS/IT profiles. Covers basics of operating systems, DBMS, networking and object-oriented concepts — enough depth to confirm you understand what you'll be working with, not deep specialization.

    OS BasicsDBMS & SQLData StructuresOOP ConceptsNetworking Basics

    OS Basics Beginner

    An operating system manages a computer's hardware and lets programs share it safely. Three ideas cover most CoCubes questions: processes, memory, and scheduling.

    Beginner move: know the difference between a process (a program in execution, with its own memory space) and a thread (a lighter unit of execution inside a process that shares memory with other threads of the same process).

    Intermediate move: understand CPU scheduling basics — FCFS (first-come-first-served), SJF (shortest job first), and Round Robin (fixed time slices) — and why each trades off fairness against throughput differently.

    Advanced move: know the four Coffman conditions required for a deadlock to occur: mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, and circular wait — removing any one of these prevents deadlock. Watch for questions that name "preemption" alone as a condition; the actual condition is no preemption.

    DBMS & SQL Intermediate

    A DBMS organises data into tables and lets you query it reliably. CoCubes tests both conceptual understanding (keys, normalization) and basic SQL syntax.

    Beginner move: a primary key uniquely identifies a row within its own table; a foreign key is a column that references the primary key of another table, creating a relationship between the two.

    SQL filteringWHERE filters individual rows before grouping; HAVING filters groups after a GROUP BY, based on an aggregate condition (like COUNT or SUM).

    Intermediate move: learn normalization step by step — 1NF removes repeating groups, 2NF removes partial dependencies on part of a composite key, and 3NF removes transitive dependencies (a non-key column depending on another non-key column instead of the primary key directly).

    Advanced move: understand JOIN types conceptually — INNER JOIN returns only matching rows from both tables, LEFT JOIN keeps every row from the left table even without a match, and know that indexes speed up reads but slow down writes, which is why they're not added blindly to every column.

    Data Structures Intermediate

    A data structure is just a way of organising data so certain operations (search, insert, delete) are efficient. Knowing which structure fits which situation matters more than memorising implementations.

    Beginner move: know the core building blocks — arrays (fixed-size, fast random access), linked lists (flexible size, sequential access), stacks (Last-In-First-Out — the last element added is the first removed), and queues (First-In-First-Out).

    Worked example

    Which structure follows LIFO? A Stack adds and removes from the same end, so the last item pushed is always the first popped.

    Answer: Stack.

    Intermediate move: understand trees (especially binary search trees) and why they enable faster search than a plain list — each comparison eliminates roughly half the remaining data.

    Time complexityBinary search on a sorted array: O(log n) — each step halves the search space, versus O(n) for a linear scan.

    Advanced move: recognise which structure a scenario implies before coding it — "process the most recent item first" is a stack; "handle requests in the order they arrived" is a queue; "quickly check if an item exists" often points to a hash table.

    OOP Concepts Intermediate

    Object-Oriented Programming organises code around "objects" that bundle data and behaviour together. Four pillars come up repeatedly.

    Beginner move: learn the four pillars by name and definition — Encapsulation (bundling data with the methods that operate on it, hiding internal details), Abstraction (exposing only what's necessary, hiding complexity), Inheritance (a class reusing and extending another class's behaviour), and Polymorphism (the same method name behaving differently depending on the object calling it).

    Worked example

    Which OOP concept lets a single function name behave differently based on the calling object? That's the definition of Polymorphism, not Inheritance (which is about reuse) or Encapsulation (which is about data-hiding).

    Answer: Polymorphism.

    Advanced move: understand runtime polymorphism specifically — in languages like C++/Java, declaring a method virtual (or overriding it in Java by default) allows the correct derived-class version to be selected at runtime based on the actual object type, not the reference type — this is called dynamic binding.

    Networking Basics Beginner

    Covers how computers communicate — the layered model that breaks communication into manageable pieces, and the core protocols built on top of it.

    Beginner move: know the rough purpose of the OSI model's key layers: Physical (raw bits over a medium), Network (routing/addressing, IP), Transport (reliable delivery, TCP/UDP), and Application (the protocols apps actually use, like HTTP).

    Intermediate move: understand the difference between TCP (connection-oriented, guarantees delivery and order — used where reliability matters, like file transfer) and UDP (connectionless, faster but no delivery guarantee — used where speed matters more than perfection, like video streaming).

    Advanced move: know what an IP address and subnet mask together define (which part of an address identifies the network vs the host), and the basic role of DNS (translating human-readable domain names into IP addresses) — questions often test recognising these roles rather than doing subnetting math.

    Coding & Specialist

    Where technical profiles are actually separated from the rest of the applicant pool.

    The Coding Round

    Available for technical roles, usually in C, C++, Java or Python. Expect a small number of problems — output-prediction, debugging a short snippet, or writing a function against sample input/output — rather than a long list of algorithm challenges. Compilable, correct code beats clever-but-broken code every time. Below is how to approach each format, from a first-timer's checklist up to the traps that catch experienced coders under time pressure.

    Output Prediction Beginner

    You're shown a short piece of code and asked what it prints. No writing required — just tracing execution line by line, which makes this the fastest-scoring format if you're careful.

    Beginner move: physically trace variable values on scratch space as you read each line, rather than trying to hold state in your head — loops are where mental tracing breaks down fastest.

    int i = 0;
while (i < 3) {
  System.out.print(i + " ");
  i++;
}
    Trace it

    i=0 → prints "0", i becomes 1. i=1 → prints "1", i becomes 2. i=2 → prints "2", i becomes 3. Now i<3 is false, loop stops.

    Output: 0 1 2 (not 0 1 2 3 — the loop exits before printing 3).

    Intermediate move: pay close attention to whether a variable is incremented before or after it's used (i++ vs ++i) — this single difference changes output in almost every language.

    Advanced move: for nested loops, trace the outer loop one iteration at a time and fully resolve the inner loop's output before moving to the next outer iteration — trying to interleave both mentally is where mistakes happen.

    Debugging a Snippet Intermediate

    You're given code with a bug — syntax error, off-by-one error, or wrong logic — and must identify or fix it.

    Beginner move: check syntax first (missing semicolons, unmatched brackets, wrong variable types) — these are faster to spot than logic errors and are eliminated before you even need to think about behaviour.

    Intermediate move: for loop bugs, check the boundary conditions specifically — most bugs live in whether a loop should use < vs <=, or whether an index starts at 0 or 1.

    Advanced move: if the code "looks correct" but gives a wrong answer, mentally run it against a small edge case (empty input, single element, negative number) rather than the "normal" case shown — most subtle bugs only appear at edges.

    Write-a-Function Advanced

    You're given a problem statement and sample input/output, and must write a working function — usually something like reversing a string, checking a palindrome, finding a maximum, or basic array manipulation.

    Beginner move: restate the problem in your own words and identify inputs, outputs, and edge cases (empty input, one element, duplicates) before writing any code — this catches misunderstandings early, when they're cheap to fix.

    Intermediate move: favour a simple, clearly correct approach over a clever one you're not fully sure about — a working O(n²) solution scores; a broken "optimised" one doesn't.

    Advanced move: once your function works on the sample case, mentally test it against at least one edge case (empty array, single character, all identical elements) before submitting — CoCubes graders often run hidden test cases beyond the one shown.

    Domain / Specialist Test

    A branch-specific module (roughly 30 questions) drawn from your core engineering subjects — for CSE/IT this leans on data structures, DBMS and OS; for ECE it's electronics and signals; for Mechanical, Civil and others it follows the equivalent core curriculum. Companies hiring for a specific role often set a minimum bar on this section alone, separate from your overall score.

    Beginner move: revise from your own semester notes and one standard textbook per subject rather than scattered online sources — domain questions are usually textbook-level, not research-level.

    Intermediate move: prioritise your branch's two or three "core" subjects (for CSE: DS, DBMS, OS; for ECE: signals, electronic devices, digital circuits) since these are sampled most heavily, before spreading revision across every elective you've taken.

    Advanced move: practice previous years' domain-specific question sets if available — the phrasing style and depth tend to repeat within a branch even as exact questions change.

    What Trips People Up

    • Spending too long perfecting one coding problem and running out of time for the rest of the section.
    • Treating the domain test as an afterthought — some recruiters filter on it before looking at anything else.
    • Not knowing which language you'll be tested in ahead of time; confirm this before test day.
    • Ignoring compiler/runtime error messages instead of reading them — they usually point almost directly at the broken line.
    • Rewriting working code from scratch instead of debugging it incrementally when something goes wrong.

    Week 3Verbal ability and, if relevant, computer fundamentals / domain revision
    Week 4Full-length timed mocks, coding practice, and reviewing weak topics

    Daily Habits That Compound

    • Aim for around 20 practice problems a day with a timer running — speed under pressure is the actual skill being tested.
    • Keep a short log of mistakes by topic instead of re-solving problems you already get right.
    • Practice one full mock per week under real time limits, sections in order, no pausing.

    Test-Day Mindset

    Because there's no negative marking, attempting every question — even a rushed guess — is always better than leaving it blank. Never leave a section with unanswered questions if you have even a few seconds left.

    Sectional Thresholds

    Your overall score matters, but many recruiters filter on individual sections too. A company hiring for a support role might weight Written English and communication heavily; one hiring for engineering roles might care almost entirely about your Aptitude and Domain scores. Doing well everywhere keeps you eligible for the widest range of openings.
