    📂 Part 1: Waterfall Life Cycle

    Running a project as a planned sequence of approved phases.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What waterfall is and when to use it

    Waterfall is a plan-driven way to run a project. Work moves through phases in order: initiation, requirements, design, build, test, deploy and close. Each phase finishes and is approved before the next starts, so scope, cost and schedule are agreed early and then controlled.

    Waterfall suits projects where requirements are clear and stable, where late change is very expensive, or where a contract or regulator needs a fixed plan: construction, infrastructure, ERP roll-outs and many government projects. It is weaker where users cannot say what they want until they see it.

    TermMeaning
    PhaseA block of related work with a clear output
    DeliverableA product or document a phase must produce
    BaselineApproved version of scope, schedule or cost
    SponsorSenior person who owns the business case and funds the project
    Project charterDocument that formally starts the project
    Phase share of duration = Phase duration ÷ Total project duration × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: A 40-week project has requirements 6 weeks, design 8, build 16, test 7 and deploy 3. Build share = 16 ÷ 40 × 100 = 40 percent. Test share = 7 ÷ 40 × 100 = 17.5 percent.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using waterfall when the client cannot describe the requirements yet
    • Starting the build before the design is approved
    • Treating the plan as fixed and never re-baselining through formal change

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A 30-week project has 6 weeks of testing. What percent of the duration is testing?

    Show answer
    6 ÷ 30 × 100 = 20 percent.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Phases, deliverables and sign-offs

    Every phase has entry criteria, activities, deliverables and exit criteria. Requirements produce a signed requirements specification; design produces design documents; build produces tested components; test produces test reports; deploy produces a live system and handover documents.

    A deliverable is complete only when the named approver signs it off. The PM tracks deliverables in a register so that nothing moves to the next phase without approval and everyone can see what is waiting for sign-off.

    PhaseKey deliverableApprover
    InitiationCharter, business caseSponsor
    RequirementsRequirements specificationBusiness owner
    DesignSolution designDesign authority
    BuildBuilt and unit-tested productTechnical lead
    TestTest completion reportTest manager and business
    Deploy and closeHandover and closure reportSponsor
    Sign-off rate = Deliverables signed off ÷ Deliverables due × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: By the end of design, 18 deliverables are due and 15 are signed off. Sign-off rate = 15 ÷ 18 × 100 = 83.3 percent. The 3 open items are chased before the design gate.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Moving to the next phase with unsigned deliverables
    • Approvers not named in advance
    • Treating an email saying 'looks fine' as formal sign-off

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    24 deliverables are due and 21 are signed off. What is the sign-off rate?

    Show answer
    21 ÷ 24 × 100 = 87.5 percent.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Phase gates and the cost of late change

    A phase gate is a formal review at the end of each phase. The sponsor or board checks that deliverables are complete, the business case still holds and the next phase is planned, then decides go, hold, recycle or stop.

    The later a defect or change is found, the more it costs, because design, code, tests and documents must all change. A requirement error that costs $100 to fix in requirements can cost many times more after go-live. Gates with real quality reviews catch errors early.

    GateKey question
    Gate 1: InitiationIs the business case sound?
    Gate 2: Plan approvalIs the plan realistic?
    Gate 3: Design approvalDoes the design meet requirements?
    Gate 4: Go-liveIs it ready to deploy?
    Gate 5: CloseWere benefits and lessons captured?
    Cost of late change = Cost to fix in original phase × Phase multiplier
    🧪
    Practical Example: A design error costs $2,000 to fix in design. The multiplier is 5 in build and 15 after go-live. In build it costs 2,000 × 5 = $10,000; after go-live 2,000 × 15 = $30,000. A $3,000 design review is good value.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Holding gates as a formality where the answer is always go
    • Passing a gate with open critical defects and no plan
    • Not re-checking the business case at each gate

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A defect costs $500 to fix in requirements and the multiplier after release is 20. What does it cost after release?

    Show answer
    500 × 20 = $10,000.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: hybrid delivery inside a waterfall plan

    Many organizations now use hybrid delivery: waterfall for the overall plan, contract, budget and gates, with agile sprints inside the build phase. This keeps the sponsor's fixed milestones while letting the team adapt the detail.

    The expert keeps one integrated baseline. The build phase is planned as a number of sprints sized from the backlog and the team's velocity, and the sprint results feed the same earned value and milestone reports the board already uses.

    ElementWaterfall layerAgile layer
    PlanningPhases, gates, milestonesSprint backlog
    Scope controlChange control boardBacklog refinement within agreed scope
    ProgressMilestones and EVMVelocity and burndown
    ApprovalGate sign-offSprint review demo
    RiskRisk registerImpediment list
    Sprints needed = Build backlog story points ÷ Average velocity per sprint (round up)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The build backlog is 420 story points and the team's velocity is 48 points per two-week sprint. 420 ÷ 48 = 8.75, so 9 sprints, or 18 weeks. The PM sets the build phase to 18 weeks plus a 2-week hardening sprint before the test gate.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Running two separate plans that never reconcile
    • Letting the backlog grow beyond approved scope
    • Dropping gates on a high-risk project because 'we are agile now'

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A backlog is 300 points and velocity is 40 points per sprint. How many sprints are needed?

    Show answer
    300 ÷ 40 = 7.5, so 8 sprints.

    📂 Part 2: PMBOK Framework

    Using PMI's PMBOK Guide: process groups, knowledge areas, the charter and the plan.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Process groups and knowledge areas

    PMBOK, published by the Project Management Institute (PMI), is the most widely used guide to project practice. Its classic structure has five process groups, which describe the flow of a project, and ten knowledge areas, which describe what a PM must manage.

    The process groups are not phases: planning, executing and monitoring repeat inside every phase. One useful PMBOK idea is communication channels: each person added creates many new lines of communication, so communication effort grows much faster than team size.

    Process groupMain purpose
    InitiatingAuthorise the project and identify stakeholders
    PlanningSet scope, schedule, cost, quality, risk and communication plans
    ExecutingDo the work and manage the team
    Monitoring & ControllingMeasure progress and control changes
    ClosingAccept deliverables and record lessons
    Communication channels = n × (n − 1) ÷ 2
    🧪
    Practical Example: A team has 8 people: 8 × 7 ÷ 2 = 28 channels. Two stakeholders join, so n = 10 and channels = 45. Two people added 17 channels, so the PM updates the communication plan.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Thinking the process groups happen only once
    • Ignoring the procurement and stakeholder knowledge areas
    • Adding people without updating the communication plan

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A project has 12 stakeholders. How many communication channels are there?

    Show answer
    12 × 11 ÷ 2 = 66.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: The project charter and the business case

    The project charter formally authorises the project and gives the PM authority to use resources. It states the purpose, objectives, high-level scope, milestones, budget, key risks, sponsor and PM. Without a signed charter the PM has no formal authority.

    The charter rests on a business case that shows why the project is worth doing. Simple measures such as return on investment and payback period help the sponsor compare projects.

    Charter sectionExample
    PurposeReplace the legacy order system
    ObjectivesCut order processing time by 30 percent
    High-level budget$1.2 million
    Key milestonesDesign approved by June, go-live by December
    Sponsor and PMCOO; named PM
    Success criteriaBenefits measured 6 months after go-live
    Payback period = Initial investment ÷ Annual net benefit
    🧪
    Practical Example: A project costs $900,000 and saves $300,000 a year. Payback = 900,000 ÷ 300,000 = 3 years. The company limit is 4 years, so the charter can go forward.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Starting work before the charter is signed
    • Writing objectives that cannot be measured
    • No sponsor named, so nobody owns decisions

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A project costs $500,000 and saves $125,000 a year. What is the payback period?

    Show answer
    500,000 ÷ 125,000 = 4 years.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: The project management plan and baselines

    The project management plan explains how the project will be run. It includes subsidiary plans for scope, schedule, cost, quality, resources, communications, risk, procurement and stakeholders, plus the three baselines: scope, schedule and cost.

    Baselines are the approved reference for measuring performance. They change only through approved change requests. Comparing the forecast with the baseline shows the variance the board needs to see.

    Plan partAnswers the question
    Scope management planHow will scope be defined and controlled?
    Schedule baselineWhen will the work be done?
    Cost baselineWhat will it cost by month?
    Communications planWho needs what information, when and how?
    Risk management planHow will risks be found and managed?
    Baseline variance percent = (Forecast − Baseline) ÷ Baseline × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The cost baseline is $750,000 and the forecast is $795,000. Variance = 45,000 ÷ 750,000 × 100 = 6 percent over. The plan says over 5 percent is amber, so it is reported as amber with actions.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Writing a plan nobody reads
    • Changing baselines without approval
    • No thresholds for when a variance must be reported

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A schedule baseline is 40 weeks and the forecast is 44 weeks. What is the variance percent?

    Show answer
    4 ÷ 40 × 100 = 10 percent late.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: PMBOK principles, performance domains and change control

    Newer PMBOK editions add 12 principles, such as stewardship, value, systems thinking and tailoring, and 8 performance domains, such as stakeholders, team, planning and delivery. The expert uses them to tailor the process groups rather than following every process blindly.

    Integrated change control is where it all comes together. Every change is assessed against scope, schedule, cost, quality and risk at once, decided by the right authority and recorded. A healthy project approves, rejects or defers changes quickly and keeps the log current.

    Change log measureHealthy sign
    Average decision timeUnder 10 working days
    Approval rateMost changes justified and approved
    Rejected changesRecorded with reasons
    Baseline updatesDone after every approval
    CommunicationTeam told within 2 days
    Change approval rate = Changes approved ÷ Changes decided × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The CCB decided 32 changes this quarter: 22 approved, 7 rejected and 3 deferred. Approval rate = 22 ÷ 32 × 100 = 68.75 percent. Most rejections were for undefined benefits, so the PM adds a benefit field to the change form.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Approving changes without checking all baselines
    • Letting changes wait weeks for a decision
    • Treating PMBOK as a checklist instead of tailoring it

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    40 changes were decided and 30 approved. What is the approval rate?

    Show answer
    30 ÷ 40 × 100 = 75 percent.

    📂 Part 3: PRINCE2 Method

    Applying the PRINCE2 principles, themes, processes and tolerances.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: The 7 principles and continued business justification

    PRINCE2 (PRojects IN Controlled Environments) is a process-based method used widely in the UK, Europe, Australia and government work. It has 7 principles that must all be applied for a project to count as PRINCE2.

    The first principle, continued business justification, means the business case must stay valid for the whole project. If benefits fall or costs rise so that the project is no longer worth it, the board should stop it. Return on investment is a simple test.

    PrincipleIn practice
    Continued business justificationBusiness case checked at every stage
    Learn from experienceLessons log used from the start
    Defined roles and responsibilitiesClear Project Board and PM roles
    Manage by stagesPlan and approve one stage at a time
    Manage by exceptionTolerances delegate authority
    Focus on productsProduct descriptions with quality criteria
    Tailor to suit the projectMethod fits size and risk
    ROI = (Total benefits − Total costs) ÷ Total costs × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Benefits over 5 years are $2.6 million and total costs are $2.0 million. ROI = 600,000 ÷ 2,000,000 × 100 = 30 percent. At the next stage costs rise to $2.4 million, so ROI = 200,000 ÷ 2,400,000 × 100 = 8.3 percent, below the 10 percent hurdle; the board must decide whether to continue.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Writing a business case once and never checking it
    • Ignoring lessons from past projects
    • Calling a project PRINCE2 while skipping principles

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Benefits are $1.5 million and costs are $1.2 million. What is the ROI?

    Show answer
    300,000 ÷ 1,200,000 × 100 = 25 percent.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Roles, themes and the Project Board

    The Project Board has three roles: the Executive, who owns the business case and has the final say; the Senior User, who represents those who will use the product; and the Senior Supplier, who represents those who build it. The PM manages day to day; team managers deliver products.

    The 7 themes are business case, organization, quality, plans, risk, change and progress. Quality is driven by product descriptions with quality criteria, and products are approved against those criteria.

    ThemeKey output
    Business caseBusiness case and benefits management approach
    OrganizationProject management team structure
    QualityProduct descriptions and quality register
    PlansProject, stage and team plans
    RiskRisk register
    ChangeIssue register and change control
    ProgressHighlight, checkpoint and exception reports
    Product acceptance rate = Products passing quality review first time ÷ Products reviewed × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The quality register shows 40 products reviewed and 34 passed first time. Rate = 34 ÷ 40 × 100 = 85 percent. Most failures came from one supplier, so the Senior Supplier is asked to review their checks.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Making the PM the Executive
    • Leaving the Senior User role empty
    • Product descriptions with no quality criteria

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    25 products were reviewed and 22 passed first time. What is the acceptance rate?

    Show answer
    22 ÷ 25 × 100 = 88 percent.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: The 7 processes from start-up to closure

    The processes describe the flow: Starting up a project checks it is viable; Directing a project is the board's work throughout; Initiating a project builds the project initiation documentation (PID); Controlling a stage is the PM's day-to-day work; Managing product delivery is the team managers' work; Managing a stage boundary prepares the next stage; and Closing a project hands over and confirms acceptance.

    A project is split into management stages. Each stage is approved by the board based on a stage plan, which keeps planning detailed for the near term and lighter for later stages.

    ProcessMain output
    Starting upProject brief
    InitiatingProject initiation documentation (PID)
    Controlling a stageHighlight reports, work packages
    Managing product deliveryCompleted products
    Managing a stage boundaryEnd stage report, next stage plan
    ClosingEnd project report, lessons report
    Average stage length = Project duration ÷ Number of management stages
    🧪
    Practical Example: A 48-week project is split into an initiation stage and 3 delivery stages, so 4 stages. Average stage length = 48 ÷ 4 = 12 weeks, which fits the board's quarterly review cycle.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Skipping initiation and starting delivery straight away
    • Stages so long that the board loses control
    • Not preparing the next stage plan before the boundary

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A 36-week project has 6 management stages. What is the average stage length?

    Show answer
    36 ÷ 6 = 6 weeks.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: tolerances and management by exception

    The board sets tolerances for time, cost, scope, quality, risk and benefits. The PM acts freely inside them. If a forecast goes outside a tolerance, it is an exception: the PM sends an exception report and the board decides, often asking for an exception plan.

    The expert sets tolerances that match the risk, monitors forecasts, not just actuals, and escalates early. Good tolerances save the board's time and give the PM room to manage.

    ToleranceExample
    Time±2 weeks per stage
    Cost±10 percent of stage budget
    ScopeCould-have requirements may be dropped
    QualityResponse time 2 to 3 seconds
    RiskNo single risk over $100,000 impact
    BenefitsAt least 90 percent of planned benefits
    Tolerance limit = Stage budget × (1 + Tolerance percent)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A stage budget is $200,000 with +10 percent tolerance, so the limit is $220,000. The forecast is $226,000, which breaks the tolerance, so the PM raises an exception report with options to the board.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Escalating every small problem
    • Waiting until actual cost breaks tolerance instead of acting on the forecast
    • Setting no tolerances at all

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A 12-week stage has +2 weeks tolerance and the forecast is 15 weeks. Is this an exception?

    Show answer
    Limit = 14 weeks; 15 is above it, so yes.

    📂 Part 4: Project Governance & Control

    Reporting, change, stakeholders and closure across the life cycle.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Status reporting and RAG thresholds

    Status reports tell the sponsor and board where the project stands: progress against milestones, cost against budget, top risks and issues, decisions needed and next steps. They should be short, factual and the same format every time.

    RAG status (red, amber, green) is useful only with agreed thresholds. Without them, everything is reported green until it suddenly turns red.

    StatusExample thresholdAction
    GreenVariance under 5 percentInform
    AmberVariance 5 to 10 percentPM recovery plan
    RedVariance over 10 percent or tolerance brokenEscalate to sponsor or board
    BlueCompleteClose out
    Variance percent = (Actual or forecast − Plan) ÷ Plan × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: A milestone was planned for week 20 and is forecast for week 22. Variance = 2 ÷ 20 × 100 = 10 percent, which is amber at the top of the band. The PM reports a recovery plan and flags the risk of turning red.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reporting green because the PM hopes it will recover
    • Long reports with no decisions asked for
    • Changing the format every week

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Cost was planned at $80,000 and is forecast at $90,000. What is the variance percent and RAG status using the thresholds above?

    Show answer
    10,000 ÷ 80,000 × 100 = 12.5 percent, so red.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Integrated change control in practice

    Any change to an approved baseline goes through change control: log, analyse, recommend, decide, update and communicate. The change control board (CCB) or Project Board decides; small changes may be delegated to the PM within tolerance.

    The impact analysis shows the full effect. A change that adds two weeks may also add cost for every person on the team, so the PM always converts time impact into money.

    StepOutput
    LogChange ID and description
    AnalyseEffect on scope, time, cost, quality, risk
    RecommendPM recommendation with options
    DecideApprove, reject or defer
    UpdateNew baselines and plans
    CommunicateTeam and stakeholders told
    Change cost = Direct cost of change + (Delay in weeks × Weekly running cost)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A new report costs $12,000 to build and delays go-live by 2 weeks. The team costs $18,000 a week. Change cost = 12,000 + 2 × 18,000 = $48,000. The sponsor rejects it for this release and defers it to phase 2.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Pricing only the direct work and forgetting the delay
    • Approving changes verbally
    • Updating the plan but not telling the team

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A change costs $8,000 directly and adds 3 weeks at $10,000 a week. What is the full change cost?

    Show answer
    8,000 + 3 × 10,000 = $38,000.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Stakeholder and communication management

    Stakeholders are people who affect or are affected by the project. The PM identifies them, analyses their power and interest, and plans how to engage each one: manage closely, keep satisfied, keep informed or monitor.

    A communication plan sets what each group gets, how often and through which channel. An engagement assessment compares each stakeholder's current level (unaware, resistant, neutral, supportive, leading) with the desired level.

    Power / interestStrategyExample
    High / highManage closelySponsor, finance director
    High / lowKeep satisfiedIT security head
    Low / highKeep informedEnd users
    Low / lowMonitorOther departments
    Engagement gap = Desired engagement level − Current level (on a 1 to 5 scale)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The finance director is neutral (3) but must be supportive (4) for budget approval, so the gap is 1. The PM sets a monthly one-to-one and shares the cost forecast before each board meeting.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating all stakeholders the same
    • Ignoring quiet but powerful stakeholders
    • Sending the same long report to everyone

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A key user is resistant (2) and needs to be supportive (4). What is the engagement gap?

    Show answer
    4 − 2 = 2 levels.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    Requirements are the needs the project must meet. They come from interviews, workshops, focus groups, surveys, observation, document review and prototypes. Different stakeholders know different things, so every affected group must be heard.

    • Forgetting non-functional requirements

    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    Not every requirement can be delivered within the budget and time. MoSCoW sorts them into Must have, Should have, Could have and Won't have this time. Must haves are essential; without them the project fails.

    A common rule is that Must haves should be no more than about 60 percent of the effort, so the Should and Could items give room to absorb problems without missing the date.

    PriorityMeaningExample
    Must haveProject fails without itTake card payments
    Should haveImportant but has a workaroundSave card for next time
    Could haveNice to haveDark mode

    • Calling everything a Must have
    • Not recording Won't haves, so they come back
    • Prioritising without the business owner

    📂 Part 3: Scope Change Control

    Controlling every change to the approved scope.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Change requests and the change control board

    Change is normal, but it must be controlled. Every request is logged with an ID, analysed, then approved, rejected or deferred by the right authority. The change control board (CCB) usually includes the sponsor, business owner, technical lead and PM.

    The PM tracks how much the scope has grown through approved changes. Rising scope change shows weak requirements or unaligned stakeholders.

    FieldExample
    IDCR-021

    DescriptionAdd discount codes to checkout
    Impact+120 hours, +$9,600, +1 week

    • Accepting changes verbally
    • No ID or log, so changes are lost
    • Not updating the baselines after approval

    A baseline of 12,000 hours gets 1,500 hours of approved changes. What is the scope change percent?

    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Impact analysis

    Impact analysis shows the full effect of a change before anyone decides. It covers new work, rework of things already built, extra testing, schedule delay, cost, risk and quality. Using the RTM and WBS, the PM finds every item the change touches.

    Rework is often forgotten. Changing a finished design means updating documents, code and tests, so the real effort is new work plus rework.

    Impact areaQuestion
    ScopeWhich deliverables change?
    ScheduleIs the critical path affected?
    CostLabour, materials, licences?
    QualityDoes testing increase?
    RiskDoes it add new risks?
    BenefitsDoes it improve the business case?

    🧪
    Practical Example: Adding a field to an approved form needs 24 hours of new work, 40 hours of rework to screens and reports and 16 hours of extra testing. Effort = 24 + 40 + 16 = 80 hours. At $85 per hour that is $6,800.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Estimating only the new work
    • Analysing cost but not the critical path
    • Not checking whether the benefit is worth it

    A change needs 30 new hours, 50 rework hours and 20 test hours. What is the change effort?

    • Saying yes to small requests to keep people happy
    • Rewarding developers for unrequested extras
    • Not auditing delivered work against scope

    📂 Part 1: Cost Estimating

    Predicting what the project will spend, with an honest range.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Estimating methods: analogous and parametric

    A cost estimate predicts spend on labour, materials, equipment, vendors, travel and overheads. Analogous estimating copies the cost of a similar past project and adjusts it. It is fast and useful early, but only as good as the comparison.

    Parametric estimating multiplies a unit rate by a quantity, such as cost per square metre, per user or per interface. It is more accurate when good historical unit data exists.

    MethodHow it worksBest used
    AnalogousAdjust a similar past project's costVery early, little detail
    ParametricUnit rate × quantityGood unit data exists
    Bottom-upSum all work packagesWBS is complete
    Three-pointWeighted O, M, PHigh uncertainty
    Vendor quoteSupplier priceBought-in items
    Parametric estimate = Unit rate × Quantity
    🧪
    Practical Example: Past projects show $2,400 per system interface. The new project needs 14 interfaces. Estimate = 2,400 × 14 = $33,600.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using an analogous project of very different size
    • Applying old unit rates without inflation
    • Treating an early estimate as exact

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Office fit-out costs $650 per square metre for 300 square metres. What is the estimate?

    Show answer
    650 × 300 = $195,000.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Three-point estimating and PERT

    Three-point estimating uses optimistic (O), most likely (M) and pessimistic (P) values. The PERT formula weights the most likely value four times. It gives a more realistic estimate when there is uncertainty.

    The standard deviation shows the spread. A large spread means more uncertainty and more need for contingency.

    ValueMeaningExample
    Optimistic (O)Everything goes well$30,000
    Most likely (M)Normal conditions$40,000
    Pessimistic (P)Many problems$62,000
    PERT(O + 4M + P) ÷ 6$42,000
    Std deviation(P − O) ÷ 6$5,333
    PERT estimate = (O + 4M + P) ÷ 6 and Standard deviation = (P − O) ÷ 6
    🧪
    Practical Example: A data migration is $30,000 O, $40,000 M and $62,000 P. PERT = (30,000 + 160,000 + 62,000) ÷ 6 = $42,000. SD = 32,000 ÷ 6 = $5,333.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Giving only the most likely value
    • Pessimistic values that are not truly pessimistic
    • Adding PERT estimates and SDs without care

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    O = $10,000, M = $14,000, P = $24,000. What is the PERT estimate?

    Show answer

    📂 Part 3: Earned Value Management

    Measuring cost and schedule performance objectively.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: PV, EV and AC

    Earned value management (EVM) compares three numbers. Planned value (PV) is the budgeted cost of work scheduled to date. Earned value (EV) is the budgeted cost of work actually done. Actual cost (AC) is what was spent on that work.

    Budget at completion (BAC) is the total cost baseline. Percent complete is EV ÷ BAC, which measures real progress, not money spent.

    TermQuestion it answers
    PVHow much work should be done by now, in budget dollars?
    EVHow much work is done, in budget dollars?
    ACHow much did that work cost?
    BACWhat is the total budget?
    Percent completeEV ÷ BAC
    EV = Percent complete × BAC
    🧪
    Practical Example: BAC is $600,000 and 35 percent of the work is complete. EV = 0.35 × 600,000 = $210,000. PV at this date is $240,000 and AC is $250,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using money spent as progress
    • Mixing up PV and EV
    • Guessing percent complete without evidence

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    BAC is $400,000 and 40 percent of the work is done. What is EV?

    Show answer
    $160,000.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: CV, CPI, SV and SPI

    Cost variance (CV = EV − AC) and the cost performance index (CPI = EV ÷ AC) show cost efficiency. Schedule variance (SV = EV − PV) and the schedule performance index (SPI = EV ÷ PV) show schedule efficiency.

    Indexes above 1.0 are good; below 1.0 are bad. Variances are in dollars, so they show the size of the problem.

    MeasureFormulaGood when
    CVEV − ACPositive
    CPIEV ÷ ACAbove 1.0
    SVEV − PVPositive
    SPIEV ÷ PVAbove 1.0
    CV = EV − AC, CPI = EV ÷ AC, SV = EV − PV, SPI = EV ÷ PV
    🧪
    Practical Example: EV $210,000, AC $250,000, PV $240,000. CV = −$40,000; CPI = 0.84; SV = −$30,000; SPI = 0.875. The project is over budget and behind schedule.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reporting CV without CPI
    • Reading SPI as time in days
    • Ignoring an early CPI drop

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Show answer

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Measuring progress: 0/100, 50/50 and milestones

    EV depends on how progress is measured. The 0/100 rule gives credit only when a task is complete. The 50/50 rule gives half when it starts and half when it finishes. Weighted milestones give set percentages at defined points.

    Objective rules stop the '90 percent done' problem, where tasks stay nearly complete for weeks.

    RuleWhen to useExample
    0/100Short tasks under one periodCode review
    50/50Tasks over 1 to 2 periodsDesign document
    Weighted milestonesLong tasksBuild: 20/50/80/100 percent
    Units completeRepeated itemsLaptops deployed
    EV (50/50) = 50% × Budget of started tasks + 50% × Budget of finished tasks
    🧪
    Practical Example: Four tasks of $10,000 each: two finished, one started, one not started. Using 50/50, finished tasks earn $20,000 and the started task earns $5,000, so EV = $25,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Asking people how complete they feel
    • Using 0/100 on long tasks, hiding progress
    • Changing rules mid-project

    📂 Part 4: Forecasting & Cost Control

    Predicting final cost and acting to keep it on track.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Estimate at completion

    EVM lets the PM forecast the final cost. If current cost performance continues, EAC = BAC ÷ CPI. If a past overrun will not repeat, EAC = AC + (BAC − EV). If both cost and schedule performance will affect the rest, EAC = AC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI).

    The PM chooses the method that matches the cause of the variance and explains it to the sponsor.

    EAC methodFormulaUse when
    TypicalBAC ÷ CPICurrent performance continues
    AtypicalAC + (BAC − EV)One-off problem
    Cost and scheduleAC + (BAC − EV) ÷ (CPI × SPI)Schedule pressure affects cost
    Bottom-upAC + new ETCOriginal plan no longer valid
    EAC = BAC ÷ CPI
    🧪
    Practical Example: BAC $600,000 and CPI 0.84. EAC = 600,000 ÷ 0.84 = $714,286. Using the atypical method with AC $250,000 and EV $210,000: 250,000 + 390,000 = $640,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Always using the atypical method to look better
    • Not explaining the method
    • Forecasting only once a quarter

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    BAC $200,000 and CPI 0.80. What is EAC?

    Show answer
    $250,000.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: ETC, VAC and TCPI

    The estimate to complete (ETC) is the money still to spend: EAC − AC. Variance at completion (VAC) is BAC − EAC; negative means an overrun. The to-complete performance index (TCPI) is the efficiency needed on remaining work to finish on budget.

    If TCPI is much higher than current CPI, finishing on budget is unrealistic, and the sponsor needs a new budget or a scope decision.

    MeasureFormulaMeaning
    ETCEAC − ACMoney still needed
    VACBAC − EACExpected over or underrun
    TCPI (to BAC)(BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC)Efficiency needed for BAC
    TCPI (to EAC)(BAC − EV) ÷ (EAC − AC)Efficiency needed for new EAC
    ETC = EAC − AC, VAC = BAC − EAC, TCPI = (BAC − EV) ÷ (BAC − AC)
    🧪
    Practical Example: BAC $600,000, EV $210,000, AC $250,000, EAC $714,286. ETC = $464,286. VAC = −$114,286. TCPI = 390,000 ÷ 350,000 = 1.11, far above CPI 0.84, so the PM asks for a revised budget.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Promising recovery without checking TCPI
    • Reporting ETC without VAC
    • Forgetting committed costs

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What a WBS is and the 100 percent rule

    A work breakdown structure is a hierarchy that breaks the total project scope into smaller parts. Level 1 is the project, level 2 is the major deliverables or phases, and the lowest level is the work package, which can be estimated, scheduled, assigned and controlled.

    The 100 percent rule says the WBS includes all the work of the project and nothing else, and each level adds up to 100 percent of the level above. If work is not in the WBS, it is not in the project.

    • Building the WBS as a list of departments

    📂 Part 1: Activity Planning & Sequencing

    Turning work packages into linked activities with realistic durations.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Activities and durations

    The schedule turns work packages into activities, which are action steps such as 'write test scripts'. Each activity has an owner, an effort estimate and a duration. Effort is hours of work; duration is working days on the calendar.

    Duration depends on effort, the number of people and their productive hours per day. People rarely have 8 productive project hours a day because of meetings, email and other work.

    TermMeaning
    ActivityA step of work with a start and finish
    EffortTotal person-hours of work
    DurationWorking days from start to finish
    MilestoneZero-duration event
    CalendarWorking days, holidays and hours
    Duration = Effort hours ÷ (People assigned × Productive hours per day)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Test scripts need 240 hours. Three testers work 6 productive hours a day: 240 ÷ 18 = 13.3, so 14 working days.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Confusing effort with duration
    • Assuming 8 productive hours a day
    • Ignoring holidays in the calendar

    🧭 Core Concepts: Dependencies, leads and lags

    Dependencies show the order. Finish-to-start (FS) is most common: B starts after A finishes. Start-to-start (SS), finish-to-finish (FF) and start-to-finish (SF) are used less. A lag adds waiting time; a lead lets a successor start early.

    Dependencies can be mandatory (physical logic), discretionary (preferred practice) or external (outside the project).

    TypeMeaningExample
    FSB starts after A finishesTest after build
    SSB starts after A startsEditing starts after writing starts
    FFB finishes after A finishesFixes end before testing ends
    SFB finishes after A startsOld shift ends when new starts
    LagWaiting timeConcrete cures 3 days
    LeadOverlapStart design 5 days early
    Successor start = Predecessor finish + Lag (or − Lead)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Pouring finishes on day 10 and finishing work has an FS link with a 3-day lag: it starts on day 13. Design has a 5-day lead on requirements finishing on day 20, so design starts on day 15.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using lags instead of real activities for work
    • Hard date constraints instead of logic
    • Forgetting external dependencies

    📘 Fundamentals: The critical path

    The critical path is the longest path through the network. It sets the shortest possible project duration. A delay on a critical activity delays the project unless recovered.

    There can be more than one critical path, and the critical path can change as work progresses.

    ActivityDurationPredecessor

    🧪

    • Thinking the critical path is the hardest work
    • Not re-checking after changes
    • Ignoring a second near-critical path

    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Forward and backward passes

    The forward pass calculates early start (ES) and early finish (EF): EF = ES + duration, and a successor's ES is the largest EF of its predecessors. The backward pass calculates late finish (LF) and late start (LS): LS = LF − duration, and a predecessor's LF is the smallest LS of its successors.

    Scheduling tools do this automatically, but understanding it lets the PM check the results.

    ActivityESEFLSLF
    A (5)0505
    B (10)515515
    C (12)5171830
    D (15)15301530
    E (8)30383038
    EF = ES + Duration and LS = LF − Duration
    🧪
    Practical Example: Activity C starts at ES 5 and lasts 12 days, so EF = 17. E must start by day 30, so C's LF = 30 and LS = 30 − 12 = 18.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Taking the smallest EF on a forward pass merge
    • Arithmetic errors at merges
    • Not checking tool results

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Show answer

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Total float and free float

    Total float is how long an activity can slip without delaying the project: LS − ES. Free float is how long it can slip without delaying any successor: successor ES − activity EF.

    Critical activities have zero total float. Float belongs to the path, so using it on one activity reduces it for others on that path.

    Float typeFormulaMeaning
    Total floatLS − ESSlip before project end moves
    Free floatSuccessor ES − EFSlip before next activity moves
    Negative floatLS earlier than ESAlready late against a constraint
    Total float = LS − ES; Free float = Earliest successor ES − EF
    🧪
    Practical Example: From the table, C has total float 18 − 5 = 13 days. Its successor E starts at 30 and C finishes at 17, so free float = 13 days.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating float as spare time for anyone
    • Ignoring negative float
    • Using float without telling the team

    📂 Part 1: Risk Identification

    Finding risks early and recording them clearly.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Risks, issues, threats and opportunities

    A risk is an uncertain event that, if it happens, will affect project objectives. It can be a threat (negative) or an opportunity (positive). An issue is something happening now. A risk that occurs becomes an issue.

    Risk management is a cycle: plan, identify, analyse, plan responses, implement and monitor. It runs through the whole project, not only at kick-off.

    TermMeaning
    RiskUncertain future event
    IssueCurrent problem
    ThreatNegative risk
    OpportunityPositive risk
    Risk ownerPerson managing a risk
    Risk appetiteHow much risk the organization accepts
    Risk score = Probability rating × Impact rating
    🧪
    Practical Example: A vendor may deliver servers late: probability 4 and impact 4 out of 5. Score = 16 out of 25, red, so it gets an owner and a response this week.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Writing issues in the risk register
    • Identifying risks only at kick-off
    • Risks owned by 'the team'

    📂 Part 2: Risk Analysis

    Measuring risks so the biggest get attention and reserves are sized well.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Qualitative analysis: the probability–impact matrix

    Qualitative analysis rates each risk's probability and impact, often on 1 to 5 scales with defined meanings, then plots them on a matrix. High scores are red, medium amber and low green.

    Defined scales stop people rating the same risk differently.

    RatingProbabilityCost impact
    1Under 10 percentUnder $10,000
    210–30 percent$10,000–50,000
    330–50 percent$50,000–100,000
    450–70 percent$100,000–250,000
    5Over 70 percentOver $250,000
    Score = Probability rating × Impact rating (red ≥ 15, amber 8–14, green ≤ 7)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A risk with 40 percent probability (3) and $120,000 impact (4) scores 12, amber.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • No defined scales
    • Rating only cost and ignoring schedule impact
    • Never re-scoring

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Probability 60 percent and impact $300,000. Using the scales, what is the score?

    Show answer
    4 × 5 = 20, red.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Expected monetary value

    Expected monetary value (EMV) multiplies probability by impact in money. Threats are negative; opportunities are positive. Summing EMVs gives a basis for contingency.

    EMV is an average; any single risk either happens fully or not at all.

    RiskProbabilityImpactEMV
    Vendor delay30%−$80,000−$24,000
    Data rework40%−$50,000−$20,000
    Staff leave10%−$60,000−$6,000
    Licence discount25%+$20,000+$5,000
    Total−$45,000
    EMV = Probability × Impact
    🧪
    Practical Example: From the table, total EMV is −$45,000, so the PM proposes a $45,000 contingency.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Adding full impacts to the reserve
    • Forgetting opportunities
    • Guessing probabilities without input

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    20 percent chance of a $150,000 loss. EMV?

    Show answer
    −$30,000.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Decision trees

    A decision tree compares options with uncertain outcomes. Each branch's expected value is the cost of the option plus the EMV of what may happen. The best option has the lowest expected cost (or highest expected value).

    Decision trees make trade-offs visible to the sponsor.

    OptionCostRiskExpected cost
    Build in-house$200,00040% chance of $100,000 overrun$240,000
    Buy package$230,00010% chance of $50,000 extra$235,000
    Expected cost of option = Option cost + Sum of (Probability × Impact)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Build = 200,000 + 0.4 × 100,000 = $240,000. Buy = 230,000 + 0.1 × 50,000 = $235,000. Buying is cheaper on expected cost.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Comparing only base costs
    • Ignoring unlikely but huge impacts
    • No sensitivity check

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Option A costs $100,000 with a 50 percent chance of $40,000 extra. Expected cost?

    Show answer
    $120,000.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: Monte Carlo and contingency sizing

    Monte Carlo simulation runs the cost or schedule model thousands of times with random values from ranges and risks, producing a probability curve. P50 is the value with a 50 percent chance of not being exceeded; P80 has 80 percent.

    Many organizations fund projects at P80 cost, so contingency equals P80 minus the base estimate.

    ResultCost
    Base estimate$2.0 million
    P50$2.15 million
    P80$2.35 million
    P90$2.5 million
    Contingency = P-level cost − Base estimate
    🧪
    Practical Example: Funding at P80: contingency = 2.35 − 2.0 = $350,000. At P90 it would be $500,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating P50 as safe
    • Ranges that are too narrow
    • No correlation between related risks

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Base $800,000, P80 $940,000. Contingency at P80?

    Show answer
    $140,000.

    📂 Part 3: Risk Response & Monitoring

    Treating risks and watching them through the project.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: Threat responses

    For threats, the PM can avoid (change the plan to remove the risk), transfer (move impact to a third party), mitigate (reduce probability or impact) or accept (do nothing now, perhaps hold reserve). Escalate is used when it is outside the PM's authority.

    Every response costs something, so check that the reduction in EMV is more than the response cost.

    ResponseExample
    AvoidRemove an unproven tool
    TransferFixed-price contract or insurance
    MitigatePrototype early, extra testing
    AcceptHold contingency for a low risk
    EscalateProgramme-level risk to sponsor
    Response value = (EMV before − EMV after) − Response cost
    🧪
    Practical Example: A data risk EMV is −$20,000. A $6,000 cleansing sprint cuts it to −$5,000. Value = 15,000 − 6,000 = $9,000, so do it.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Responses that cost more than the risk
    • Transfer through a weak contract
    • Accepting high risks silently

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    EMV falls from −$40,000 to −$10,000 for a $12,000 response. Value?

    Show answer
    $18,000.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Opportunity responses

    Opportunities are risks with a positive effect, such as a vendor discount or finishing early. Responses are exploit (make it certain), share (partner with someone better placed), enhance (raise probability or impact) and accept.

    Opportunities are often forgotten, but they can offset threats in the contingency.

    ResponseExample
    ExploitAssign the best team to finish early
    ShareJoint venture with a partner
    EnhanceOrder early to get a volume discount
    AcceptTake it if it happens
    Opportunity EMV = Probability × Benefit
    🧪
    Practical Example: Ordering before 31 March gives a 30 percent chance of a $40,000 discount: EMV = $12,000. Placing the order early (enhance) raises probability to 80 percent: EMV = $32,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Only listing threats
    • Not assigning owners to opportunities
    • Ignoring opportunity EMV in contingency

    Show answer
    +$15,000.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Triggers, contingency plans and reserve tracking

    A trigger is a warning sign that a risk is about to happen, such as a missed vendor date. A contingency plan says what to do if it happens; a fallback plan is used if the contingency plan fails.

    Each draw on the contingency reserve is recorded against a risk. Tracking the remaining reserve against remaining exposure shows whether the reserve is still enough.

    ElementExample
    TriggerVendor misses order confirmation
    Contingency planSwitch to second supplier
    Fallback planLease temporary servers
    Reserve draw$18,000 for expedited shipping, R-07
    Remaining reserve = Contingency reserve − Approved draws
    🧪
    Practical Example: Contingency is $45,000 and draws total $18,000 + $9,000 = $27,000, leaving $18,000. Remaining EMV of open risks is $22,000, so the PM warns the sponsor.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • No triggers, so risks surprise everyone
    • Using reserve without a record
    • Not comparing reserve with remaining exposure

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: The issue log

    An issue log records current problems with an ID, description, priority, owner, action, due date and status. The PM resolves what is within authority and escalates the rest.

    Issue age, the days an issue has been open, shows whether decisions are stuck.

    FieldExample
    IDI-12
    DescriptionTest environment down for 2 days
    PriorityHigh

    DueFriday

    🧪

    • Issues with no owner or due date
    • Mixing issues and risks
    • Closing issues without checking the fix

    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Root cause analysis

    Fixing the symptom is not enough. Root cause analysis finds why the issue happened so it does not come back. Common tools are the 5 Whys, fishbone (Ishikawa) diagrams and Pareto charts.

    The recurrence rate shows whether fixes address root causes.

    ToolHow it works
    5 WhysAsk why until the root cause appears
    FishboneGroup causes: people, process, tools, environment
    ParetoFind the few causes behind most issues
    TimelineSequence of events
    Recurrence rate = Issues that reoccurred after closure ÷ Issues closed × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 40 closed issues, 6 came back: 15 percent. Four were environment failures, so the PM funds monitoring, the root cause.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Closing after a quick fix
    • Blaming people instead of process
    • No follow-up check

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: crisis management and communication

    A crisis is a severe issue that threatens objectives, safety, data or reputation: a failed go-live, a data breach, a vendor collapse. The expert acts fast: protect people and data, contain the damage, set one decision-maker, hold short decision meetings and communicate facts on a fixed rhythm.

    After the crisis, a review finds root causes and improvements. Time to restore service is a key measure.

    Crisis stepAction
    ProtectSafety, data and users first
    ContainStop the damage spreading; consider rollback
    CommandOne crisis lead; clear roles
    CommunicateFacts, next update time
    RecoverRestore service
    ReviewRoot cause and lessons
    Average time to restore = Sum of outage hours ÷ Number of incidents
    🧪
    Practical Example: Three go-live incidents took 4, 6 and 2 hours to restore: average 4 hours. The target is 2 hours, so rollback scripts are tested before the next release.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Blame during the crisis
    • Silence towards stakeholders
    • No review afterwards

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Projects handle sensitive information: business cases, budgets, contracts, vendor prices, personal data, system designs and risk registers that show where an organization is weak. They also approve spending and vendor payments. One weak control can cause a data leak, a contract dispute or payment fraud.

    Security here means protecting the organization and the people involved: keeping information confidential, controlling who can see and change plans and money, preventing fraud, keeping a clear record of decisions and following data protection, procurement and governance rules. Confirm the exact duties with your information security, finance and legal teams.

    🛡️ Security for: Traditional Waterfall Methodology (PMBOK, PRINCE2)

    Waterfall projects create many formal records: charters, business cases, plans, gate decisions, board papers and contracts. These contain commercial, financial and sometimes personal information, and they are the evidence auditors and courts rely on.

    Waterfall Life Cycle

    • Store phase deliverables and sign-offs in a controlled repository.
    • Use named approvals, not shared accounts.
    • Keep gate minutes with dates and attendees.

    PMBOK Framework

    • Restrict who can edit baselines in MS Project.
    • Keep the charter and plan under version control.
    • Share stakeholder registers only with the project team.

    PRINCE2 Method

    • Share board papers and exception reports only with board members.
    • Keep the issue and risk registers locked against deletion.
    • Record board decisions formally.

    Project Governance & Control

    • Check RAG reports for accuracy before sending.
    • Archive closure records for the required retention period.
    • Remove project access for leavers.

    🛡️ Security for: Scope Management

    Scope work handles requirements, designs, contracts and test data. Requirements often contain business rules and personal data, and change and acceptance records decide what the client pays.

    Scope Planning & Definition

    • Store the scope statement in a controlled library.
    • Share draft scope only with named stakeholders.
    • Record who signed and when.

    Requirements Management

    • Mask personal data in requirement examples.
    • Limit RTM edit rights.
    • Keep workshop notes in the project repository.

    Scope Change Control

    • Accept change requests only from authorised requesters.
    • Lock approved change log entries.
    • Keep supplier prices confidential.

    Scope Validation & Acceptance

    • Use masked data in UAT.
    • Keep an authorised signatory list for acceptance.
    • Store acceptance forms securely.

    🛡️ Security for: Budgeting & Cost Control

    Cost work handles rates, salaries, vendor prices, invoices, payments and forecasts. These are attractive targets for fraud and are commercially sensitive.

    Cost Estimating

    • Keep rate cards and salaries confidential.
    • Store estimates in controlled files.
    • Record the basis of estimate.

    Budget Development

    • Restrict who can change the cost baseline.
    • Log every reserve draw with approval.
    • Share funding plans only with finance and sponsor.

    Earned Value Management

    • Base EV on evidence, not claims.
    • Lock EVM reports after issue.
    • Audit progress evidence.

    Forecasting & Cost Control

    • Require two approvals for invoices and bank detail changes.
    • Match invoices to POs and acceptance.
    • Reconcile with the ledger monthly.

    🛡️ Security for: Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

    The WBS and its dictionary reveal the full shape of a project: its systems, sites, vendors and costs. For sensitive projects, such as security upgrades or acquisitions, this is confidential.

    WBS Fundamentals

    • Label sensitive WBS branches.
    • Share the WBS only with the team.
    • Store templates in a controlled library.

    Decomposition & Work Packages

    • Restrict who can add packages.
    • Approve control account budgets.
    • Track planning package changes.

    WBS Dictionary & Coding

    • Use the same codes in timesheets and finance to detect misbooking.
    • Close codes when work ends.
    • Audit code use.

    Responsibility Assignment (OBS & RACI)

    • Use role names in shared RACI charts.
    • Remove leavers from the RACI.
    • Keep vendor RACI with the contract.

    🛡️ Security for: Schedule & Resource Management

    Schedules reveal dates of launches, outages, moves and cut-overs. Resource plans contain names, rates and availability. Both can be sensitive and both can be manipulated to hide problems.

    Activity Planning & Sequencing

    • Restrict who edits the schedule logic.
    • Keep version history.
    • Share draft plans only internally.

    Critical Path & Float

    • Protect dates of sensitive events such as outages and cut-overs.
    • Share critical path reports with the project team only.
    • Store risk analysis securely.

    Gantt Charts & Schedule Control

    • Lock baselines with role-based access in MS Project Online or P6.
    • Keep an audit trail of updates.
    • Publish read-only views to stakeholders.

    Resource Management

    • Approve overtime before it is worked.
    • Match overtime claims to timesheets and access logs.
    • Limit personal data in resource sheets.

    🛡️ Security for: Risk & Issue Management

    Risk registers and issue logs record weaknesses: vendor problems, security gaps, failing systems and people risks. In the wrong hands they show exactly where an organization is vulnerable. Security incidents also have legal reporting duties.

    Risk Identification

    • Restrict access to security-related risks.
    • Avoid naming individuals in people risks.
    • Store the register in a controlled tool.

    Risk Analysis

    • Share EMV and contingency only with sponsor and finance.
    • Keep Monte Carlo models version-controlled.
    • Document assumptions.

    Risk Response & Monitoring

    • Require approval for every reserve draw.
    • Link draws to a risk ID.
    • Audit the reserve log.

    Issue & Crisis Management

    • Report security incidents through the official process at once.
    • Use approved spokespeople in a crisis.
    • Preserve evidence for reviews.

    🧪 Measuring security

    Traditional Waterfall Methodology (PMBOK, PRINCE2)

    Approval traceability = Decisions with named, dated approval ÷ Decisions sampled × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: An auditor samples 50 gate and change decisions; 47 have named, dated approvals. Traceability = 94 percent. The 3 gaps were verbal approvals, now banned.

    Scope Management

    Authorised acceptance rate = Acceptances signed by authorised signatories ÷ Acceptances checked × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: 40 acceptance forms are checked; 38 are signed by people on the signatory list. Rate = 95 percent. The 2 others are re-signed.

    Budgeting & Cost Control

    Invoice match rate = Invoices matched to accepted deliverables ÷ Invoices checked × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: Finance checks 120 invoices and 117 match accepted work: 97.5 percent. One mismatch bills a milestone never accepted.

    Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

    Timesheet code validity = Hours booked to open, valid WBS codes ÷ Total hours booked × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: Of 4,000 hours booked, 3,940 used valid open codes: 98.5 percent. 60 hours on a closed code are investigated.

    Schedule & Resource Management

    Overtime approval rate = Overtime hours approved in advance ÷ Overtime hours claimed × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: 500 overtime hours are claimed and 470 were approved in advance: 94 percent. The 30 unapproved hours are reviewed with timesheets and building access logs.

    Risk & Issue Management

    Incident reporting timeliness = Security incidents reported within required time ÷ Security incidents × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: Of 8 security-related issues, 7 were reported to the security team within 24 hours: 87.5 percent. The late one is reviewed and the issue log now flags security issues automatically.
