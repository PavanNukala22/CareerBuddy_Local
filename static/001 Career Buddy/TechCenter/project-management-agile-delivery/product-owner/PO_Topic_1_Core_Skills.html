    Module 01

    Product Owner — Core Skills

    Build the core craft of a Product Owner: set product vision and strategy, discover customer needs, define an MVP, manage and prioritize the backlog, and write clear user stories with Definition of Ready and Done.

    🎯
    Role focus: Product Vision & Strategy · Customer Voice & Discovery · MVP Definition · Backlog Management & Prioritization (MoSCoW, RICE, WSJF) · User Stories & Acceptance Criteria · Definition of Ready / Done.

    📘 Course overview

    The Product Owner (PO) is the single accountable person for the value of the product. The PO decides what the team builds and why, while the team decides how. Core skills turn a vague idea into a clear, ordered, testable list of work.

    🧭
    Learning path: vision → discovery → MVP → backlog → prioritization → stories → ready/done.
    🔭
    Vision
    Why the product exists and where it is going.
    🗣️
    Discover
    Learn real customer problems before building.
    ⚖️
    Prioritize
    Order work by value, effort and urgency.
    📝
    Define
    Write stories, criteria and quality gates the team can deliver.

    🎯 Learning objectives

    • Explain the PO role and how it differs from Scrum Master and Project Manager.
    • Write a product vision and connect it to strategy and measurable goals.
    • Run customer discovery and turn findings into problem statements.
    • Define an MVP as the smallest experiment that tests the riskiest assumption.
    • Manage a healthy product backlog and refine it continuously.
    • Prioritize with MoSCoW, RICE and WSJF and know when to use each.
    • Write INVEST user stories with testable acceptance criteria.
    • Apply Definition of Ready and Definition of Done consistently.

    🧩 Prerequisites

    No prior product job is needed. Basic familiarity with software projects and Agile vocabulary helps.

    Useful starting knowledgeWhat it means in practice
    Agile basicsSprints, backlog, team roles, iterative delivery.
    Business senseCustomers, revenue, cost and competition.
    Clear writingShort, unambiguous requirements.
    CuriosityAsking why before deciding what.

    🧱 Product ownership fundamentals

    A product is something that delivers value to users and the business. The PO maximizes that value by owning the product backlog and the decisions behind it.

    Project mindset

    Deliver a fixed scope on time and on budget.

    Success = plan followed.

    Product mindset

    Deliver outcomes that customers value and keep improving.

    Success = value created.

    📌
    Beginner rule: a feature shipped is an output. A customer problem solved is an outcome. Own outcomes.

    🧭 Core responsibilities

    ResponsibilityWhat the PO doesEvidence
    Vision & strategySets direction and goalsVision statement, goals, roadmap themes
    DiscoveryLearns customer problemsInterviews, insights, problem statements

    📌 Beginner checklist

    • Can I state who the customer is and what problem we solve?
    • Can I explain why this item is ranked above the next one?
    • Can I tell an output from an outcome?
    • Can the team start any top backlog item without questions?

    Module 02

    Product Vision

    Learn what a product vision is, how to write one and how to share it so everyone moves in the same direction.

    🔭 Vision · What is a product vision

    A vision is a short, inspiring statement of the future the product creates for its users. It is stable, simple and shared by the whole team.

    💡
    Vision template: For [target customer] who [need], our [product] is a [category] that [key benefit]. Unlike [alternative], we [differentiator].

    🎨 Vision · Vision board canvas

    A vision board is a one-page canvas that keeps the vision honest.

    BlockQuestion
    VisionWhat future do we create?
    Target groupWho is it for?
    NeedsWhat problem or job matters most?
    ProductWhat key features deliver value?
    ValueHow does the business benefit?

    🧭 Vision · Vision, strategy and roadmap

    LayerQuestion answeredChanges
    VisionWhere are we going and why?Rarely
    StrategyHow will we win there?Yearly or on market shift
    Goals / OKRsWhat results show progress?Quarterly
    RoadmapWhich themes and bets, in what order?Often

    📣 Vision · Communicating the vision

    • Put the vision in the first slide of every planning session.
    • Repeat it in plain words, not slogans.
    • Connect each sprint goal back to it.
    • Test understanding: ask team members to restate it.

    🧪 Vision · Practical example

    A budgeting app for students sets its vision as helping young adults feel in control of money. Strategy targets first-time earners. The goal is to raise 30-day retention from 25% to 40%. Roadmap themes: easy onboarding, spend alerts, savings goals.

    ⚠️ Vision · Common mistakes

    ⚠️
    Vision written as a feature list, goals with no numbers, and roadmaps treated as fixed promises. Fix: keep the vision timeless, make goals measurable and describe roadmap items as themes with outcomes.

    Module 03

    Product Strategy & Goals

    Turn the vision into a strategy with clear segments, differentiators, measurable goals and roadmap themes.

    🌍 Strategy · Market and segments

    Strategy starts with choosing where to play. Size the market, split it into segments and pick the one where your product can be clearly better.

    • Who are the target segments and which comes first?
    • What alternatives do they use today?
    • Why would they switch?

    🎯 Strategy · Building a strategy

    • Understand market, customer segments and competitors.
    • Choose a target segment and a clear value proposition.
    • Pick differentiators you can defend.
    • Set measurable goals such as retention, activation or revenue.
    • Name the key bets and the risks behind them.

    🏰 Strategy · Competitive positioning

    Positioning explains why customers should choose you. Compare yourself with alternatives on the few dimensions customers care about most.

    Feature parity

    Copy every competitor feature.

    Never ahead.

    Differentiation

    Be clearly better on one valued dimension.

    Easy to explain.

    📏 Goals · OKRs and measures

    Use OKRs: an Objective is qualitative and inspiring; Key Results are measurable outcomes.

    Weak goal

    Launch the new dashboard.

    Output only.

    Strong goal

    Increase weekly active users from 20% to 35% of accounts.

    Measurable outcome.

    🗺️ Roadmap · Themes and outcomes

    A strategic roadmap groups work into themes tied to goals, such as improve onboarding. Show outcomes and confidence rather than detailed dates.

    • Now: committed and well understood.
    • Next: planned, still shaping.
    • Later: ideas under consideration.

    🛠️ Strategy · Hands-on practice

    • Write a vision statement for a product you use daily.
    • Draft one objective with three key results.
    • List three roadmap themes and the goal each supports.

    Module 04

    Customer Voice & Discovery

    Gather real evidence from customers, find patterns and turn them into clear problem statements.

    🗣️ Discovery · Customer voice sources

    Customer voice is evidence from the people who use or could use the product. Combine several sources, never rely on one.

    InterviewsSurveysSupport ticketsUsage dataReviewsSales callsObservation

    🎤 Discovery · Interview technique

    DoAvoid
    Ask about past behavior and real storiesAsk would you use this feature
    Ask why and follow upPitch your solution
    Listen for pain and workaroundsLead with yes/no questions

    🧩 Discovery · From insight to problem

    • Cluster notes into themes.
    • Write a problem statement: user, need, obstacle, impact.
    • Use Jobs To Be Done: When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome].
    • Rank problems by frequency and pain.

    🌳 Discovery · Opportunity solution tree

    Place the desired outcome at the top, map customer opportunities beneath it, then solutions and experiments under each opportunity. It prevents jumping straight to a favorite solution.

    Outcome → Opportunities → Solutions → Experiments
    Opportunity solution tree

    📊 Discovery · Mixing numbers and stories

    Quantitative data shows what happens; interviews explain why. Use both before you decide.

    Module 05

    MVP Definition

    Define the smallest valuable experiment, test the riskiest assumption and decide using clear success metrics.

    🧪 MVP · Assumptions and experiments

    Every idea hides assumptions about desirability, viability and feasibility. List them and test the riskiest first with cheap experiments such as interviews, landing pages or prototypes.

    🚀 MVP · What an MVP really is

    An MVP is the smallest version that delivers value and teaches you something. It is a learning tool, not a low-quality product.

    Common mistake

    Build half of every feature.

    Little value, little learning.

    Correct MVP

    Build one complete path that solves the core problem.

    Real value and real data.

    Problem → Assumption → Smallest test → Measure → Learn → Decide
    MVP learning loop

    🧰 MVP · Types of MVP

    TypeHow it worksBest for
    Landing pageDescribe the product and measure sign-upsDemand
    ConciergeDeliver the service manuallyValue of the outcome
    Wizard of OzLooks automated, humans work behindWorkflow fit
    Single-featureBuild one core pathUsability and retention

    📐 MVP · Success metrics and decision rules

    Before building, write what result means go, pivot or stop. Example: at least 10% of visitors join the waitlist within two weeks.

    • Pick one primary metric.
    • Set the threshold before launch.
    • Set a time limit.

    🛠️ MVP · Hands-on practice

    • Write five interview questions about past behavior.
    • Convert one insight into a JTBD statement.
    • Define an MVP for a food-delivery idea with one success metric.

    ⚠️ MVP · Common mistakes

    ⚠️
    Asking leading questions, trusting only loud customers, and shipping an MVP with no success measure. Always define what result means continue, change or stop.

    Module 06

    Backlog Management

    Keep the product backlog ordered, refined, clean and ready for the team.

    📋 Backlog · What it is

    The backlog is an ordered list of everything needed to improve the product. The PO owns it. It is emergent: it changes as learning arrives.

    • Top items are small, clear and ready.
    • Lower items are larger and less detailed.
    • Every item has a reason to exist.

    🧱 Backlog · Structure: epics, stories, tasks

    Large items (epics) are split over time into stories small enough for one sprint. Tasks are the team's own how-to steps.

    LevelSizeOwner
    EpicWeeks to monthsPO
    StoryDaysPO with team
    TaskHoursTeam

    ✅ Backlog · The DEEP principle

    LetterMeaning
    Detailed appropriatelyTop items detailed, lower items rough
    EstimatedSized by the team
    EmergentChanges with learning
    PrioritizedOrdered by value

    🔁 Backlog · Refinement

    Refinement is ongoing work to clarify, split and estimate items. Spend a small share of team capacity on it each sprint. Remove stale items rather than hoarding them.

    📅 Backlog · Planning releases and sprints

    Use the ordered backlog and team capacity to propose a sprint goal. A sprint goal is a single outcome statement, not a list of tickets.

    🧹 Backlog · Common smells

    • Hundreds of old items nobody reads.
    • Duplicates and near duplicates.
    • Items with no clear user or value.
    • Everything marked high priority.

    Module 07

    Prioritization Frameworks

    Rank backlog items using MoSCoW, RICE and WSJF, and know the limits of each method.

    🧱 MoSCoW · Must, Should, Could, Won't

    CategoryMeaning
    Must haveProduct fails or is illegal without it
    Should haveImportant, but a workaround exists
    Could haveNice if time allows
    Will not haveAgreed out of scope for now
    📌
    Best for fixed-scope releases. Limit Musts to about 60% of capacity.

    📊 RICE · Score calculation

    RICE score = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort.

    FactorMeaningExample
    ReachUsers affected per period2,000
    ImpactEffect per user (0.25 to 3)2
    ConfidenceCertainty of estimates80%
    EffortPerson-months4

    Score = (2000 × 2 × 0.8) ÷ 4 = 800. Higher scores rank first.

    ⏱️ WSJF · Cost of Delay over size

    WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Size. Cost of Delay = user/business value + time criticality + risk reduction/opportunity enablement, each scored relatively. Short, valuable, urgent jobs go first.

    Use RICE when

    Comparing product features with measurable reach.

    Customer-facing ideas.

    Use WSJF when

    Sequencing work where timing and delay costs matter.

    Scaled or enterprise backlogs.

    🔀 Other lenses · Value vs effort and Kano

    A value-versus-effort grid finds quick wins fast. The Kano model sorts features into basic needs, performance and delighters, reminding you that delighters become expected over time.

    🧪 Prioritization · Worked example

    Item A: score 800, 4 months. Item B: score 300, 1 month. Raw RICE favors A, but a PO also checks dependencies and learning value, then explains the final order to stakeholders.

    ⚠️ Prioritization · Common mistakes

    ⚠️
    Treating scores as truth, scoring alone, and never re-scoring. Frameworks inform judgement; they do not replace it.

    Module 08

    User Stories & Acceptance Criteria

    Write small, valuable, testable stories and criteria that remove ambiguity.

    📝 Stories · Format

    As a [user], I want [goal], so that [benefit].

    💡
    Example: As a shopper, I want to save items to a wishlist so that I can buy them later.

    ✅ Stories · INVEST checklist

    LetterMeaning
    IndependentCan be built in any order
    NegotiableDetails open to discussion
    ValuableDelivers user or business value
    EstimableTeam can size it
    SmallFits inside a sprint
    TestablePass or fail is clear

    ⚖️ Stories · Good vs weak examples

    WeakBetter
    Build the login APIAs a returning user, I want to log in with my email so that I can see my orders.
    Improve searchAs a shopper, I want filters by price so that I find affordable items.

    🎯 Criteria · Given / When / Then

    Criteria define when a story is accepted. Use Given / When / Then or a rule list.

    • Given I am logged in, when I tap the heart icon, then the item appears in my wishlist.
    • Given the item is already saved, when I tap again, then it is removed.

    Cover the happy path, edge cases and errors.

    ✂️ Stories · Splitting

    • Split by workflow step.
    • Split by user role.
    • Split by data or rule variation.
    • Split happy path from edge cases.

    Never split by technical layer such as front end and back end alone.

    🔬 Stories · Spikes and non-functional needs

    A spike is a time-boxed investigation that answers a question. Capture non-functional needs such as performance or security as criteria or in the Definition of Done.

    Module 09

    Definition of Ready & Done

    Set shared entry and exit gates so work starts clearly and finishes with quality.

    🚦 DoR · Definition of Ready

    Entry gate: a story the team can start without blocking questions.

    • Clear value and user.
    • Acceptance criteria written.
    • Dependencies known.
    • Sized to fit one sprint.
    • Design or data available if needed.

    🏁 DoD · Definition of Done

    Exit gate: a shared quality bar for every increment.

    • Code reviewed and merged.
    • Tests passed.
    • Acceptance criteria met.
    • Documentation updated.
    • Deployable to production.

    Acceptance criteria

    Specific to one story.

    Defines this story.

    Definition of Done

    Applies to all stories.

    Defines quality.

    🛠️ DoD · Building your team's version

    • Draft it together with the team.
    • Keep it short and checkable.
    • Include quality, testing and release steps.
    • Review it each quarter.

    🔗 DoR and DoD · Acceptance vs completion

    A story is ready to start, built, accepted against its criteria and done against the DoD. The PO accepts; the team ensures done.

    🧪 DoR and DoD · End-to-end example

    The wishlist story is ready: value stated, criteria written, sized at 3 points. The team builds it, passes all tests and review, and the PO accepts it against the criteria in sprint review.

    ⚠️ DoR and DoD · Common mistakes

    ⚠️
    Stories that are tasks, vague criteria such as works well, and a DoD ignored under deadline pressure. Quality gates only work when they are consistent.

    Final Preparation

    Interview Preparation

    Prepare to explain vision, discovery, MVP, prioritization frameworks and story writing clearly with examples.

    🎤 Interview preparation

    Strong answers show a structured way of thinking and a real example, not just definitions.

    💡
    Answer principle: context → decision → framework → result → learning.

    💬 Common interview questions

    Role
    What does a Product Owner do and not do?
    Vision
    How do you create and communicate a product vision?
    Discovery
    How do you validate an idea before building it?
    MVP
    How would you define the MVP for a new app?
    Prioritization
    When would you choose RICE over MoSCoW or WSJF?
    Stories
    What makes a good user story and good acceptance criteria?

    🧠 Answer framework

    StepWhat to explain
    1. ContextProduct, user and goal.
    2. DecisionWhat you had to choose.
    3. MethodFramework or evidence used.
    4. ResultOutcome with a number if possible.
    5. LearningWhat you would improve.

    🎯 Interview practice

    💡
    Practice question: “Stakeholders say five features are all must-haves. How do you decide the order?”

    A strong answer clarifies goals, scores items with RICE or WSJF, checks dependencies, tests Must claims against real impact and explains the final order openly.
