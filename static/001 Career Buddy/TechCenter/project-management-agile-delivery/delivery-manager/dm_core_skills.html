    Module 01 · Basic · Foundations

    End-to-End Solution Delivery Foundations

    Understand the full delivery journey, the main methodologies and where the Delivery Manager fits.

    🚚 What is End-to-End Solution Delivery?

    End-to-end solution delivery means taking responsibility for an idea all the way from first discussion to a working, supported product in the hands of users. The Delivery Manager does not own one phase. They own the outcome: the right solution, on time, at the agreed quality and cost.

    🔍
    Discover
    Understand the problem, users and success measures.
    🗺️
    Plan
    Scope, estimate, sequence work and agree milestones.
    🛠️
    Build
    Engineering teams design and code in small increments.
    🧪
    Test
    Verify quality with automated and manual checks.
    🚀
    Release
    Deploy safely and tell users what changed.
    🔧
    Operate & improve
    Run the service, meet SLAs, learn and improve.
    💡
    Think of the Delivery Manager as the person who connects every hand-off. Most delays hide between stages, not inside them.

    🔄 Delivery Methodologies

    The methodology decides how work flows and how often value reaches users. A good Delivery Manager picks the approach that fits the product, the team and the risk, instead of following one method blindly.

    ApproachHow work flowsBest fit
    WaterfallPhases run once in sequence: requirements, design, build, test, releaseFixed scope, regulated or hardware-linked projects
    ScrumFixed-length sprints deliver a working increment with defined roles and ceremoniesEvolving product with a stable team
    KanbanContinuous flow with visible work-in-progress (WIP) limitsSupport, operations and unpredictable demand
    HybridUpfront planning for budget and milestones, agile execution inside itLarge programmes with fixed deadlines and changing detail
    Example

    A bank must meet a regulator deadline in six months, but the screens inside the project are still unclear. A hybrid approach fixes the deadline and compliance milestones upfront, and the team builds screens in two-week sprints.

    🧭 The Delivery Manager's Role

    The Delivery Manager sits between the business, product and engineering. They do not decide what to build (Product Owner) or manage people's careers (Engineering Manager). They make sure the work gets delivered.

    RoleMain focusTypical question
    Product OwnerValue and priority of the backlogWhat should we build next?
    Engineering ManagerPeople, skills and technical healthIs the team growing and healthy?
    Project ManagerScope, budget and schedule of a projectWill the project finish on time and budget?
    Delivery ManagerFlow, predictability, quality and release across teamsWill this reach users safely and on time?
    📝
    Titles differ between companies. In some firms the Delivery Manager also covers project management duties. Always agree on responsibilities in writing.

    📖 Key Terms

    DeliverableA tangible output that is handed over, such as a release, document or service.
    MilestoneA checkpoint that marks a significant piece of progress.
    IncrementA usable, tested piece of the product produced in an iteration.
    StakeholderAnyone affected by or influencing the delivery: users, sponsors, teams.
    DependencyWork or an approval that must finish before another item can start.
    Definition of DoneShared checklist that decides when work is truly complete.
    Go-liveThe moment a solution becomes available to real users.

    Module 02 · Basic · Planning & Governance

    Planning, Governance & Risk in Delivery

    Turn a goal into a realistic plan, manage risks and dependencies, and report status honestly.

    🗺️ Scope, Milestones and the Delivery Plan

    A delivery plan turns a goal into a sequence of milestones that teams can commit to. A good plan is visible, honest and easy to update.

    1. Agree the outcome and the measures of success with the sponsor.
    2. Break the scope into epics and milestones with clear exit criteria.
    3. Estimate with the engineers who will do the work, using ranges rather than a single date.
    4. Sequence the work around dependencies and team capacity.
    5. Review the plan every sprint and re-forecast when facts change.

    Forecast sprints = Remaining backlog ÷ Average velocity

    Example

    The remaining backlog is 120 story points and the team averages 30 points per sprint. 120 ÷ 30 = 4 sprints, so with two-week sprints the forecast is about 8 weeks. Add a buffer for risk and share it as a range such as 8 to 10 weeks.

    ⚠️ Dependencies, Risks and the RAID Log

    Delivery fails mostly because of unmanaged dependencies and risks. The RAID log is a simple living record of what could go wrong.

    LetterMeaningExample
    RRisk: something that might happenKey vendor API may be delayed
    AAssumption: believed true but unprovenTest environment will be ready by sprint 3
    IIssue: a risk that has already happenedThe payment partner missed its date
    DDependency: something we rely on others forSecurity review before go-live

    Risk exposure = Probability × Impact

    Example

    A risk has a 40% chance and would cost 10 days of delay. Exposure = 0.4 × 10 = 4 days. Compare exposures to decide which risks get a mitigation plan first.

    📣 Stakeholder Communication and Status Reporting

    Stakeholders want to know three things: where are we, what is at risk, what do you need from us. Use a fixed, short format so people read it every time.

    StatusMeaningExpected action
    🟢 GreenOn track against scope, date and qualityKeep informing
    🟡 AmberAt risk but recovery is within the team's controlShare the recovery plan
    🔴 RedOff track and needs a decision or helpEscalate with options and a clear ask
    ⚠️
    Do not hide amber status until it turns red. Early, honest bad news gives sponsors time to help. Late bad news destroys trust.

    ✅ Release Readiness and Definition of Done

    Before go-live, run a short release readiness review. It replaces opinion with evidence.

    CheckEvidence
    Scope completeAll agreed stories done or consciously deferred
    QualityNo open critical defects, tests passed, performance checked
    Operations readyMonitoring, runbook and support team briefed
    Rollback planTested way to return to the previous version
    CommunicationUsers and support teams know what is changing and when
    Example

    A launch looked ready, but the support team had not been trained. The Delivery Manager moved go-live by two days. This avoided a spike of unanswered tickets in week one.

    Module 03 · Intermediate · Resourcing

    Engineering Team Resource Allocation

    Plan capacity, map skills and choose an allocation model that keeps delivery predictable.

    👥 What Resource Allocation Means

    Engineering team resource allocation is deciding which people, skills, tools and environments work on which priorities, and when. The aim is to deliver the most valuable work without overloading anyone.

    🧑‍💻
    People
    Developers, testers, DevOps, designers and their availability.
    🎯
    Skills
    Matching the type of work to the right expertise.
    🖥️
    Environments
    Test, staging and tooling that teams share.
    💰
    Budget
    Cost of staff, contractors, licences and cloud.
    📅
    Time
    Sprints, release windows and fixed deadlines.
    🤝
    Partners
    Vendors and shared teams who support the work.
    💡
    Allocation is never one decision. It is a continuous balancing act as priorities, leave and attrition change every week.

    🧮 Capacity Planning

    Capacity is how much work a team can really take on. It is lower than the headcount suggests because of leave, meetings, support work and context switching.

    Capacity = (People × Working days − Leave) × Focus factor

    Example

    Eight engineers, a 10-day sprint, 6 leave days in total, and a focus factor of 0.8. Capacity = (8 × 10 − 6) × 0.8 = 74 × 0.8 = 59.2, so about 59 productive person-days.

    Capacity killerWhat to do
    Unplanned support and incidentsReserve a fixed share of capacity for run work
    Too many meetingsProtect focus time and review recurring meetings
    Context switchingLimit each person to one or two active items
    Planned leave and holidaysPlan with a shared team calendar

    🧩 Skill Mapping and Matching Work to People

    A skill matrix shows who can do what and at what level. It reveals gaps and single points of failure early.

    SkillAshaRaviMeenaRisk
    Payments APIExpertLearningNoneSingle expert
    Front end (React)GoodExpertGoodCovered
    CI/CD pipelinesNoneGoodNoneThin cover
    Performance testingNoneNoneGoodSingle expert

    The bus factor is how many people could leave before a skill is lost. A bus factor of one is a delivery risk. Fix it with pairing, cross-training and shared documentation.

    📝
    Aim for T-shaped engineers: deep in one area, working knowledge in neighbouring areas. This makes allocation flexible.

    🏗️ Allocation Models

    ModelHow it worksTrade-off
    Dedicated teamA stable team owns one product or serviceHigh focus and ownership, but can leave slack capacity
    Shared pool (matrix)People move between projects as neededEfficient use of rare skills, but more context switching
    Feature teamCross-functional team delivers a whole feature end to endFewer hand-offs, needs broad skills
    Component teamTeam owns one technical layer or serviceDeep expertise, but more dependencies between teams
    Example

    Only two engineers know the search engine. Rather than assign them full time to every project, the Delivery Manager gives one of them to each of two squads for half the sprint and schedules pairing sessions so a third person can learn.

    Module 04 · Intermediate · Resource Management under Pressure

    Allocation Under Pressure

    Keep delivery steady when demand spikes, people leave or priorities clash.

    🚦 Utilisation vs Flow

    It is tempting to keep everyone 100% busy. But a team at full utilisation has no room for the unexpected, so queues grow and every item waits longer.

    Very high utilisation (95%+)

    Long queues, slow reviews, little time for learning, rising burnout. Small surprises cause big delays.

    Healthy utilisation (about 70 to 85%)

    Slack absorbs urgent work, reviews happen quickly, and work flows steadily.

    Example

    A code review queue at 95% utilisation can take days. At 75% the same reviewer responds within hours. The team delivers more by being less busy.

    🔥 Handling Overload, Attrition and Urgent Requests

    Resource management under pressure is a core Delivery Manager skill. Use a calm, repeatable approach.

    1. Make the load visible: show demand versus capacity on one board.
    2. Protect WIP: finish started work before starting new work.
    3. Negotiate trade-offs: offer scope, date or extra people, never silent overtime.
    4. Swarm on blocked critical items so the whole team clears them together.
    5. Plan for attrition: keep knowledge shared and onboarding notes current.
    ⚠️
    Adding people to a late project often makes it later, because new members need onboarding time and increase communication paths.
    Urgent requestGood response
    "Add this feature before launch"Show the cost and offer to swap it for a lower-value item
    A key engineer resignsReassign priorities, run knowledge transfer, review the plan with sponsors
    Two teams need the same specialistUse the priority order agreed with the Product Owner and time-box each team's share

    ⚖️ Prioritisation Frameworks

    When capacity is short, a shared framework keeps choices fair and explainable.

    FrameworkIdea
    MoSCoWSort work into Must, Should, Could and Won't have
    RICEScore by Reach, Impact, Confidence and Effort
    WSJFWeighted Shortest Job First: do the work with the highest cost of delay per unit of effort first

    WSJF = Cost of delay ÷ Job size

    Example

    Item A has cost of delay 20 and size 5, giving WSJF 4. Item B has cost of delay 30 and size 15, giving WSJF 2. Item A goes first, even though B has the larger total value.

    🌱 Team Health and Sustainable Pace

    Allocation that burns people out is not allocation that works. Watch the signals and act early.

    • Rising unplanned overtime and weekend work.
    • Falling quality, more defects and more rework.
    • Quiet team members, skipped retrospectives, missed reviews.
    • Unfair on-call rotation where the same people carry the pager.
    💡
    Rotate on-call fairly, cap overtime, and use retrospectives to ask directly: is our pace sustainable?

    Module 05 · Intermediate · Service & Quality

    SLA & Operational Quality Management

    Define service commitments, set targets by severity and control quality with gates and defect management.

    📜 SLA, SLO, SLI and OLA

    A Service Level Agreement (SLA) is a commitment to a customer about the level of service, usually with consequences if it is missed. Delivery Managers use four related terms.

    TermMeaningExample
    SLAAgreement with the customer, often with penaltiesP1 incidents resolved within 4 hours, 95% of the time
    SLOInternal target set tighter than the SLAResolve P1 within 3 hours, 97% of the time
    SLIThe measured indicatorPercentage of P1 tickets resolved within target this month
    OLAInternal agreement between teams that supports the SLADatabase team responds to DevOps requests within 30 minutes
    💡
    Set the internal SLO tighter than the SLA. It gives an early warning before a customer commitment is breached.

    🏅 Defining Operational Quality

    Operational quality is how reliably and consistently the service does what users expect. It is more than "no bugs".

    🛡️
    Reliability
    The service is available and behaves as expected.
    ⚡
    Performance
    Pages and APIs respond fast enough under real load.
    🐞
    Defect control
    Few defects reach production, and they are fixed fast.
    🔁
    Repeatability
    Releases and fixes follow the same tested process every time.
    😊
    User satisfaction
    Customers are happy: CSAT and complaint trends.
    🔒
    Compliance
    Security and regulatory rules are met and evidenced.
    Example

    A support portal is "up" 99.9% of the time, but search takes 12 seconds. Availability looks fine, yet the operational quality is poor. Measure what the user actually feels.

    🎚️ Setting Targets and Priorities

    Targets must be tied to severity so the team knows what to drop and what to fix first.

    SeverityBusiness impactResponse targetResolution target
    P1 / SEV1Service down, many users affected15 minutes4 hours
    P2 / SEV2Major feature degraded30 minutes8 hours
    P3 / SEV3Minor issue with a workaround4 hours3 days
    P4 / SEV4Cosmetic or a question1 day10 days
    📝
    These numbers are examples. Always use the targets written in the actual contract.

    Priority is how urgently the team works it, and severity is how serious the impact is. A cosmetic error on the CEO's dashboard can be low severity but high priority.

    🚧 Quality Gates and Defect Management

    A quality gate is a pass or fail rule a change must meet before it moves forward, for example "no critical defects open and unit test coverage at least 80%".

    1. Log the defect with steps to reproduce, severity and affected version.
    2. Triage daily: confirm, assign an owner and set the target date.
    3. Fix, retest and add an automated test so it cannot return.
    4. Review escaped defects to learn which gate missed them.

    Defect escape rate = Defects found in production ÷ (Defects found before release + in production) × 100

    Example

    The team finds 54 defects before release and 6 after. Escape rate = 6 ÷ 60 × 100 = 10%. A target of 5% means the test approach needs improving.

    Module 06 · Advanced · Tracking & Improvement

    SLA Tracking, Quality Metrics & Improvement

    Measure service health, manage breaches, report honestly and improve continuously.

    📏 Core Service Metrics

    MetricWhat it tells youFormula
    SLA compliance %How often commitments are metTickets within SLA ÷ Total tickets × 100
    MTTAHow quickly someone acknowledges an alertTotal acknowledge time ÷ Incidents
    MTTRHow quickly service is restoredTotal repair time ÷ Incidents
    First-time fix rateIssues solved without reworkFixed on first contact ÷ Total issues × 100
    Availability %Share of time the service works(Total time − Downtime) ÷ Total time × 100
    Change failure rateHow risky releases areFailed changes ÷ Total changes × 100
    CSATCustomer happinessSatisfied responses ÷ Total responses × 100
    💡
    Pair every metric with a target and a trend. A single number without context cannot drive a decision.

    🧮 Worked Calculations

    Example 1

    SLA compliance: 190 of 200 tickets were resolved within target. 190 ÷ 200 × 100 = 95%.

    Example 2

    MTTR: six incidents took 300 minutes in total to restore. 300 ÷ 6 = 50 minutes.

    Example 3

    Availability: a 30-day month has 43,200 minutes. At a 99.9% target, allowed downtime = 43,200 × 0.001 = 43.2 minutes. At 99.5% it is 216 minutes (3.6 hours).

    Example 4

    Error budget used: the budget is 43.2 minutes and 30 minutes were lost. 30 ÷ 43.2 ≈ 69% of the budget is gone, so risky releases should slow down.

    🔬 Breach Management and Root Cause

    When a breach happens or is about to, respond in a fixed order.

    1. Warn early: alert at about 75% of the SLA time elapsed so there is still time to act.
    2. Escalate to the owner and, if needed, the incident commander.
    3. Communicate to the customer before they ask.
    4. Find the root cause with a blameless review.
    5. Fix the system, assign owners and dates, and track the actions to closure.

    5 Whys

    Ask "why" repeatedly until you reach a cause you can fix. Fast and good for single incidents.

    Pareto analysis

    Count causes and focus on the few that create most breaches (the 80/20 idea). Good for recurring patterns.

    Example

    Breaches were spread over many tickets. A Pareto chart showed that 60% came from one slow approval step. Fixing that one step lifted compliance from 88% to 94%.

    📊 Dashboards and Honest Reporting

    Reports are only useful when leaders trust them. Keep the cadence regular and the definitions fixed.

    AudienceCadenceContent
    Delivery teamDaily or per sprintOpen breaches, ageing tickets, blockers
    Delivery and product leadsWeeklyTrends, top causes, risks, actions
    Senior leadershipMonthlySLA compliance, availability, customer impact, decisions needed
    ⚠️
    Do not change metric definitions to make a month look better. If a definition must change, flag it and restate the earlier months.

    Use percentiles such as P90 or P95 for response and resolution times. An average can look healthy while the slowest 10% of customers wait for days.

    ♻️ Continuous Improvement

    Improvement is a loop, not a project. The PDCA cycle keeps it simple: Plan a change, Do it on a small scale, Check the result against the metric, and Act by adopting or adjusting it.

    • Run a monthly service review: top breaches, repeat incidents and open actions.
    • Limit improvement actions to a few at a time and give each one an owner and a date.
    • Celebrate the metric that moved, so the team sees that the effort is worth it.

    Module 07 · Advanced · Flow

    Bottleneck Identification

    Find where work waits, measure flow and remove the constraint that limits delivery.

    🧱 What a Bottleneck Is

    A bottleneck is the step in the delivery flow with the lowest capacity. The whole system can never deliver faster than that step. Improving anything else only moves work into a bigger queue.

    The Theory of Constraints gives five focusing steps:

    1. Identify the constraint.
    2. Exploit it: make sure it never idles or does low-value work.
    3. Subordinate everything else to the constraint's pace.
    4. Elevate it: add capacity or automate if needed.
    5. Repeat, because a new bottleneck appears elsewhere.
    💡
    Do not start with "who is slow?". Start with "where does work wait?" Bottlenecks are system problems, not people problems.

    ⏱️ Flow Metrics

    MetricMeaning
    Lead timeTime from request to delivery, as the customer sees it
    Cycle timeTime from work started to work finished
    ThroughputNumber of items finished per period
    WIPWork in progress: items started but not finished
    Flow efficiencyShare of lead time when work is actively being done

    Average cycle time = WIP ÷ Throughput (Little's Law)

    Example

    A team has 30 items in progress and finishes 10 items per week. Cycle time = 30 ÷ 10 = 3 weeks. To cut it to 1.5 weeks without extra capacity, reduce WIP to 15.

    Flow efficiency = Touch time ÷ Lead time × 100

    🔭 Finding Bottlenecks: Tools and Signals

    ToolWhat it shows
    Kanban boardColumns where cards pile up
    Cumulative flow diagram (CFD)Bands that widen over time show a growing queue
    Value stream mapTouch time versus waiting time for every step
    Ageing work in progressItems that have been stuck longest
    Cycle time scatterplotOutliers and the spread of delivery times
    SymptomLikely cause
    Cards pile up in "In Review"Not enough reviewers or large pull requests
    Work waits for "Ready for QA"Test environment or tester capacity limits
    Releases bunch up at month endManual deployment or approval batching
    Same person named on many itemsSingle-expert dependency

    🛠️ Common Delivery Bottlenecks and Fixes

    BottleneckFix
    Slow code reviewsSmaller pull requests, review rota, a target of first review within a few hours
    Shared test environmentCreate environments on demand with infrastructure as code
    Manual regression testingAutomate stable regression tests in the pipeline
    Manual deploymentsScript and automate the release with rollback
    Approval delaysDefine pre-approved low-risk changes and set response times for approvers
    Unclear requirementsRefine the backlog earlier with product, design and engineering together
    Single expertPair, document and cross-train to raise the bus factor
    ⚠️
    Do not "fix" a bottleneck by pushing more work into it. That only grows the queue and the stress.

    🧪 Worked Example

    A feature takes 20 days from request to release. A value stream map shows only 4 days of real work. The other 16 days are waiting: 6 for requirement clarification, 5 for code review, 3 for the test environment and 2 for approval.

    Flow efficiency = 4 ÷ 20 × 100 = 20%

    The biggest wait is requirements (6 days), then reviews (5 days). Improving the build speed from 1 day to 0.5 days would barely matter. Fixing refinement and review would cut about 8 days.

    Module 08 · Expert · CI/CD Alignment

    CI/CD Alignment for Delivery

    Align plans, quality rules and release promises with what the pipeline can deliver.

    🔗 What CI/CD Alignment Means

    CI/CD alignment means making sure plans, team structure, quality rules and release commitments match what the pipeline can really do. A Delivery Manager does not write the pipeline, but must know whether the promises made to the business are supported by it.

    Continuous Integration (CI)

    Developers merge small changes often. Each merge triggers an automated build and tests, giving fast feedback.

    Continuous Delivery / Deployment (CD)

    Every passing change is always ready to release (delivery), or released automatically (deployment).

    Example

    The business promises a weekly release, but the pipeline needs three days of manual regression and a manual deployment. The promise is not aligned. Either automate the steps or change the promise.

    ⚙️ The Pipeline from the Delivery Manager's View

    StageWhat happensWhat the Delivery Manager watches
    CommitCode is pushed and a pull request is raisedSize and age of pull requests
    Build and unit testThe system builds and runs fast testsBuild success rate and duration
    Quality gatesCode quality, security and coverage checksGate failures and how long they take to fix
    Integration and regression testsAPI and end-to-end checksFlaky tests and test duration
    Deploy to staging and productionAutomated releases with rollbackDeployment frequency and failures
    MonitorDashboards and alerts after releaseIncidents linked to releases
    💡
    A pipeline that takes 90 minutes to give feedback will not be used often. Aim for fast feedback on every commit, and run slower checks in parallel or less often.

    📅 Aligning Planning with the Pipeline

    • Release cadence: promise a release rhythm the pipeline can sustain, not the one that looks good on a slide.
    • Definition of Done: include "pipeline green, tests automated and monitoring in place".
    • Small batches: split work into small changes so each can pass the pipeline quickly.
    • Feature flags: deploy code in production switched off, then release the feature to users when the business is ready.
    • Trunk-based development: short-lived branches avoid painful merges and keep CI meaningful.
    • Environment plan: agree who owns test and staging environments and how conflicts are resolved.
    Example

    A large feature needs two sprints. The team ships it in six small pull requests behind a feature flag. Each change flows through CI/CD safely. On launch day the business simply turns the flag on, which is a one-minute release.

    🛡️ Governance without Friction

    Heavy approval boards slow delivery, but no control is risky. Mature teams automate evidence instead of adding meetings.

    Old wayAligned way
    Change board meets weekly for every changeStandard low-risk changes are pre-approved; only high-risk changes are reviewed
    Manual test sign-off documentsAutomated test reports attached to the release record
    Security review at the endSecurity scans run in the pipeline on every commit
    Release notes written by handNotes generated from linked tickets
    📝
    Keep auditors comfortable by showing that every change has a ticket, a review, passing tests and an approver on record. The pipeline can capture all of this automatically.

    📈 Measuring Alignment with DORA Metrics

    MetricQuestion it answers
    Deployment frequencyHow often do we release to production?
    Lead time for changesHow long from commit to production?
    Change failure rateWhat share of releases cause a failure?
    Time to restore serviceHow fast do we recover from a failure?

    Change failure rate = Failed deployments ÷ Total deployments × 100

    Example

    Over a quarter the team made 40 deployments and 6 needed a rollback or hotfix. CFR = 6 ÷ 40 × 100 = 15%. If the team deployed weekly before and now deploys daily, but CFR rises sharply, speed has outrun quality gates.

    💡
    Read the four metrics together. Speed without stability, or stability without speed, means the delivery system is not yet aligned.

    Module 09 · Practice

    Practice: Recovering a Late Portal Delivery

    Apply all five core skills to one realistic scenario.

    🏗️ Scenario

    A customer portal programme has three squads and 24 engineers. The release planned for week 12 is now three weeks late. Releases are manual and happen every four weeks, and manual regression takes four days. Pull requests wait an average of three days for review. Only one senior engineer understands the payments module, and he is allocated at 120% across two squads. Last month 267 of 300 support tickets met their SLA against a 95% target. Leadership wants a recovery plan and a monthly service report.

    0 / 0 correct

    1️⃣ Task 1 · End-to-End Delivery Plan

    ✍️ How will you re-plan the delivery and report status to the sponsor?
    Model answer: Re-forecast from current velocity and remaining backlog and share a date range, not a single date. Update the RAID log, name the dependencies and risks (payments expert, manual release), and report Amber with a recovery plan and a clear ask for decisions (scope trade-off, extra capacity or a moved date). Agree a release readiness checklist for the new date and give a short weekly update.

    2️⃣ Task 2 · Resource Allocation

    ✍️ Fix the allocation of the single payments expert and protect team capacity.
    Model answer: Cap the expert at 100% and stop splitting his time across two squads; give him to the squad with the most critical payments work and pair a second engineer with him to raise the bus factor. Document the module, rebalance the other squad's work, and keep utilisation around 70 to 85% so reviews and urgent work can flow. If scope exceeds capacity, negotiate trade-offs using WSJF rather than asking for overtime.

    3️⃣ Task 3 · SLA and Quality

    ✍️ Explain what the 89% compliance means and how you will recover it.
    Model answer: 267 ÷ 300 = 89%, which is 6 points below the 95% target (285 tickets). Set an early-warning alert at 75% of SLA time, analyse breaches with a Pareto chart to find the few causes behind most of them, run a blameless review, and fix the top cause first. Add quality gates and an automated test for each escaped defect, and report compliance, MTTR and defect escape rate monthly with fixed definitions.

    4️⃣ Task 4 · Bottleneck Identification

    ✍️ Which bottlenecks would you look at first, and how would you confirm them?
    Model answer: Check the board and a cumulative flow diagram for queues in "In Review" and "Ready for QA". The three-day review wait and four-day manual regression are likely constraints. Map the value stream to compare touch time with waiting time. Fix review first with smaller pull requests and a review rota, then automate regression. Reduce WIP so each item finishes faster, and re-measure cycle time after each change.

    5️⃣ Task 5 · CI/CD Alignment

    ✍️ How do you align the release plan with the pipeline?
    Model answer: Do not promise more than the pipeline can do. Put build, unit and API tests in CI on every commit, automate regression, and script deployments with rollback. Use feature flags to separate deployment from release, move to small frequent releases, and pre-approve low-risk changes. Track DORA metrics (deployment frequency, lead time, change failure rate, time to restore) to show progress to leadership.
