    Module 01 · Basic · Mindset

    Pragmatism Foundations

    Pragmatism means choosing the approach that works within real constraints and judging success by outcomes, not by perfection on paper.

    🧭 What Pragmatism Means in Delivery

    A pragmatic Delivery Manager asks, "What is the simplest approach that achieves the outcome, given what we have?" Plans are tools for getting results, not goals in themselves.

    🎯
    Outcome first
    Judge decisions by the result for the customer and the business.
    ✂️
    Good enough, on purpose
    Define "good enough" up front instead of chasing perfection.
    ⚖️
    Trade-off aware
    Every choice gives something up; say what.
    📊
    Evidence over opinion
    Use data and small experiments to settle debates.
    🪜
    Small, reversible steps
    Prefer steps that are cheap to undo and quick to learn from.
    🗣️
    Honest about limits
    State constraints early rather than over-promising.

    🔍 Pragmatic, Perfectionist or Shortcut-Taker?

    ApproachBehaviourTypical result
    PerfectionistWaits for the ideal solution; keeps polishingLate delivery, missed learning
    PragmaticChooses fit-for-purpose option; records the trade-offsPredictable value, managed risk
    Shortcut-takerSkips quality or checks silentlyHidden debt, rework, incidents
    🚨
    Pragmatism is not cutting corners. A conscious shortcut is acceptable only when it is visible, agreed with stakeholders and has a plan to pay it back.

    🔺 Trade-offs: Scope, Time, Cost and Quality

    Most delivery pressure is a clash between these levers. If all of them are fixed, quality usually gives way quietly. A pragmatic manager makes the choice explicit.

    If this is fixedFlex thisTypical move
    DeadlineScopeDeliver the must-haves, defer the rest
    ScopeTime or costMove the date or add capacity early
    Budget and deadlineScope (never hidden quality)Reduce features, keep the Definition of Done
    Example

    The deadline is in 2 weeks and 10 stories are planned, but capacity fits 7. Deliver the 7 highest-value stories at full quality and schedule the other 3 for the next release, rather than rushing all 10.

    🛠️ Practical Decision Tools

    Pareto (80/20)Roughly 80% of the effect comes from 20% of the causes; start with the vital few.
    MoSCoWMust, Should, Could, Won't (this time): a quick way to rank scope.
    Two-way doorA reversible decision: decide fast, adjust later. One-way doors deserve more care.
    MVPSmallest version that delivers value and teaches you something.
    TimeboxFixed time for an activity; decide with what you have learned when it ends.
    Definition of DoneAgreed quality bar that is not traded away under pressure.
    💡
    When stuck, ask: what is the smallest step that delivers value and teaches us something?

    Module 02 · Basic · Application

    Pragmatism in Practice

    Apply pragmatism to everyday delivery: communicate trade-offs, manage technical debt and know the non-negotiables.

    💼 Pragmatism in Everyday Delivery Situations

    SituationPerfectionist reactionPragmatic move
    Requirements are unclear two weeks before releaseWait until everything is definedConfirm the must-haves, build the clear part, timebox discovery for the rest
    Team debates two similar tools for daysKeep researchingSet a decision deadline and criteria; pick the reversible option
    Release night finds a minor cosmetic bugDelay the releaseLog it, release, fix in the next iteration
    Long document requested for a small changeWrite everythingWrite what the reader needs to act, no more
    A dependency team cannot deliver on timeBlame and waitAgree an interim workaround and a firm hand-over date

    🗣️ Communicating Trade-offs to Stakeholders

    Pragmatic decisions only work if stakeholders understand them. Present options, not problems, and keep it short.

    Situation → Options with impact → Recommendation → Decision needed by when

    Example

    "We have capacity for 7 of the 10 stories before the 15th. Option 1: ship 7 on time at full quality. Option 2: ship all 10 on the 22nd. Option 3: ship 10 on time with reduced testing (not recommended: higher defect risk). I recommend option 1 and a follow-up release for the remaining 3. I need your decision by Friday."

    🏦 Technical Debt and Risk Decisions

    Technical debt is work postponed that will cost more later. It can be a deliberate choice ("ship now, refactor next iteration") or inadvertent (poor design discovered later). Deliberate debt is a pragmatic tool only when recorded and repaid.

    Risk responseMeaningExample
    AvoidChange the plan to remove the riskDrop a risky feature from this release
    MitigateReduce probability or impactAdd automated tests around a fragile module
    TransferMove the risk to someone elseInsurance, vendor contract terms
    AcceptKnowingly live with itMinor issue with a cheap workaround
    💡
    Keep a visible debt register and reserve a share of each iteration (many teams use 10–20%) to repay it.

    ⛔ Where Pragmatism Stops: Non-Negotiables

    "Good enough" must never cross certain lines. Agree these before pressure arrives.

    🔐
    Security
    No known critical vulnerabilities shipped.
    ⚖️
    Compliance and legal
    Regulatory and contractual obligations are met.
    🧪
    Core quality gates
    Critical tests pass; Definition of Done respected.
    💾
    Data safety
    No risk of customer data loss or corruption.
    🧑‍🤝‍🧑
    People safety
    Sustainable pace; no hidden burnout cost.
    🤝
    Honesty
    Status and risks reported accurately.

    Module 03 · Intermediate · Value

    Customer Value Foundations

    Delivery is judged by the value customers receive. Define value, know your customers and stakeholders, listen, and understand the types of value.

    💎 Defining Customer Value

    Customer value is the benefit a customer gets relative to the cost, effort and risk of getting it. Busy teams can ship a lot and still deliver little value, so separate outputs from outcomes.

    Output (what we produced)

    Features shipped, tickets closed, story points completed.

    Outcome (what changed for the customer)

    Checkout time cut by 30%, fewer complaints, higher renewal rate.

    🗺️ Knowing Your Customers and Stakeholders

    Customers can be external (people who buy or use the product) or internal (teams that depend on your output). Map stakeholders by power and interest to decide how to engage them.

    GroupEngagement approach
    High power, high interestManage closely: regular detailed updates, involve in decisions
    High power, low interestKeep satisfied: short, relevant updates
    Low power, high interestKeep informed: demos, release notes, open channels
    Low power, low interestMonitor: light-touch communication

    Personas (short profiles of typical users: goals, frustrations, context) keep discussions anchored on real people instead of opinions.

    👂 Listening to the Customer

    SourceWhat it tells you
    Customer interviews and demosReal needs, context and priorities
    Support tickets and incidentsWhere the product hurts today
    CSATSatisfaction with a specific interaction or release
    NPSLoyalty: likelihood to recommend
    CESHow easy it was for the customer to get something done
    Usage analyticsWhat customers actually do, not what they say

    NPS = % Promoters (score 9–10) − % Detractors (score 0–6)

    CSAT = satisfied responses ÷ total responses × 100

    ⭐ Kano Model: Types of Value

    TypeMeaningExample
    Basic (must-be)Expected; absence causes anger, presence earns little praiseThe app does not crash
    PerformanceMore is better; satisfaction rises with qualityFaster page load
    DelighterUnexpected; creates enthusiasmOne-click reorder suggestion
    📝
    Over time delighters become basics. Protect basic quality first, then invest in performance and delight.

    Module 04 · Intermediate · Delivering Value

    Prioritising & Delivering Value

    Rank work by value, protect value in delivery practices, remove waste from the value stream and handle difficult customer conversations.

    📈 Prioritising by Value

    Rank work by value delivered per unit of effort. WSJF divides Cost of Delay (value + time criticality + risk reduction) by job size so that small, valuable, urgent items go first.

    WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Size

    ItemCost of DelayJob sizeWSJF
    A1682.0
    B1234.0
    C991.0
    Example

    Item B scores 4.0, so it goes first even though A has a higher Cost of Delay: smaller jobs with solid value beat big ones.

    🚚 Delivery Practices that Protect Value

    PracticeWhy it protects value
    Small, frequent releasesCustomers get value sooner; mistakes are cheaper to fix.
    Regular demos and feedbackConfirms the team is building the right thing.
    Acceptance criteria in DoneWork counts only when the customer need is met.
    SLAs tied to customer expectationsTargets reflect what matters to the customer.
    Saying "not now" with dataKeeps focus on the highest value, with reasons.
    Closing the feedback loopTell customers what was done with their input.
    📝
    A request is not automatically a requirement. Ask "what problem are you trying to solve?" before agreeing to a solution.

    🌊 Value Streams and Waste

    A value stream is every step from customer request to delivered value. Mapping it shows where time is lost waiting rather than working.

    Flow efficiency = Active work time ÷ Total elapsed time × 100

    Example

    A request takes 10 days from raise to delivery, but only 2 days involve real work. Flow efficiency = 2 ÷ 10 = 20%. The other 8 days are waiting (approvals, hand-offs, queues): that is where improvement is cheapest.

    Lean wasteDelivery example
    WaitingWork idle in an approval queue
    HandoffsKnowledge lost between teams
    Defects and reworkBugs found late in testing
    Over-processingReports no one reads
    Unused featuresBuilt but never adopted
    Task switchingPeople split across many tasks

    🤝 Handling Difficult Customer Conversations

    Escalations are signals of unmet expectations. Follow a calm, repeatable sequence.

    StepWhat to do
    ListenLet them finish; take notes; do not defend
    AcknowledgeShow you understand the impact on them
    ClarifyAsk what outcome they need and by when
    Offer optionsGive realistic choices with trade-offs
    CommitAgree an owner, action and date you can keep
    Follow upReport back even if there is no news

    Do

    Be factual, own the next step, keep promises small and dated.

    Avoid

    Blaming other teams, over-promising, going silent.

    Module 05 · Advanced · Capacity

    Capacity & Planning

    Size capacity honestly, estimate under uncertainty, keep utilisation healthy and prioritise competing demands.

    📦 Capacity versus Demand

    Start every plan with honest capacity: not headcount, but effective person-days after leave, meetings and support work.

    Capacity = People × Available days × Focus factor

    Example

    8 engineers × 9 available days × 0.7 focus factor = 50.4 person-days. If the planned work is 62 person-days, demand is about 23% above capacity (a gap of 11.6 person-days). Plan to close that gap with scope, date or support, not with hope.

    🔮 Estimating and Forecasting under Uncertainty

    An estimate is a best guess with uncertainty; a commitment is a promise. Early estimates are wide (the cone of uncertainty) and narrow as you learn. Use historical data (throughput, cycle time) rather than gut feel.

    Three-point estimate = (Optimistic + 4 × Most likely + Pessimistic) ÷ 6

    Example

    Optimistic 4 days, most likely 6, pessimistic 14: (4 + 24 + 14) ÷ 6 = 7 days. Notice the result is higher than "most likely" because the downside risk is larger.

    💡
    Give ranges and confidence ("7 to 10 days, 80% confident") instead of a single date. It builds trust and exposes risk.

    🎚️ Allocation, Utilisation and Slack

    Utilisation (rough guide)Effect
    Below about 60%Idle capacity; check for blocked or missing work
    About 70–85%Healthy flow with room for incidents and learning
    Above about 90%Queues grow, delays increase, quality and morale suffer
    100% plannedNo slack at all; any surprise causes a miss
    🚨
    Splitting one person across many projects looks efficient on paper but context switching reduces real output. Prefer few, stable assignments.

    🧮 Prioritising Multiple Demands

    QuadrantMeaningAction
    Urgent and importantProduction incident, customer escalationDo now
    Important, not urgentAutomation, hiring, planningSchedule protected time
    Urgent, not importantMany interruptions and requestsDelegate or batch
    NeitherLow-value tasksDrop

    When demands still collide, rank by cost of delay and customer impact, and show the ranking openly so people understand why something waits.

    Module 06 · Advanced · People & Pressure

    People & Pressure

    Handle crunch periods: triage and re-plan, manage bottlenecks and skills, delegate with clear ownership, protect people and raise risk early.

    🚑 Handling Pressure: Triage and Re-plan

    StepAction
    StabiliseStop adding new work; limit work in progress.
    TriageRank remaining work by customer value and deadline.
    Re-planNegotiate scope, date or resources with stakeholders, with options.
    Protect the teamNo unplanned heroics; protect focus time and rest.
    CommunicateShare status and risks early and honestly.
    RecoverHold a retrospective; rebuild buffer and fix root causes.
    📝
    Brooks's law: adding people to a late project usually makes it later, because onboarding and coordination cost time. Use extra people early, or for well-separated tasks.

    🧩 Bottlenecks, Skills and Bus Factor

    The Theory of Constraints says output is limited by the scarcest resource. Identify the constraint, make full use of it, align everything else to it, then elevate it (add capacity) and repeat.

    BottleneckStep or person whose limited capacity slows the whole flow.
    Skills matrixGrid of who can do what, used to plan cross-training.
    Bus factorHow many people could leave before the work stalls; low is risky.
    Cross-trainingSpreading knowledge so work does not wait for one person.
    BufferPlanned slack that absorbs variability; protect it.
    WIP limitCap on work in progress to keep flow healthy.

    🤲 Delegation and Clear Ownership

    Under pressure, managers often hold on to work. Effective delegation gives outcome, authority, constraints and a check-in point, not step-by-step instructions.

    RACI roleMeaning
    ResponsibleDoes the work
    AccountableOwns the result; one person only
    ConsultedGives input before decisions
    InformedTold after decisions
    🚨
    If two people are both "accountable", nobody is. Name exactly one owner for every deliverable.

    🛡️ Protecting People

    Sustained pressure damages quality and retention. Watch for early warning signs: rising defects, missed commitments, long hours, withdrawal in meetings, more sick days.

    Healthy response

    Cut scope, rotate on-call, grant time off after a crunch, delegate with clear ownership.

    Unhealthy response

    Mandatory overtime, hiding the gap, rewarding heroics, ignoring warning signs.

    📣 Communicating Resource Risk Upward

    Raise resource problems early, with data and options. Leaders can help only if they know what you need and by when.

    Example

    "Capacity for the release is 63 person-days against 80 needed. Options: reduce scope by 17 person-days, delay by one week, or add one contractor now for the integration work. I recommend reducing scope and need your decision by Wednesday."

    DoAvoid
    Quantify the gapSaying "we are very busy"
    Offer 2–3 options with impactBringing only the problem
    State the decision and deadlineLeaving the ask vague

    Module 07 · Advanced · Structured Thinking

    Problem-Solving Foundations

    Turn messy problems into clear definitions: a structured approach, the right framework, root-cause techniques and evidence.

    🧠 A Structured Approach

    Under pressure people jump to solutions. A repeatable approach avoids solving the wrong problem.

    Define → Understand with data → Find root cause → Generate options → Decide → Act → Review

    StepKey question
    DefineWhat exactly is wrong, for whom, and since when?
    UnderstandWhat do the facts and numbers say?
    Root causeWhy is it really happening?
    OptionsWhat are at least 2–3 ways to fix it?
    DecideWhich option best fits the criteria and constraints?
    Act and reviewDid the fix work, measured against the original problem?
    💡
    PDCA (Plan, Do, Check, Act) is the same idea as a loop: try a change, measure it, keep what works.

    🧩 Matching the Approach to the Problem

    Not every problem needs the same method. The Cynefin framework helps you choose.

    DomainNatureApproach
    ClearCause and effect obvious; best practice existsSense, categorise, respond (follow the checklist)
    ComplicatedNeeds expertise to analyse; good practices existSense, analyse, respond (ask experts, root-cause analysis)
    ComplexCause and effect only clear in hindsightProbe, sense, respond (small experiments)
    ChaoticCrisis; no time to analyseAct, sense, respond (stabilise first)

    An issue tree breaks a big question into smaller, non-overlapping parts, and a hypothesis-driven approach states a likely cause up front and then tests it with data.

    🔍 Finding the Root Cause

    TechniqueUse it to
    5 WhysAsk "why" repeatedly until you reach a cause you can fix.
    Fishbone (Ishikawa)Group possible causes: people, process, tools, environment, materials.
    Pareto chartRank causes by frequency or impact; fix the vital few first.
    Timeline / change analysisCompare what changed just before the problem began.
    Is / Is-NotSharpen the problem by contrasting where it does and does not occur.
    Example

    Release late → why? Test environment was down → why? Three teams share it → why? No booking or ownership → why? Nobody was assigned. Fix: name an owner, add booking rules and automate environment creation.

    📈 Using Data and Evidence

    IdeaWhy it matters
    Pick metrics tied to the problemPrevents measuring what is easy rather than what matters
    Correlation is not causationTwo things moving together may share a third cause
    Look at trends and variationA control chart separates normal variation from real change
    Baseline before and afterWithout a baseline you cannot prove the fix worked
    Avoid vanity metricsCounts that look good but drive no decision
    📝
    Beware of small samples. One bad week may be noise; a sustained shift outside the control limits is a signal.

    Module 08 · Expert · Decisions

    Decisions, Workshops & Escalation

    Choose between options, avoid biases, run a problem-solving workshop, work a full case and escalate systemic problems with data.

    ⚖️ Choosing Between Options

    When options are close, use a weighted decision matrix: score each option 1–5 on criteria, multiply by the criterion weight and add up.

    Criterion (weight)Option AOption B
    Cost (30%)34
    Speed (40%)53
    Risk (30%)24
    Weighted total3.53.6
    Example

    A: 3×0.3 + 5×0.4 + 2×0.3 = 3.5. B: 4×0.3 + 3×0.4 + 4×0.3 = 3.6. B wins narrowly; because the gap is small, check how sensitive the result is to the weights and record the decision, rationale and owner in a decision log.

    🎭 Biases and Facilitation

    TrapWhat happensCounter-measure
    AnchoringFirst number or idea dominatesCollect estimates independently first
    Confirmation biasOnly evidence that agrees is noticedAsk what would prove us wrong
    Sunk costContinue because of past spendJudge on future value and cost
    GroupthinkDissent stays silentInvite objections; assign a devil's advocate

    Good facilitation alternates divergent thinking (many ideas, no judging) with convergent thinking (compare, choose).

    🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Running a Problem-Solving Workshop

    MinutesSegmentOutput
    5Frame the problem and ground rulesAgreed problem statement
    10Review the dataShared facts
    15Find causes (5 Whys / fishbone)Ranked root causes
    15Generate and compare optionsShortlist with criteria scores
    10DecideChosen option and owner
    5Actions and follow-up dateWritten action list
    💡
    Collect ideas silently first (sticky notes or a shared board), then discuss. It prevents the loudest voice from setting the direction.

    🧪 Worked Case: Recurring Release Delays

    Over the last 12 releases, 8 were delayed. The delay causes were counted:

    CauseDelayed releasesShare of delays
    Unavailable test environment562.5%
    Late requirement changes225%
    Failed regression tests112.5%
    Total8100%

    A Pareto view shows the environment alone explains 5 of 8 delays (5 ÷ 8 = 62.5%). A 5 Whys session found that three teams share one environment with no booking rule. Options (dedicated environments, booking calendar, automated environment creation) were scored in a weighted matrix; automated creation won. The team set a target: environment-related delays from 5 per 12 releases to 1 or fewer, and re-measured after two months.

    🧱 Advanced: Systemic Problems and Escalation

    If the same issue returns, you are treating symptoms. A systemic problem sits outside one team's control (policy, tooling, structure) and needs escalation with evidence.

    Escalation elementWhy
    Problem statementClear and factual
    Impact in numbersBlocked days, cost of delay, customer effect
    Options and recommendationShows ownership
    Decision needed by whenDrives action
    Owner and follow-up dateCloses the loop
    ✅
    After the fix, verify with data that the original problem measure improved, then share the learning so others avoid it.

    Module 09 · Practice

    Practice: Rescuing a Late Release

    Apply the four soft skills to one realistic scenario.

    🏗️ Scenario

    A key customer release is due in 3 weeks. One of 8 engineers has resigned, two others are spending half their time on production support, and the shared QA environment is often down. The customer has just asked for 3 extra features. The team is already working late and morale is dropping. The customer sponsor has asked for a short written update and a recovery plan by tomorrow.

    0 / 0 correct

    1️⃣ Task 1 · Pragmatism & Customer Value

    ✍️ How would you re-plan the scope, and how would you respond to the 3 extra features?
    Model answer: Confirm the customer outcome that matters most, rank scope with MoSCoW or WSJF, protect the must-haves at full quality and defer the rest to a dated follow-up release. Show the trade-off to the customer: the extra features can be added only by swapping out lower-value items or moving the date. Deliver in small increments with a demo each week.
    Q. The customer asks for 3 extra features with a fixed date. The best response is to…
    • A Accept all of them silently
    • B Refuse without explanation
    • C Show the trade-offs and add them only by swapping scope or moving the date
    • D Skip testing to fit them in

    2️⃣ Task 2 · Resource Management

    ✍️ Estimate the effective capacity (7 engineers left, 2 of them at 50%, 15 working days, 0.7 focus factor) and propose actions.
    Model answer: Effective people = 5 + 2×0.5 = 6. Capacity = 6 × 15 × 0.7 = 63 person-days. If demand is about 80 person-days there is a gap of 17. Close it by deferring scope, reducing support load (rotate or backfill support), fixing the QA environment, and possibly a short-term contractor brought in early for a well-separated task. Protect the team: limit WIP, no mandatory overtime, plan recovery time.
    Q. Adding two new engineers in week 2 of a 3-week rescue will most likely…
    • A Finish the release sooner for certain
    • B Remove the support load
    • C Fix the QA environment automatically
    • D Slow the team short-term because of onboarding and coordination

    3️⃣ Task 3 · Problem-Solving

    ✍️ The QA environment keeps failing. How would you analyse it and escalate?
    Model answer: Define the problem with data (hours or days lost per week, teams affected). Use 5 Whys and a Pareto chart to find the biggest cause. List options (dedicated environments, booking rules, automated environment creation), score them in a weighted matrix and recommend one. Escalate with the impact, options, decision needed by a date and an owner, then verify that lost days fall.
    Q. Environment failures hit three teams repeatedly. This is best treated as…
    • A A systemic problem needing root-cause analysis and escalation with data
    • B A one-off that will fix itself
    • C Each team's private problem
    • D A reason to skip testing

    4️⃣ Task 4 · Communication & Leadership

    ✍️ Write a short update to the customer sponsor covering status, risks, options and the decision you need.
    Model answer: Open with the current status in one line, name the top risks (lost engineer, support load, unstable QA environment) with their impact on the date, then give 2–3 options: (1) deliver must-haves on time and move the 3 extra features to a dated follow-up, (2) deliver everything one week later, (3) add a contractor now for a separable task. Recommend option 1, state the quality bar that will not change, and ask for a decision by a specific date. Promise the next update time.
    Q. Which update style builds the most trust with a customer sponsor?
    • A Only good news until the deadline
    • B Honest status, quantified risks, options with a recommendation and a clear ask
    • C A long technical log with no recommendation
    • D A promise that everything will be delivered, no matter what
