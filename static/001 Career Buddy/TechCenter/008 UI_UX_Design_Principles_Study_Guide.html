UI, UX, and everything around themCore

UI (User Interface) is the visual and interactive layer — type, colour, spacing, components, states, motion. UX (User Experience) is the whole felt experience of reaching a goal — findability, flow, effort, recovery from mistakes, and how the person feels afterwards.

The common beginner error is treating them as a pair. They are actually two layers in a stack of five, and confusing the layers is why teams "redesign the UI" when the real problem lives two layers down.

Fig 1 — The five planes of user experience (after Jesse James Garrett). Decisions on lower planes constrain everything above them.
⚠️
The most expensive mistake in the industry: fixing a strategy-layer problem with a UI-layer solution. If users abandon your checkout because shipping cost appears only at the final step, no amount of button polish will help — that is a scope/strategy decision about when to reveal price.

Adjacent disciplines you will be asked aboutIntermediate

  • Interaction Design (IxD) — the behaviour of the system over time: what happens on tap, on error, on empty, on slow network. Sits between UI and UX.
  • Information Architecture (IA) — how content is organised, labelled and found. Bad IA is invisible in a mockup and lethal in production.
  • Content design / UX writing — the words are the interface. Button labels, error messages and empty states usually change task success more than layout does.
  • Service design — the end-to-end experience including offline touchpoints (delivery, returns, call centre). For an e-commerce product, the return flow is service design, not screen design.
  • Design engineering — the role that owns the translation from tokens and components into shipped, accessible, performant code.

Norman's model: why interfaces confuse peopleIntermediate

Don Norman's framework explains failure mechanically rather than as "bad design". A person carries a mental model — their belief about how the system works. The designer holds a conceptual model — how it actually works. The interface is the only channel between them. Every usability problem is a mismatch, and it shows up in one of two gulfs.

Fig 2 — Bridge the gulf of execution with signifiers and constraints; bridge the gulf of evaluation with feedback and visible system state.

The six design leversIntermediate

LeverDefinitionConcrete UI example
AffordanceWhat an object can do, given its properties and the user's capabilities.A text input can receive text. A disabled button affords nothing — which is why disabled buttons with no explanation are a usability trap.
SignifierThe perceivable cue that tells the user the affordance exists.Underlined blue text signifies a link. A caret signifies a dropdown. Removing signifiers for "cleanliness" is the flat-design failure mode.
ConstraintA limit that makes the wrong action impossible or hard.Date pickers that grey out past dates; card fields that reject letters; max-length on OTP inputs.
MappingThe relationship between control and effect.A volume slider that goes up for louder. A "next" arrow pointing right in an LTR locale — and mirrored in RTL.

Conceptual modelA simplified story of how the system works that the UI teaches.Gmail's "Archive vs Delete" only works because the interface teaches that mail is stored, not deleted.
🔍 Worked example — diagnosing with the framework

Symptom: On a shopping cart, 12% of users tap "Update quantity" twice and end up with double the items.

Wrong diagnosis: "Users are careless." Right diagnosis: gulf of evaluation. The quantity update takes ~900ms and gives no feedback, so the user cannot tell whether their first tap registered.

Fix stack: (1) Feedback — optimistic UI updates the number instantly. (2) Constraint — disable the control while the request is in flight and show an inline spinner. (3) Signifier — a brief "Updated" micro-confirmation. (4) Safety net — idempotent request key on the backend so a duplicate submit cannot double-charge. Note that three of the four fixes are interface-level and one is architectural; senior design work routinely crosses that line.

Fidelity: choosing the cheapest artefact that answers the questionAdvanced

Junior designers jump to high fidelity because it looks impressive. Senior designers pick the lowest fidelity that can still kill a bad idea, because fidelity is a commitment device — the prettier the mock, the harder it is for anyone to say "scrap it".

Why this matters commerciallyCore

Design arguments win in most organisations only when they are framed in money, risk or time. Learn to translate.

Design statementBusiness translation
"The checkout has too many steps.""Each additional required field measurably reduces completion. Removing three fields is worth an estimated X% of monthly revenue at current traffic."
"The error messages are unclear.""38% of our support tickets this month reference a single error. Rewriting it removes recurring support cost."
"We should do accessibility work.""Roughly 15–16% of people live with a disability, WCAG 2.2 AA is a procurement requirement for enterprise customers, and accessibility litigation is rising year on year."
"We need a design system.""Engineers currently rebuild the same button four times. A shared component library reduces duplicated build and QA effort per release."
📌
Retain this: the cost of fixing a usability problem rises sharply the later it is caught — cheapest in a sketch, expensive after launch, and most expensive when it becomes a support process people depend on. This is the single strongest argument for research before build.

Laws, heuristics and the psychology underneath

These are not decorative trivia for interviews. Each one is a compressed research finding that converts an opinion ("this feels cluttered") into a defensible prediction ("this menu exceeds working-memory capacity, so selection time will rise and errors will increase"). Learn them as decision rules, not as facts.

Cognitive load: the master conceptIntermediate

Nearly every law below is a special case of one idea: human working memory is small, slow and easily overwritten. Cognitive load theory splits the demand on it into three parts, and only one of them is worth spending.

Fig 4 — Three types of cognitive load. Designers cut the red one and spend the saving on the other two.

Memory & attention lawsCore

Miller's Law — and the correction nobody quotes

Miller (1956) observed that people hold about 7 ± 2 items in immediate memory. This is the version everyone repeats. The important part is the mechanism: capacity is measured in chunks, not raw items, and chunking is learned. A phone number is 10 digits but 3 chunks.

The modern refinement (Cowan, 2001) puts the practical limit closer to 4 ± 1 chunks when rehearsal is prevented — which is exactly the condition users are in when they are also trying to do something. Design to the smaller number.

Fig 5 — Working-memory capacity and what it means for menus, filters and forms
🚫
Common misuse: "Miller's Law says a navigation menu must have max 7 items." That is not what it says. Miller applies to items held in memory, not items visible on screen. A well-organised 30-item mega-menu with clear grouping is fine, because nothing is being memorised. The law bites when you make people remember — an OTP shown on one screen and typed on another, or filters that reset when they navigate back.

Hick–Hyman Law

Decision time increases logarithmically with the number of equally probable choices: T = a + b · log₂(n + 1). Two consequences that matter:

  • Logarithmic, not linear. Going from 2 to 4 options costs about as much as going from 4 to 8. So consolidating 20 options into 16 rarely helps; consolidating 20 into 5 categories of 4 does.
  • "Equally probable" is the escape hatch. If one option is obviously the likely one — a highlighted recommended plan, a "Most popular" badge, a sensible default — the effective n drops and decision time collapses. This is why defaults are the most powerful tool in the interface.
🛒 Applied — a product filter panel

Before: 24 flat checkboxes for brand, unsorted. After: the 5 highest-traffic brands surfaced first, then a collapsed "Show all 24" group, plus a type-ahead search inside the facet.

Three laws are working together: Hick–Hyman (fewer visible choices → faster decisions), Pareto (the top 5 brands cover most demand), and progressive disclosure (the long tail is still reachable, just not in the way). Notice that no functionality was removed — this is the key move.

Serial position effect & Von Restorff

Serial position: items at the beginning (primacy) and end (recency) of a list are recalled best; the middle is where things go to die. Put your most important navigation items first and last. In a bottom navigation bar on mobile, the leftmost and rightmost slots are the memorable ones.

Von Restorff (isolation effect): the item that visually differs is the one remembered. This is the mechanism behind a single primary button per screen. It also explains why everything emphasised means nothing is: contrast is a limited currency, and spending it on five elements bankrupts all five.

Zeigarnik effect & the goal-gradient effect

Zeigarnik: people remember incomplete tasks better than completed ones — the tension of the unfinished. This is why progress indicators, "3 of 5 steps", and "your profile is 60% complete" produce measurable follow-through.

Goal-gradient: effort accelerates as the goal gets closer. The classic loyalty-card study found that a 10-stamp card with 2 stamps pre-filled outperformed an 8-stamp empty card, despite requiring identical purchases — the illusion of progress mattered. In product terms: give the user a head start on onboarding (mark "Account created ✓" as step one, already complete).

⚖️
Ethical line: these two effects are exactly where persuasive design becomes manipulative. Showing genuine remaining steps is helpful. Fabricating an artificial incompleteness ("Your profile is only 40% complete!" when nothing useful is missing) is a dark pattern, and it erodes trust the moment users notice. See Module 08.

Motor & time lawsCore

Fitts's Law

Time to acquire a target is MT = a + b · log₂(2D / W) — where D is distance and W is target width along the axis of motion. Bigger and closer is faster, and the relationship is logarithmic, so doubling a tiny target helps far more than doubling an already-large one.

Fig 6 — Fitts's Law in practice: screen edges on desktop, thumb reach on mobile
  • Minimum touch target: 44×44 pt (Apple HIG), 48×48 dp (Material). WCAG 2.2 sets an absolute floor of 24×24 CSS px at AA (2.5.8) and 44×44 at AAA (2.5.5).
  • Hit area ≠ visual size. A 20px icon can carry a 44px invisible padding box. Do this rather than making every icon visually huge.
  • Spacing between targets matters as much as size. Two adjacent 48px buttons with 0 gap still produce mis-taps; keep at least 8px between interactive targets.

Doherty Threshold & response-time budgets

Productivity rises sharply when system response drops below roughly 400ms — fast enough that the person never disengages from the task. Combine this with Nielsen's three classic thresholds:

DurationPerceptionRequired UI response
< 100 msFeels instantaneous; direct manipulationNothing needed. Hover, press and toggle states must land here.
0.1 – 1 sNoticeable delay, but flow of thought is unbrokenNo spinner (a spinner that flashes for 300ms is worse than none). Consider optimistic UI.
1 – 10 sAttention starts to wanderShow a determinate indicator or skeleton. Keep the layout stable to avoid shift.
> 10 sUser leaves, switches tabs, or repeats the actionProgress with an estimate, ability to cancel, and ideally a "we'll email you" escape.
⏱️
Advanced nuance — perceived vs actual duration. Perceived wait is roughly actual duration modified by uncertainty and by whether the user is occupied. A determinate progress bar feels shorter than an indeterminate spinner of identical length. A skeleton screen feels shorter than a blank page. And a progress bar that accelerates toward the end is perceived as faster than a linear one. You can buy real satisfaction without making the backend faster — but only up to a point, and never by faking completion.

Behavioural & perceptual lawsIntermediate

Jakob's Law

"Users spend most of their time on other sites." Their expectations are formed elsewhere, so conventions are not creative failures — they are accumulated shared knowledge. A cart icon in the top right, a logo that returns home, a hamburger on mobile: deviating from these costs you real task success in exchange for novelty nobody asked for.

The senior version of this law is knowing when to break it: deviate only where you have a genuine differentiating advantage, and where you can afford to teach the new pattern.

Aesthetic–Usability Effect

People perceive attractive designs as more usable, and — critically — are more tolerant of minor usability problems in attractive interfaces. Two implications pull in opposite directions:

  • Positive: visual craft buys goodwill and forgiveness. It is not decoration; it is a usability asset.
  • Negative: it contaminates usability testing. Participants testing a beautiful prototype under-report problems. If you test high-fidelity work, watch behaviour (did they complete the task, how long, how many errors) rather than trusting what people say.

Peak–End Rule

Retrospective judgement of an experience is dominated by its most intense moment and its ending — not the average, and famously not the duration. Design deliberately for both.

Fig 7 — Peak–End: find your worst moment and your final moment, and fix those first
🎯 How to actually apply Peak–End

Step 1 — locate the peak. It is usually negative and usually already visible in your data: the step with the highest drop-off, the error with the most support tickets, the screen with the most rage-clicks.

Step 2 — locate the end. Not the last screen you designed — the last screen the user actually sees. For e-commerce that is often the confirmation email, not the confirmation page.

Step 3 — invest disproportionately in those two. A recovery-oriented error state at the peak, and a confirmation that answers the three questions everyone has: did it work, what happens next, when.

Tesler's Law (conservation of complexity)

Every process has irreducible complexity. It can be moved but not deleted. The design decision is always who absorbs it: the user, the interface, or the engineering system.

ComplexityPushed to the user (bad)Absorbed by the system (good)
Address formats vary by countryA free-text box and a validation error after submitAddress autocomplete + country-aware field layout and labels (postcode vs ZIP vs PIN)
Card type detectionA dropdown asking "Visa / Mastercard / Amex?"Detect from the first digits, show the brand inline, format spacing automatically
Time zones"Enter time in UTC"Show local time with the zone labelled, store UTC, convert silently
Duplicate submissions"Please don't press back or refresh"Idempotency keys server-side + disabled control during flight

Postel's Law applied to interfaces

"Be liberal in what you accept, conservative in what you send." Accept the phone number with spaces, the card number with dashes, the name in any case, the date as 5/6/25 and 05-06-2025 — then normalise silently. Rejecting input that you could obviously have parsed is one of the most common and most avoidable sources of form abandonment.

Gestalt principlesCore

Perception groups things before conscious thought happens. These principles describe the grouping rules, and they operate whether or not you designed for them — which means an accidental grouping reads as a real one.

The core principles, applied

Module 02 gave you the science. This module is the working checklist — the seven principles you apply on every screen, plus Nielsen's ten heuristics, which are the industry-standard vocabulary for evaluating an interface you did not design.

1. Visual hierarchyCore

Hierarchy is the deliberate ordering of attention. Before someone reads a screen, they scan it in roughly 2–3 seconds and form a plan. Hierarchy determines whether that plan is the right one.

The six levers, ranked by strength

  1. Size — the crudest and strongest. A 32px headline dominates 15px body text regardless of anything else.
  2. Weight — bold reads as more important at identical size. Cheaper than size because it costs no layout space.
  3. Colour & contrast — a saturated accent on a neutral field wins attention. This is why the accent colour must be rationed.
  4. Space — isolation creates importance. A button with generous surrounding whitespace reads as primary even without colour.
  5. Position — top-left in LTR reading order gets seen first; the F-pattern and Z-pattern describe common scan paths on text-heavy and sparse pages respectively.
  6. Depth — elevation, shadow, and overlay pull an element forward. Use sparingly; layered shadows on everything flattens the effect.
Fig 9 — Four levels of hierarchy on a product page. Note there is exactly one filled button.
Do
  • Define 3–5 hierarchy levels and use only those.
  • Squint at the screen — the shapes you still see should be the things that matter.
  • Make secondary actions genuinely secondary (outline or text button).
  • Use space, not lines, as the first tool for separation.
Don't
  • Give two elements the same visual weight when one is more important.
  • Use more than one filled/primary button per view.
  • Rely on colour alone to signal importance (fails for colour-blind users).
  • Add a divider line wherever spacing was the real fix.

2. Consistency — four kindsIntermediate

"Be consistent" is uselessly vague advice until you split it. Most real arguments in design reviews are about which kind of consistency to prioritise.

TypeMeaningViolation example
VisualSame look for the same thing across the productThree different shades of "primary blue" because three teams picked their own
FunctionalSame control produces the same result everywhereSwipe-left archives in one list and deletes in another
InternalConsistency within your own product familyThe mobile app calls it "Orders", the web calls it "Purchase history"
ExternalConsistency with the wider world's conventions (Jakob's Law)A magnifying-glass icon that opens settings
🔀
When to break consistency deliberately: when a difference in behaviour must be noticed. A destructive confirm dialog should look different from a routine one. A permanently irreversible action ("Delete workspace") should not use the same button style as "Save". Here, inconsistency is the safety mechanism — this is Von Restorff used on purpose.

3. Progressive disclosure & its cousinsIntermediate

Reveal complexity in proportion to the user's demonstrated need. Three related techniques, often confused:

TechniqueMechanismUse when
Progressive disclosureAdvanced options hidden behind a control the user opensA minority of users need the advanced option, but they need it reliably. e.g. "Advanced shipping options"
Staged disclosureA linear sequence of steps, each on its own screenThe task is genuinely sequential and long. e.g. checkout, onboarding, KYC
Progressive onboardingFeatures introduced contextually, at the moment of first relevanceReplacing an upfront tutorial nobody reads. e.g. a tooltip the first time a user opens the filter panel
⚠️
The failure mode: progressive disclosure becomes obstruction when you hide something the user needs in order to decide. Hiding shipping cost, cancellation terms, or the total price behind an extra step is not progressive disclosure — it is a dark pattern called drip pricing, and it is increasingly regulated.

4. ContrastCore

Contrast does two separate jobs and they are often confused. Attentional contrast decides what gets noticed. Legibility contrast decides what can be read at all. The second one has hard numeric requirements — see Module 06.

Fig 10 — A four-level action hierarchy. Tertiary still meets contrast minimums; low attention never means low legibility.
🎨
The greyed-out trap: designers reduce contrast to de-emphasise, then fail WCAG. "Secondary" text at #b0b0b0 on white is 2.5:1 — unreadable for many users and non-compliant. De-emphasise with size, weight and position first, and only lower colour contrast down to the 4.5:1 floor (3:1 for large text).

5. Proximity, alignment & the invisible gridCore

Proximity communicates grouping; alignment communicates order. Neither is decorative — both change how quickly a person can parse a screen.

Proximity in forms — the classic bug

Label spacing is the most common proximity error in production software. If the gap between a label and its own input is larger than the gap to the field above, users bind the label to the wrong field. The rule: inner spacing (label→field) must be visibly tighter than outer spacing (field→next label). A ratio of roughly 1:2 or 1:3 is safe.

Spacing that respects proximity
.field        { margin-bottom: 24px; }  /* outer: between fields  */
.field label  { margin-bottom: 6px;  }  /* inner: label to input  */
.field .hint  { margin-top: 6px;     }  /* hint binds to its field */

Alignment

Every element should align to something else. A single consistent left edge for labels, inputs and headings dramatically reduces the eye movement needed to scan a form. Centre alignment looks elegant for short hero text and becomes hostile past two lines, because each line has a different starting point.

Writing error messages that workIntermediate

A usable error message answers three questions in plain language: what happened, why, and what do I do now. It never blames the user and never exposes internal codes as the primary message.

Unusable
  • "Error 4021: invalid input."
  • "Payment failed."
  • "Invalid email address." (shown while still typing)
  • A red banner at the top of a long form with no indication of which field is wrong.
Usable
  • "That postcode doesn't match the selected state. Check the postcode, or change the state."
  • "Your bank declined the payment. Try a different card, or contact your bank — nothing was charged."
  • Validate on blur, not on keystroke; re-validate live once an error exists.
  • Inline message next to the field, field focused, plus a summary at top linking to each error.

7. User control, freedom & error preventionCore

Two related principles: prevent the error if you can, and if you can't, make it cheap to undo.

TechniqueHow it worksBest for
ConstraintMake the invalid state unreachableDate pickers that disable past dates; quantity steppers capped at stock level
Good defaultsThe most common correct answer is pre-filledCountry from IP, saved address, standard shipping
ConfirmationAn extra deliberate step before actingRare, truly irreversible actions only. Overused, it becomes noise people click through.
UndoAct immediately, offer reversal for a windowAlmost always better than confirmation. Gmail's Undo Send is the canonical example.
Type-to-confirmUser types the resource name to proceedCatastrophic, unrecoverable actions: deleting a production database or an organisation
↩️
Prefer undo over confirm. A confirmation dialog interrupts everyone, including the 99% who meant it, and habituation means people stop reading it within a week. Undo interrupts nobody and protects the 1% who didn't. Reserve confirmation for actions that genuinely cannot be reversed after the fact.

Nielsen's 10 usability heuristicsCore

These are the shared vocabulary of the field. You will be asked about them in interviews, and more importantly they are the checklist used in a heuristic evaluation — the cheapest formal way to find usability problems without recruiting users.

#HeuristicWhat it demands, with an example
1Visibility of system stateKeep users informed through timely feedback. A delivery tracker showing "Packed → Shipped → Out for delivery"; an upload showing percent complete.
2Match between system and the real worldUse the user's language and familiar concepts, not internal jargon. "Trash" not "Soft-deleted entity"; "Order total" not "Aggregate line item sum".
3User control and freedomClearly marked emergency exits. Undo, cancel, a visible close on every modal, back that actually goes back.
4Consistency and standardsSame words and actions mean the same thing; follow platform conventions. Don't invent a new gesture for a standard action.
5Error preventionDesign out the error class. Address autocomplete prevents malformed addresses better than any validation message.
6Recognition rather than recallMake options, actions and objects visible. Recently viewed items; a visible list of applied filters rather than requiring memory of what was set.
7Flexibility and efficiency of useAccelerators for experts that don't burden novices. Keyboard shortcuts, saved addresses, one-click reorder, bulk select.
8Aesthetic and minimalist designEvery extra unit of information competes with the relevant units. This is about relevance, not about being sparse for its own sake.
9Help users recognise, diagnose and recover from errorsPlain language, precise cause, constructive suggestion. See the error-message rules above.
10Help and documentationIdeally unnecessary; when needed, searchable, task-focused, and available in context rather than in a separate manual.
📋 How to run a heuristic evaluationAdvanced

1. Recruit 3–5 evaluators. One evaluator finds roughly a third of problems; five find the large majority. They must evaluate independently first, then merge — group evaluation from the start produces anchoring.

2. Walk defined tasks, not the whole product. "Buy a specific product as a guest", "return an item".

3. Log each issue against a heuristic number, with a screenshot and the location.

4. Rate severity 0–4 using frequency × impact × persistence:

  • 0 — not a usability problem
  • 1 — cosmetic; fix if time permits
  • 2 — minor; low priority
  • 3 — major; important to fix, high priority
  • 4 — usability catastrophe; must fix before release

5. Merge and prioritise by severity, then by fix cost. Severity 4 items ship first regardless of cost.

Limitation to state out loud: heuristic evaluation finds violations of known principles. It does not tell you whether you built the right thing, and it produces false positives that real users would never hit. It complements usability testing; it does not replace it.

Design systems & visual craft

A design system is not a sticker sheet. It is a shared, versioned contract between design and engineering: tokens, components, patterns, documentation, and a governance process for changing them. This module covers the parts you are expected to reason about at intermediate and senior level.

Atomic structureIntermediate

Fig 12 — Atomic design (Brad Frost). The last stage is where long product names and empty states break your beautiful components.
🧪
Stress-test rule: design every component with the worst realistic content, not the best. A product card must survive a 90-character title, a missing image, a 4-digit price, a discount badge and a 5-line variant list — simultaneously. Components that only look good with lorem ipsum are the number-one source of "the build doesn't match the design".

Design tokens: the three-tier modelAdvanced

Tokens are named design decisions stored as data, so design and code share one source of truth. The mistake most teams make is using a single flat layer of tokens. A three-tier structure is what makes theming and rebranding possible.

Spacing, grid and layoutIntermediate

Use a 4px base unit with an 8px rhythm for most spacing. The 4px steps exist for tight, small-component spacing (icon-to-label); the 8/16/24/32 steps carry the page rhythm. Two rules make spacing look intentional:

  1. Space is a token, not a number. If a value is not on the scale, you have made an exception — justify it or fix the component.
  2. Relationship distance encodes meaning. Space within a group < space between groups < space between sections. Get the ratios right and you can delete most divider lines.

Breakpoints

Design for content, not devices. Set a breakpoint where the layout actually breaks, not at "iPad width". A workable default set: 640px (small), 768px (tablet), 1024px (small laptop), 1280px (desktop), 1536px (wide). Mobile-first means the base styles are the small layout and media queries add complexity upward — this produces less CSS and fewer overrides.

Elevation, depth and motionIntermediate

Elevation communicates layering: what is on top, what is temporary, what is interactive. Define a small ladder (0, 1, 2, 3) and stick to it. Layered shadows read far more naturally than a single large blur, because real shadows have both a tight contact shadow and a soft ambient one.

Motion rules that hold up

  • Duration: 100–150ms for small state changes (hover, toggle), 200–300ms for entrances and panels, up to 400ms for full-screen transitions. Longer than ~500ms feels sluggish on repeat use.
  • Easing: ease-out for entering elements (fast start, gentle settle), ease-in for exits, ease-in-out for elements moving between two on-screen positions. Linear only for continuous loaders.
  • Motion must have a job: show causality (this panel came from that button), maintain continuity across a transition, or direct attention. Decorative animation costs performance and attention.
  • Always honour reduced motion. Vestibular disorders make large parallax and zoom transitions genuinely nauseating.

Pattern: update the interface immediately as if the request succeeded, send the request, and reconcile.

The three things people forget: (1) a rollback design — what the UI does when the request fails 900ms later, ideally a non-blocking toast with retry rather than yanking the state back silently; (2) idempotency — the same action fired twice must not apply twice; (3) ordering — if two optimistic updates race, the last server response must win, not the last client render.

Forms — where most conversions are lostCore

Structure

  • One column. Multi-column forms cause skipped fields and unpredictable tab order. The only routine exceptions are naturally paired short fields (expiry + CVC, city + postcode).
  • Labels above fields, left-aligned. Fastest completion, works with long translated labels, and survives narrow screens.
  • Never use placeholder text as a label. It disappears on focus, destroys the ability to review answers, usually fails contrast, and is inconsistently announced by screen readers.
  • Field length as an affordance. A postcode field should be short and a street address long — the width is a signal about expected input.
  • Mark optional fields, not required ones, when most fields are required. Whichever is rarer is the one to mark.

Validation timingIntermediate

This is a genuinely tricky detail that most products get wrong:

  1. Do not validate while the user is first typing — telling someone their email is invalid after two characters is hostile.
  2. Validate on blur (when they leave the field). This is the correct default.
  3. Once a field is in an error state, re-validate live on each keystroke so the error clears the moment it is fixed. This asymmetry is what makes validation feel helpful rather than nagging.
  4. On submit, focus the first field with an error and show a summary list at the top linking to each one.

  • Show applied filters as removable chips above results — recognition over recall, and it prevents the "why are there no results?" dead end.

Keyboard & focusCore

Keyboard access is the backbone of accessibility — screen readers, switch devices and voice control all ride on the same underlying focus model. If it works properly with a keyboard, most of the rest follows.

  • Everything interactive must be reachable with Tab and operable with Enter/Space. If you cannot reach it, it does not exist for a large group of users.
  • Focus order must follow visual order. Reordering visually with CSS (order, row-reverse, absolute positioning) without changing the DOM breaks this.
  • Never remove the focus outline without replacing it with something at least as visible. outline: none with no substitute is the single most common accessibility defect on the web.
  • Modals must trap focus while open, return focus to the trigger on close, and close on Escape.
  • Provide a skip link as the first focusable element so keyboard users can bypass repeated navigation.
  • Never use positive tabindex. Use tabindex="0" to add something to the natural order and -1 for programmatic focus only.

Semantics, ARIA and screen readersIntermediate

⚠️
The first rule of ARIA is: don't use ARIA. A native <button> gives you role, keyboard operation, focusability and platform behaviour for free. A <div role="button"> requires you to reimplement all of it, and most implementations forget Space, Enter, or focus. Bad ARIA is measurably worse than no ARIA.

The five rules of ARIA, condensed

  1. Use a native HTML element if one exists with the semantics and behaviour you need.
  2. Do not change native semantics unless you truly must (<h2 role="tab"> is almost always wrong).
  3. All interactive ARIA controls must be usable by keyboard.
  4. Never put role="presentation" or aria-hidden="true" on a focusable element — you create an element that is focusable but invisible to screen readers.
  5. Every interactive element needs an accessible name.
Bad vs good — the same "button"
<!-- BAD: no role, no keyboard, no focus, no name -->
<div class="btn" onclick="addToCart()">
  <img src="cart.svg">
</div>

<!-- GOOD: native semantics + accessible name -->
<button type="button" onclick="addToCart()">
  <img src="cart.svg" alt="">
  <span class="visually-hidden">Add Nike Air Max to cart</span>
</button>

Alt text: a decision tree

  • Decorative (adds no information): alt="". An empty alt is correct and necessary — omitting the attribute entirely makes some screen readers read the filename.
  • Informative: describe the information, not the picture. "Bar chart: mobile conversion fell from 3.1% to 1.8% after the redesign", not "chart".
  • Functional (the image is a link or button): describe the destination or action — "Search", not "magnifying glass icon".
  • Product photography: include what a sighted shopper gains — colour, material, view. "Navy wool overcoat, front view, buttoned".
  • Text in an image: reproduce the text. Better still, don't put text in an image.

Announcing dynamic changes

When content changes without a page load — a cart badge, a filter result count, a toast — screen-reader users get nothing unless you announce it.

Live regions
<!-- Polite: waits for a pause. Use for most updates. -->
<div aria-live="polite" aria-atomic="true" id="cart-status">
  3 items in cart
</div>

<!-- Assertive: interrupts immediately. Errors only. -->
<div role="alert">
  Payment declined. Nothing was charged.
</div>

The live region must exist in the DOM before the content changes — injecting the region and its text at the same time usually announces nothing.

A testing routine you can actually runIntermediate

  • Run an automated scan (axe DevTools, Lighthouse). This catches roughly 30–40% of issues — necessary, never sufficient.
  • Unplug the mouse. Complete the primary task using only the keyboard. Note anywhere focus disappears or gets stuck.
  • Zoom the browser to 200%, then 400%. Nothing may be cut off, overlapped, or require horizontal scrolling of the page body.
  • Set the OS to a 320px-wide viewport equivalent. Content must reflow to a single column.
  • Turn on the screen reader (NVDA on Windows, VoiceOver on Mac/iOS) and navigate by headings, then by landmarks, then by form controls.
  • Check every form error path with a screen reader — this is where products fail most often.
  • Enable "reduce motion" and confirm nothing essential is lost.
  • Grayscale the screen and confirm every status, error and chart is still readable.
🌍

This is the module that separates designers who have opinions from designers who have evidence. It is also the most common weak spot in mid-level portfolios: plenty of screens, no measurement.

Choosing a research methodIntermediate

Two axes decide almost everything. Attitudinal vs behavioural: what people say versus what they do. Qualitative vs quantitative: why versus how many. Pick the quadrant that answers your actual question.

🗣️
The cardinal rule of user research: never ask people to predict their own future behaviour. "Would you use this?" and "How much would you pay?" produce confidently wrong answers. Ask about the past ("Tell me about the last time you returned something online — walk me through what you actually did") and observe the present (give them the task and watch). Both are reliable; prediction is not.

Sample size, honestly

The famous "5 users find 85% of problems" figure comes from Nielsen and Landauer's model, and it is widely misapplied. What it actually means and what it doesn't:

  • It applies to qualitative usability testing, for finding problems — not for measuring anything.
  • It assumes one homogeneous user group and one task set. Two distinct personas means roughly 5 each. Mobile and desktop are separate contexts.
  • Three rounds of 5 beat one round of 15, because you fix problems between rounds and uncover the ones that were hidden behind them.
  • For quantitative claims (task times, conversion, SUS comparison) you need far more — typically 20+ per group for a rough estimate, and a proper power calculation for a real comparison.

📐 A worked GSM chain

Goal: Shoppers can find a product they will buy without needing to contact support.

Signals: They use filters and refine rather than bouncing; they reach a product page from search; they don't repeat the same search; they don't open the help widget from a listing page.

Metrics: (1) search-to-product-page rate; (2) zero-result search rate; (3) filter usage per session; (4) support contacts originating on listing pages per 1,000 sessions.

Guardrail metric: average order value must not fall — otherwise you may have "improved findability" by only surfacing cheap items.

A/B testing without fooling yourselfAdvanced

  • State a hypothesis before you look. "Because [evidence], we believe [change] will cause [metric] to move [direction]. We'll know we're right when [threshold]." Without this, any result can be rationalised.
  • Calculate sample size and duration up front, and do not stop early because it looks good. Peeking and stopping at significance is the most common way teams ship changes that do nothing.
  • Run full weekly cycles. Weekend traffic behaves differently from Tuesday traffic.
  • Watch for the novelty effect — a new UI often lifts engagement for a week purely because it is new, then reverts. Long-running holdouts detect this.
  • Always define guardrail metrics. A variant that lifts add-to-cart but raises returns or support contacts is a loss.
  • Testing cannot invent. A/B tests choose between options you already had. They will optimise you to a local maximum and never tell you that a fundamentally different design would be better.
🧪
Multiple-comparison trap: if you test one change against twenty metrics, roughly one will look "significant" at p<0.05 by chance alone. Pick one primary metric before the test. Everything else is secondary and exploratory, and must be labelled as such when you present.

Presenting research so it changes decisionsAdvanced

A finding that nobody acts on has zero value. Three habits make research land:

  1. Lead with the decision, not the method. Open with "We should remove the account-creation requirement before checkout" and put the methodology in an appendix.
  2. Quantify severity and reach. "7 of 8 participants failed this step, and it sits on the path of 100% of purchases" is actionable. "Some users found it confusing" is not.
  3. Show, don't summarise. A 20-second clip of a real person failing changes more minds than any slide. Anonymise and get consent.

Core AI UX patterns

  • Progressive/streamed output. Showing tokens as they arrive converts a 10-second wait into a 0.5-second wait plus reading time. It also lets the user cancel early once they see the direction is wrong.
  • Editable, not applied. AI output should arrive as a draft the user accepts, edits or rejects. Auto-applying changes removes the control that makes the feature trustworthy.
  • Provenance and citation. Where did this come from? Linking to sources converts an unverifiable claim into a checkable one.
  • Calibrated uncertainty. Communicate confidence honestly — and avoid false precision. "87% confident" implies a rigour that usually is not there; "I'm not certain — verify this against the invoice" is more useful.
  • Scoped entry points. A blank chat box has infinite affordance and therefore none. Task-specific entry points ("Summarise this order history") outperform an open prompt for most users.
  • Feedback loops. Thumbs up/down, "regenerate", and inline correction give the user agency and give you training signal — but only if you act on the data.
  • Graceful refusal. When the system can't or shouldn't answer, say so plainly and offer the nearest thing it can do. Silent failure is the worst option.
⚖️
Trust calibration is the central design problem. Two failure modes, and they need opposite fixes. Over-reliance: the user accepts wrong output because the interface looks authoritative — fix with visible uncertainty, citations, and friction before consequential actions. Under-reliance: the user ignores correct output because they were burned once — fix with transparency about what changed and reliable performance on easy cases. A confident tone applied uniformly causes over-reliance; that is a design choice, not a model property.
🤖 Applying the classic principles to an AI feature

Feature: "Write a product description" inside an e-commerce admin panel.

  • User control (Nielsen #3): output lands in an editable field, never saved automatically; undo restores the previous description.
  • Visibility of system state (#1): streaming output plus a visible "Generating… Cancel" affordance.
  • Error recovery (#9): if generation fails, keep the existing text intact and offer retry — never leave the field empty.
  • Hierarchy: AI output is visually marked as a draft (a distinct container and label) so it is never confused with published copy.
  • Accessibility: the streaming region is an aria-live="polite" region; the cancel control is keyboard-reachable; the completion is announced.
  • Tesler's Law: the system absorbs prompt engineering — the user picks a tone and length from a short set, rather than learning to write prompts.

A dark pattern (the regulatory term is now "deceptive design") is an interface that manipulates a user into a choice they would not otherwise make. This is no longer just an ethics discussion — the EU's Digital Services Act, the GDPR consent rules, the FTC in the US and India's CCPA guidelines all now target specific patterns.

DesignOps & handoffAdvanced

At scale, the bottleneck stops being design quality and becomes design throughput and consistency. DesignOps is the practice of removing that friction.

What a complete handoff contains

  • All eight states from Module 05, not just the ideal one.
  • Responsive behaviour at each breakpoint — including what reflows, what stacks, what is hidden and why.
  • Interaction specs: hover, focus, active, disabled, loading, selected — for every interactive element.
  • Motion specs: duration, easing, what animates, and the reduced-motion fallback.
  • Content rules: max lengths, truncation behaviour, pluralisation, empty and overflow copy.
  • Accessibility notes: heading structure, tab order, accessible names, live-region announcements, focus return on modal close.
  • Edge cases: offline, slow network, permission denied, expired session, extreme data values.
  • Token references rather than raw hex values and pixel numbers.
🤝
Handoff is not a moment, it's a relationship. The single most effective practice is involving an engineer during design, not after. Ten minutes of "can we actually do this with our data model?" prevents weeks of rework — and engineers routinely propose simpler solutions that designers would not have thought of because they know what is cheap in the system.

Design QA

Design review of the built product, before release, against the spec. Log issues in the same tracker as engineering bugs with the same severity scale — a separate "design nitpick" list gets ignored. Distinguish clearly between defects (does not match the agreed spec) and change requests (the spec was wrong); conflating them is why design QA gets a reputation for scope creep.

Where the field is goingAdvanced

  • Interfaces that adapt per user raise a new consistency problem: if the UI differs for everyone, shared mental models and peer support break down. Adaptation needs to be predictable and reversible.
  • Multimodal input (voice, camera, gesture) means designing the same task across modalities with different affordances and error profiles — voice has no visible affordances at all, so discoverability must be solved differently.
  • Agentic systems that take actions on the user's behalf make consent, reversibility and auditability into primary design surfaces. The core question shifts from "how do I do this?" to "what did it just do, and can I undo it?"
  • Accessibility of generated interfaces is an unsolved problem: if the UI is produced at runtime, semantic correctness cannot be hand-verified. Structural guarantees have to be built into the generator.
🧠
The durable takeaway: the technology layer changes every few years; the constraint layer does not. Working memory is still small, attention is still expensive, people still need to know what happened and how to undo it. Every "new" AI UX pattern in this module is one of Nielsen's ten heuristics applied to a harder substrate. Learn the constraints, and each new medium is a variation rather than a fresh start.

Practice brief — do this before the assessmentIntermediate

✍️ Your turn

Pick any e-commerce or booking product you use and produce a one-page audit:

  1. Define one task precisely ("buy a specific item as a guest, delivered to a new address").
  2. Walk it on mobile, capturing a screenshot at every step. Count the taps and the required fields.

Section A1 — Foundations, laws & psychology

1
A team is redesigning the visual style of a checkout because 40% of users abandon it after seeing the shipping cost on the final step. Using the five planes model, what kind of problem is this?
  • AA visual/UI-layer problem — the cost is not styled prominently enough
  • BAn interaction-layer problem — the button lacks feedback
  • CA scope/strategy-layer problem — the decision about when price information is revealed
  • DA structure-layer problem — the navigation labels are unclear
Answer: C. The failure is a decision about what information exists at which stage, which lives in the scope/strategy planes. Restyling the cost (a UI fix) will not stop people abandoning when they discover an unexpected charge. This is the classic pattern of trying to solve a lower-plane problem with a higher-plane tool.
2
A user taps "Save", nothing visibly changes for 900ms, so they tap again and create a duplicate record. Which gulf has failed, and what is the primary fix?
  • AGulf of execution — add a larger button
  • BGulf of evaluation — give immediate feedback and disable the control while in flight
  • CGulf of execution — add an onboarding tooltip explaining the button
  • DGulf of evaluation — display an error message after the second tap
Answer: B. The user knew what to do and did it (execution succeeded). What failed was their ability to tell whether it had worked — the gulf of evaluation. Feedback plus an in-flight disabled state addresses it; server-side idempotency is the safety net behind it.
3
Which statement about Miller's Law is correct?
  • ANavigation menus must never contain more than seven items
  • BUsers can only look at seven things on a screen at once
  • CSeven is the maximum number of form fields per page
  • DIt concerns items held in working memory; visible on-screen options are not memorised and so are not directly limited by it
Answer: D. Miller's finding is about immediate memory capacity in chunks, not about how many things can be displayed. The law bites whenever you force someone to carry information across screens — an OTP shown on one screen and entered on another, or filters that reset on back navigation. A well-grouped 30-item mega-menu is not a Miller violation.
4
A pricing page has eight plans and users take a long time to decide. Which change best exploits the "equally probable" condition in Hick–Hyman's law?
  • AMark one plan as "Recommended for most teams" and visually emphasise it
  • BReduce eight plans to seven
  • CMake all eight cards exactly the same size and colour for fairness
  • DMove the plans into a dropdown menu
Answer: A. Hick–Hyman assumes options are equally probable. Breaking that assumption — with a default, a recommendation, or a highlighted option — collapses effective decision time far more than shaving one option off (B, a logarithmic saving of almost nothing). C actively makes it worse by removing all differentiating signal, and D hides the options entirely, adding an interaction cost.
5
A "Delete" and a "Duplicate" button sit adjacent, each 30×30px, with no gap. Which law most directly predicts the resulting problem?
  • AJakob's Law
  • BPeak–End Rule
  • CFitts's Law
  • DTesler's Law
Answer: C. Fitts's Law relates acquisition time and accuracy to target size and distance; small, adjacent targets produce mis-taps. The compounding factor is that one of the two is destructive, so the error is expensive — which is why destructive actions should be separated from frequent ones and paired with undo.
6
A data request reliably takes about 400ms. What is the best loading treatment?
  • AA full-screen blocking spinner
  • BAn immediate press state and no spinner — 400ms is inside the "flow uninterrupted" band
  • CA determinate progress bar with a percentage
  • DA modal saying "Please wait, this may take a while"
Answer: B. Below roughly one second the user's flow of thought is unbroken, and a spinner that appears and vanishes in under half a second reads as a visual glitch. Acknowledge the press instantly (under 100ms) and let the result arrive. Reserve spinners and skeletons for waits above about one second.
7
Users report that a beautifully designed prototype "felt easy", yet only 3 of 8 completed the core task. What explains the gap, and what should you trust?
  • AParticipants were being polite; discard the session entirely
  • BThe Peak–End rule; only the ending mattered to them
  • CSerial position effect; they forgot the middle of the task
  • DThe aesthetic–usability effect; trust the behavioural data (task success) over the self-report
Answer: D. Attractive interfaces are perceived as more usable and users under-report problems in them. This is precisely why usability testing prioritises observed behaviour — completion, time, errors, hesitation — over what participants say afterwards.
8
Which is the clearest example of Tesler's Law being applied correctly?
  • ADetecting the card brand from the first digits instead of asking the user to select it
  • BRemoving the card-brand question entirely and letting payments fail
  • CAdding a help article explaining how to identify your card brand
  • DMaking the card-brand dropdown visually larger and more prominent
Answer: A. The complexity (knowing the card brand) still exists — it has been moved from the user to the system. B deletes a necessary step and breaks the outcome; C and D leave the burden with the user while decorating it.
9
In a form, the gap between a field and the label below it is 8px, while the gap between that label and its own input is 16px. What will happen and why?
  • ANothing — the DOM association is what matters
  • BUsers will find it faster because everything is evenly spaced
  • CUsers will bind each label to the field above it, because proximity overrides the logical relationship
  • DScreen readers will announce the wrong label
Answer: C. Gestalt proximity operates pre-consciously: whatever is closer is perceived as related, regardless of your intent or your markup. The rule is that inner spacing (label→input) must be visibly tighter than outer spacing (field→next label). D is wrong — screen readers follow the for/id association, which is exactly why this bug survives automated testing and only affects sighted users.
10
Which pair of Gestalt principles is correctly ranked by grouping strength when the two conflict?
  • ASimilarity is stronger than proximity
  • BCommon region (a card or border) is stronger than proximity
  • CClosure is stronger than common region
  • DContinuity is stronger than proximity
Answer: B. The practical ordering is common region > proximity > similarity. This is why a bordered card reads as one group even when the internal spacing is uneven — and why an accidental border can create a grouping you never intended.

Section A2 — Principles, systems, patterns & accessibility

11
Which is the strongest reason to prefer an undo pattern over a confirmation dialog for archiving an email?
  • AUndo is cheaper to build
  • BConfirmation dialogs are not accessible
  • CUndo looks more modern
  • DConfirmation interrupts everyone including the majority who meant it, and habituation means people stop reading it — undo interrupts nobody and still protects the minority
Answer: D. Confirmation taxes the common case to protect the rare one, and users learn to dismiss dialogs reflexively within days. Undo inverts that trade. Confirmation (or type-to-confirm) is still correct for genuinely irreversible actions where no undo window is possible.
12
You need a product to support light mode, dark mode and a white-label theme per client. Which token architecture makes this tractable?
  • APrimitive tokens → semantic tokens → component tokens, with theming applied at the semantic layer
  • BOne flat list of named colours used directly in components
  • CHex values in components, with a find-and-replace script per theme
  • DA separate stylesheet per theme, duplicated and maintained by hand
Answer: A. The semantic layer is the indirection that makes theming a data change rather than a code change: redefine --color-action-primary and every consumer follows. Flat tokens (B) require editing every component; C and D guarantee drift and accessibility regressions.
13
Which statement about dark mode is correct?
  • AIt is produced by inverting the light palette
  • BPure white text on pure black is the optimum for legibility
  • CElevation is usually signalled by raising surface lightness, because shadows are much weaker cues on dark backgrounds, and accent saturation typically needs reducing
  • DContrast requirements are relaxed in dark mode
Answer: C. Dark themes are a separate design, not an inversion. Maximum-contrast white on black causes halation for many readers, so slightly off-white on a dark grey is standard; shadows barely read, so lighter surfaces indicate elevation; and saturated accents appear to glow, so they are usually desaturated. WCAG thresholds are unchanged (D is false).
14
What is the correct validation timing for a form field?
  • AValidate on every keystroke from the first character
  • BValidate on blur; then, once the field is in an error state, re-validate live on each keystroke so the error clears as soon as it is fixed
  • CValidate only on submit, with a single banner at the top
  • DValidate on blur and never re-validate until the next submit
Answer: B. The asymmetry is the point: don't accuse someone mid-typing (A is hostile), but once an error exists, live feedback confirms the fix immediately. C forces a full round trip to discover problems, and D leaves a stale error message sitting on a field the user has already corrected.

Which is the best use of optimistic UI?
  • APlacing an order
  • BApplying a discount coupon that may be expired
  • CTransferring money between accounts
  • DMarking an item as a favourite
Answer: D. Optimistic UI suits actions that nearly always succeed and are cheap to reverse. A, B and C all have consequential or uncertain outcomes: showing success before the server confirms is either misleading (B — the coupon may be rejected) or actively dangerous (A, C). Even for D you must still design the rollback state and guarantee idempotency.

A designer removes the focus outline because "it looks ugly on click". What is the correct resolution?
  • AUse :focus-visible so the ring shows for keyboard users but not on mouse click, and style it to meet 3:1 contrast
  • BSet outline: none globally and rely on hover styles
  • CKeep outline: none but add a note in the accessibility statement
  • DRemove it only on buttons, since buttons are obvious
Answer: A. :focus-visible exists exactly for this: it resolves the aesthetic objection without removing the indicator keyboard, switch and screen-reader users depend on. 17
Under WCAG 2.2 AA, what is the minimum contrast ratio for the border of a text input and for a focus indicator?
  • A4.5:1, the same as body text
  • B7:1
  • C3:1, because they are non-text UI components
  • DThere is no requirement for non-text elements

Which is the correct alt text for a magnifying-glass icon inside a button that opens site search?
  • Aalt="magnifying glass icon"
  • Balt="" on the image, with the button given the accessible name "Search"
  • CNo alt attribute at all
  • Dalt="search.svg"
Answer: B. For a functional image the accessible name must describe the action, and it belongs on the interactive element. Your product scores 71 on the System Usability Scale. What does that mean?
  • A71% of users completed their tasks

  • DThe product meets WCAG AA
Answer: B. SUS produces a 0–100 score that is explicitly not a percentage; roughly 68 is the long-run average across studies, and ~80+ is considered excellent. Its main value is comparison — against your own previous release, or against a competitor measured the same way.

Which is the most defensible reading of "5 users find 85% of usability problems"?
  • AFive participants are sufficient to measure task completion rates reliably
  • BAny five people will find 85% of problems in any product
  • CFive participants per study is the maximum useful number
  • DFor qualitative problem-discovery within one user group and task set; distinct personas need their own participants, and three rounds of five beat one round of fifteen
Answer: D. The figure applies to finding problems, not measuring anything (A is the most common misuse).   • BSearch-to-product-page rate
  • CZero-result search rate
  • DFilter usage per session
Answer: A. A guardrail metric protects against winning your primary metric in a harmful way.   • DRestart the test with a larger sample
Answer: C. Peeking and stopping at significance is the most common way teams ship changes that turn out to do nothing — with repeated looks, a "significant" result will appear by chance eventually. An AI feature drafts replies to customer messages. Which combination best supports appropriate trust?

Part B — Written answers

Answer in 2–4 sentences unless told otherwise. Write your answer first, then reveal the model answer and mark yourself against the listed points.

B1.Define UI design and UX design in one sentence each, and state one metric you would use to evaluate each. [3 marks]
Model answer UI design is the craft of the visual and interactive layer — typography, colour, spacing, components and their states — so that a product is legible, predictable and pleasant to operate. UX design is the practice of shaping the whole journey toward a user's goal, covering research, information architecture, flows and recovery, so that the goal is achievable with acceptable effort.
  • UI metric: contrast-ratio compliance, design-system component adoption, or visual QA defect count.
  • UX metric: task success rate, time on task, funnel completion, or SUS.
Marking: 1 mark each definition, 1 mark for two appropriate and distinct metrics.
B2.Explain the difference between an affordance and a signifier, and give one interface example where the affordance exists but the signifier is missing. [3 marks]
Model answer An affordance is what an element makes possible given its properties and the user's capabilities; a signifier is the perceivable cue that communicates that possibility. Affordances can exist invisibly — a signifier is what makes them discoverable.
  • Example: a card in a list that is clickable but has no hover state, no cursor change, no chevron and no visual boundary. The affordance (it navigates) exists; the signifier does not, so users never click it.
  • Another: a swipe-to-delete gesture with no visual hint — invisible to everyone who was not told.
Marking: 1 mark per definition, 1 mark for a valid example where the two come apart.
B3.State Tesler's Law and describe one checkout example where complexity was moved from the user to the system. [3 marks]
Model answer Tesler's Law (conservation of complexity) states that every process has an irreducible amount of complexity that cannot be removed — only relocated. The design decision is who absorbs it: the user, the interface, or the engineering system.
  • Example: instead of asking the user to select their card brand, the system detects it from the leading digits, formats the number with spacing automatically, and shows the brand inline.
  • Equally valid: auto-filling city and state from a PIN/ZIP code; detecting country from locale; normalising a phone number typed with spaces or dashes.
Marking: 2 marks for an accurate statement including "cannot be eliminated, only moved", 1 for a valid example.
B4.Why is it wrong to say "Miller's Law means a menu must have at most 7 items"? Give a situation where Miller's Law does genuinely apply in an interface. [3 marks]
Model answer Miller's finding concerns the number of chunks a person can hold in working memory, not the number of items that can be displayed. Visible options are being read, not memorised, so a well-grouped long menu does not violate it. Modern work (Cowan) puts the practical limit nearer 4±1 under load, which is lower still.
  • Where it genuinely applies: requiring a user to carry information across screens — reading an OTP on one screen and typing it on another, remembering a reference number from a confirmation page, or filters that reset when the user navigates back and must be reconstructed from memory.
Marking: 2 marks for the memory-vs-display distinction, 1 for a valid cross-screen memory example.
B5.List the eight interface states a production screen should have designs for, and explain why the empty state actually needs three separate designs. [4 marks]
Model answer Ideal, empty, loading, partial, error, overflow, success, and permission/access-denied.
  • First-use empty — nothing exists yet. Job: explain the value and give one clear action to create the first item.
  • User-cleared empty — they finished or deleted everything. Job: acknowledge the achievement and offer undo; a "get started" prompt here is patronising.
  • No-results empty — a filter or search returned nothing. Job: show what was searched, identify which filter caused it, and offer a way to relax or clear it.
Marking: 2 marks for six or more states, 2 marks for the three distinct empty variants with different jobs.
B6.Write an improved version of this error message and explain the three things a good error message must convey: "Error: invalid input." [3 marks]
Model answer A usable error message states what happened, why, and what to do next, in plain language, positioned next to the thing that caused it, without blaming the user.
  • Rewrite: "That PIN code doesn't match the selected state. PIN codes are 6 digits — check the code, or change the state."
  • Placement matters as much as wording: inline beside the field, with focus moved to it on submit, plus a summary at the top of a long form linking to each error.
Marking: 1 mark per component correctly named, with a rewrite that demonstrates all three.
B7.Explain the three-tier design token model and why the middle tier is what makes theming possible. [3 marks]
Model answer Tier 1 primitives hold raw values (--blue-500: #2f80ed) and are never referenced directly by components. Tier 2 semantic tokens express meaning (--color-action-primary) and point at primitives. Tier 3 component tokens scope values to one component for genuine exceptions.
  • The semantic tier is the indirection layer: because components reference meaning rather than colour, a theme is applied by redefining tier 2 only. Dark mode, a white-label client theme, or a rebrand becomes a data change instead of a code change across every component.
Marking: 2 marks for the three tiers with an example each, 1 mark for the indirection argument.
B8.Give the WCAG 2.2 AA contrast minimums for (a) body text, (b) large text, (c) a UI component border, and name two situations that pass an automated checker yet still fail real users. [3 marks]
Model answer (a) 4.5:1   (b) 3:1 for text ≥24px, or ≥18.66px bold   (c) 3:1 for non-text UI components and graphical objects.
  • Text over an image — the ratio varies per pixel and checkers sample a flat background; fix with a scrim or a solid plate.
  • Disabled controls — formally exempt from the requirement, so they pass, but an unreadable disabled button with no explanation is still a usability failure.
  • Also acceptable: placeholder-as-label, or colour-only status indicators that meet contrast but fail 1.4.1 Use of Colour.
Marking: 1.5 marks for all three ratios, 1.5 for two valid gaps.
B9.Explain why "the first rule of ARIA is don't use ARIA", and give a case where ARIA is the right tool. [3 marks]
Model answer Native HTML elements come with correct role, keyboard behaviour, focusability and platform conventions already implemented and tested. Recreating them with ARIA on a <div> means reimplementing all of that by hand, and most implementations miss something — Space vs Enter, focus management, or disabled semantics. Incorrect ARIA actively misinforms assistive technology and is worse than none.
  • Where ARIA is right: communicating things HTML has no element for — aria-live regions announcing dynamic updates such as a filter result count, aria-expanded on a custom disclosure, aria-describedby linking a field to its error text, or role="alert" for an urgent message.
Marking: 2 marks for the native-semantics argument, 1 for a correct ARIA use case.
B10.A stakeholder says "let's just A/B test it" for every design question. Give two categories of question that A/B testing cannot answer, and say what you would use instead. [3 marks]
Model answer
  • "Why did this happen?" — A/B tests report which variant performed better, never the cause. Use moderated usability testing, session replay or interviews to find the mechanism.
  • "What should we build that doesn't exist yet?" — testing can only compare options you already thought of, so it optimises toward a local maximum. Use generative research: interviews, contextual inquiry, JTBD analysis.
  • Also valid: low-traffic products where a test can never reach adequate power; long-horizon effects such as trust or retention that a two-week test cannot capture; and anything unethical to withhold, such as an accessibility fix.
Marking: 1 mark per category with a named alternative, 1 for the local-maximum or power argument.
B11.Distinguish persuasive design from manipulative design, and give one test you can apply to a specific pattern. [3 marks]
Model answer Persuasive design makes a genuinely good option easier to choose while leaving the user's understanding and freedom intact. Manipulative design (deceptive design / dark patterns) produces a choice the user would not have made had they understood what was happening — through concealment, asymmetric effort, or false information.
  • Test 1: would the user still choose this if they fully understood the mechanism? If not, it is manipulation.
  • Test 2: could you explain the design rationale out loud to the user in the moment without embarrassment?
  • Test 3 (structural): is the effort to decline symmetrical with the effort to accept?
Marking: 2 marks for a definition contrasting informed vs uninformed choice, 1 for a usable test.
B12.Name the three Core Web Vitals, state a good threshold for each, and name one design decision that damages each one. [3 marks]
Model answer
  • LCP — Largest Contentful Paint, good at ≤2.5s. Damaged by an oversized unoptimised hero image, a render-blocking web font, or an above-the-fold carousel.
  • INP — Interaction to Next Paint, good at ≤200ms. Damaged by long unvirtualised lists and expensive re-rendering on every keystroke in a filter or search box.
  • CLS — Cumulative Layout Shift, good at ≤0.1. Damaged by images without declared dimensions, banners injected above existing content, and skeletons whose layout differs from the loaded result.
Marking: 1 mark per vital with a correct threshold and a genuine design-side cause.

Part C — Applied scenarios

Longer answers. Aim for structured reasoning: identify the problem, name the principle, propose a fix, and say how you would know it worked.

C1.Your analytics show that 62% of users who reach the payment step do not complete it. You have one week and no budget for research recruitment. Describe what you would do, in order, and justify each step. [6 marks]
Model answer
  • 1. Segment the data first. Split by device, browser, payment method, new vs returning, and country. A 62% drop that is really "94% on mobile Safari" is a bug, not a design problem — and that distinction changes everything you do next.
  • 2. Read what you already have. Support tickets and chat transcripts from the last 30 days are free qualitative data. Look for repeated phrases.
  • 3. Instrument error events. If payment failures are not logged by reason, add that — you may be looking at declines, not abandonment.
  • 4. Walk the flow yourself on a mid-range Android with throttled 3G, keyboard-only, and with a screen reader. Log issues against Nielsen heuristics with 0–4 severity.
  • 5. Run a 5-person guerrilla test — colleagues outside the product team, or an unmoderated tool. Enough to find the catastrophes.
  • 6. Fix the severity-4 items and ship. Likely candidates given the pattern: costs revealed late, forced account creation, an unrecoverable error state, session expiry clearing the form, wrong mobile keyboards.
  • 7. Define the measurement before shipping: payment-step completion rate as the primary metric, with refunds and support contacts as guardrails, measured over full weekly cycles.
Marking: 1 mark each for segmenting before designing, using existing qualitative sources, self-audit including accessibility and slow network, small-n testing, prioritising by severity, and defining measurement with a guardrail.
C2.A product manager asks you to add a countdown timer showing "Offer ends in 09:58" that resets every time the page loads, and a "Only 2 left in stock!" label that is not connected to inventory. Respond as a designer. [5 marks]
Model answer
  • Name it accurately: both are false urgency / false scarcity — deceptive design patterns, not persuasion, because the information is untrue.
  • Regulatory exposure: fabricated urgency and scarcity claims are explicitly targeted by consumer-protection regulators (EU DSA and UCPD, FTC, and CCPA guidelines in India). This is a legal risk, not only an ethical one.
  • Commercial argument: the pattern works only while unnoticed. A resetting timer is trivially detectable — one refresh — and discovery damages trust in every other claim on the page, including your genuine ones.
  • Offer the real alternative: connect the label to actual inventory and show it only when the count is genuinely low; use real, non-resetting deadlines for real promotions; show honest social proof such as verified purchase counts.
  • Propose a test: run honest scarcity against no scarcity and measure conversion plus returns and repeat-purchase rate. Framing it as a measurable question usually resolves the disagreement without it becoming a values argument.
Marking: 1 mark each for correct naming, the legal point, the trust/commercial argument, a concrete honest alternative, and proposing measurement rather than refusing flatly.
C3.An engineer says the new dashboard cannot use a design-system table because "our data is different", and builds a custom one. Three months later there are four custom tables. Diagnose and propose a process fix. [5 marks]
Model answer
  • Diagnosis: this is a symptom, not a discipline problem. Repeated divergence usually means the component API is too rigid for real needs, or the system is not discoverable, or the contribution path is too slow to be worth using.
  • Investigate the first divergence specifically: what did the data actually require — column pinning, virtualisation, nested rows, per-cell editing? That requirement is now proven four times over.
  • Process fix: establish a federated contribution model with a lightweight RFC. Product teams propose extensions, the system team reviews and owns the merge, with a published response-time commitment so the queue is not the reason people go around it.
  • Add escape hatches deliberately: a small documented set of override slots is far better than either total rigidity or uncontrolled className injection.
  • Measure adoption: track component-adoption percentage and count hard-coded values and detached instances. Falling adoption is an early warning that the system is drifting away from what teams need.
  • Repay the debt: extend the shared table to cover the proven cases, then migrate the four custom ones — a plan with a schedule, not an aspiration.
Marking: 1 mark each for treating it as a system failure rather than blaming the engineer, identifying the real requirement, a contribution/RFC process, documented escape hatches, and adoption measurement.
C4.You are designing an AI feature that suggests a shipping address from a user's past orders. List the interface decisions you would make and the principle behind each. [6 marks]
Model answer
  • Suggest, never auto-apply — the address is pre-filled but clearly marked as a suggestion with a one-tap way to change it. User control and freedom (Nielsen #3).
  • Show provenance — "Used for your order on 12 Aug". Visibility of system state; supports trust calibration.
  • Make the alternative equally easy — "Deliver somewhere else" must be a peer action, not buried. Avoids obstruction; symmetrical effort.
  • Full address visible, not summarised — showing "Home" alone invites a costly mis-delivery. Recognition over recall (#6); error prevention (#5).
  • Handle the cold start — with no history there is no suggestion, so design that empty state as the normal manual entry, not a degraded one. Empty-state design.
  • Accessibility — the suggestion is announced via a polite live region, is keyboard reachable, and its accepted state is programmatically conveyed. Robust / Operable.
  • Privacy — do not surface an address from a shared or corporate account without confirmation, and make it clear the data came from the user's own history. Ethical use of user data.
  • Measure it — suggestion acceptance rate as the primary metric, with mis-delivery and address-correction rates as guardrails.
Marking: up to 6 marks, 1 per decision correctly paired with a principle. Answers that only list features without naming principles cap at 3.
C5.Perform a mini heuristic evaluation. A cart page has: a "Remove" link 12px tall directly beside "Move to wishlist"; no confirmation and no undo on remove; a coupon field that shows "Invalid code" with no explanation; a total that excludes shipping until checkout; and a spinner overlaying the whole page for 3 seconds on every quantity change. Identify the issues, name the heuristic, and assign severity. [6 marks]
Model answer
  • Sev 4 — Total excludes shipping until checkout. Nielsen #1 Visibility of system state; also drip pricing. It sits on 100% of purchase paths and is the top cart-abandonment cause. Fix: estimate shipping in the cart from PIN/ZIP and show the full total.
  • Sev 3 — Remove has no undo and no confirmation. #3 User control and freedom; #5 Error prevention. Fix: keep the immediate removal but show a toast with Undo for ~10 seconds.
  • Sev 3 — 12px target adjacent to a similar action. Fitts's Law; #5 Error prevention. Fix: minimum 44px targets, visible separation between the two, and different visual weight since one is destructive.
  • Sev 3 — "Invalid code" with no cause. #9 Help users recognise, diagnose and recover. Fix: state the actual reason — expired, minimum-spend not met, not valid on sale items — and what to do about it.
  • Sev 2/3 — Full-page blocking spinner for 3s on quantity change. #1 Visibility of state, Doherty threshold. Fix: optimistic update of the line and the total with an inline spinner on that row only; roll back with a toast if the request fails; make the request idempotent.
  • Cross-cutting: the removal has no live-region announcement, so screen-reader users are not told the item is gone. WCAG 4.1.3 Status Messages.
Marking: 1 mark per issue correctly identified with a named heuristic and a plausible severity; 1 bonus mark for spotting the accessibility gap not stated in the brief.
C6.Your company must make an existing e-commerce site WCAG 2.2 AA compliant in one quarter. Outline your plan, including how you would prioritise and how you would prevent regression. [6 marks]
Model answer
  • 1. Audit by journey, not by page. Cover the critical paths first — search → product → cart → checkout → order tracking. A perfectly accessible About page is worth nothing if checkout is unusable.
  • 2. Combine automated and manual. Automated scanning (axe, Lighthouse) catches roughly a third of issues. Add keyboard-only walkthroughs, screen-reader passes on every form error path, 200%/400% zoom, and a 320px reflow check.
  • 3. Prioritise by blocker severity × journey criticality. Anything that makes a task impossible — keyboard traps, unlabelled form fields, missing focus indicators, inaccessible modals — ranks above anything cosmetic.
  • 4. Fix at the system layer where possible. Correcting contrast in the semantic token layer and fixing focus styling, button semantics and modal focus management in shared components repairs hundreds of instances at once. This is the highest-leverage move available.
  • 5. Address the WCAG 2.2 additions specifically: focus not obscured by the sticky header and cookie banner, target sizes, dragging alternatives for any slider or reorder control, no re-entry of data already given, and paste allowed in password fields.
  • 6. Prevent regression: automated a11y checks in CI, accessibility acceptance criteria in every ticket definition-of-done, a11y items in the design-QA checklist, and component-level tests for keyboard and focus behaviour.
  • 7. Involve disabled users. Compliance is the floor, not the goal — testing with screen-reader users finds problems no checklist contains.
Marking: 1 mark each for journey-based scoping, combined automated + manual method, severity-based prioritisation, fixing at the design-system layer, naming specific 2.2 criteria, and a concrete regression-prevention mechanism.

Final takeaway: tools, frameworks and platforms turn over every few years. The constraints do not — working memory is still small, attention is still expensive, people still need to know what just happened and how to undo it, and a product that excludes people excludes revenue. Every advanced technique in this course is one of those constraints, applied carefully to a harder problem. Learn the constraints and you can design for any medium, including the ones that don't exist yet.
