    Module 01

    Technical Interface for Product Owners

    How a Product Owner turns broad business requirements into clear technical tasks developers can build: decomposition, technical literacy, specifications and collaboration.

    🏛️ Course Overview: What the Role Needs

    The technical interface of a Product Owner (PO) is where business intent meets engineering reality. You translate broad requirements into clear, testable, technically informed work, and you keep a continuous conversation with developers about options and trade-offs.

    🧩
    Decomposition
    Break business goals into epics, stories and tasks.
    🔌
    Technical literacy
    Understand APIs, data, environments and release pipelines.
    📐
    Specifications
    Write clear requirements, contracts and non-functional needs.
    🤝
    Developer partnership
    Make trade-offs and decisions together.
    💡
    You do not need to write code, but you do need to speak enough technology to ask the right questions and write requirements engineers can trust.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    By the end of this course you will be able to:

    1. Decompose a business requirement into epics, stories and tasks with traceability.
    2. Explain core technical concepts: APIs, databases, architecture, environments, CI/CD.
    3. Write technical stories and spikes with clear acceptance criteria.
    4. Specify API contracts, data needs and non-functional requirements at PO level.
    5. Collaborate with developers and architects on estimates, trade-offs and technical debt.
    6. Manage dependencies and integrations across teams.
    7. Check quality, security and observability expectations before release.

    ✂️ Intermediate: Vertical Slicing and Traceability

    ✅ Vertical slices

    Each slice delivers end-to-end value through UI, logic and data; linked to the epic and goal; testable on its own; can be released behind a flag.

    ❌ Horizontal layers

    Horizontal layers ("build database", then "build API", then "build UI"); no user value until the end; no link to goal; huge stories.

  Epic E2: Faster payment methods
    Story S4: Wallet payment

    Module 04

    Writing Technical Stories, Spikes & Specs

    Writing stories that carry technical detail: data, API notes, NFRs, spikes, technical enablers and decision records.

    📝 Story with Technical Notes

    textSTORY: Guest checkout
As a shopper, I want to pay with only my email so that I can buy without creating an account.

Acceptance criteria
  AC1 Valid email + address + payment → order created, confirmation within 60 s.
  AC2 Invalid email → inline error; no order created.
  AC3 Declined card → "Payment declined" and cart kept.

Technical notes
  - New endpoint POST /orders/guest (reuse payment service)
  - Store guest email in orders table; link to account if created later
  - NFR: p95 response < 800 ms; idempotent on retry
  - Analytics events: checkout_started, order_completed
  - Security: rate-limit by IP; no card data stored
Dependencies: payment service v2 (Team Pay); Risk: fraud on guest orders
    📋
    Criteria
    Testable behaviour.
    🔧
    Technical notes
    Hints, not orders.
    ⚙️
    NFRs
    Performance, security.
    🔗
    Dependencies
    Teams and APIs.

    🤝 Technical Refinement

    Figure 5.1 · Technical refinement conversation
    Developer saysYou ask
    "That is hard.""What makes it hard? What would make it easier?"
    "We need to refactor first.""What risk or cost does it remove? What is the smallest version?"
    "It depends.""On what? Can we split to isolate the dependency?"
    "That will take 3 weeks.""What drives it? Is there a smaller slice?"

    ⚖️ Trade-offs and Estimates

    LeverEffect
    Reduce scopeFaster; delivers less
    Reduce qualityFaster; debt and risk — avoid for core
    Add peopleNot always faster (coordination cost)
    Move dateMore time; may miss value
    Reuse / buyFaster; dependency and cost
    Figure 5.2 · Elements of a technical trade-off conversation
    ⚠️
    An estimate is a forecast with uncertainty. Pressuring for smaller numbers produces padding or hidden debt.

    Real-World Scenarios & Troubleshooting

    Case studies: a vague business request, developers rejecting a story as unclear, a surprise dependency and a technical-debt standoff.

    🌫️ Case Study: "Make It Like the Competitor"

    A VP asks for "a dashboard like Competitor X" with no further detail.

    Figure 8.1 · Clarifying a vague request
    ✅
    Outcome: the real need was weekly sales visibility; a 3-metric dashboard shipped in one sprint, then grew.

    ❓ Troubleshooting: Developers Say the Story Is Unclear

    1. Listen to what is unclear: need, rules, data, edge cases.
    2. Add examples and acceptance criteria; draw the flow.
    3. Run a short clarification with the developer and tester.
    4. Update the story; improve Definition of Ready.
    Figure 8.2 · Fixing an unclear story

    🧱 Troubleshooting: A Surprise Dependency

    StepAction
    1Assess impact on sprint goal and release
    2Talk to the other team; agree interface and date
    3Mock or stub to continue
    4Re-order or re-scope if needed; inform stakeholders
    5Add to dependency map and refinement checklist

    ⚖️ Trade-off Scenario: Tech Debt vs Features

    Engineers want two sprints to refactor the order module; sales want three features. Quantify the debt's cost (incidents, slowdown), propose a split (for example 20% capacity each sprint), tie refactoring to upcoming features, and agree measures such as cycle time and incident count.

    Module 11

    Interview Preparation

    Questions asked in Product Owner interviews about the technical interface, grouped by level. Draft your answer, then compare with the model answer.

    🌱 Beginner Interview Questions

    IQ1.How do you translate business requirements into developer tasks?
    Clarify goal and users, break into epics and thin vertical stories with acceptance criteria, add technical notes and NFRs, refine with developers, and let them break stories into tasks. Keep traceability to the goal.
    IQ2.What technical concepts should a PO understand?
    How frontend, backend, APIs and databases fit together, environments and CI/CD, performance and security basics, and the meaning of tech debt, spikes and NFRs.
    IQ3.What is a spike?
    A time-boxed investigation to answer a specific question or reduce uncertainty. It ends with findings and a decision, not a feature.

    🛠️ Intermediate Interview Questions

    IQ4.What is the difference between a story and a task?
    A story describes user value. Tasks are the technical steps developers use to deliver it. POs own stories; developers own tasks.
    IQ5.What are non-functional requirements?
    Quality attributes such as performance, security, availability, accessibility and scalability, written with measurable thresholds and treated as part of acceptance.
    IQ6.How do you handle a developer who says a story is too big?
    Ask what makes it large, look for the thinnest vertical slice, split by workflow or rule, and agree acceptance for each slice.

    🚀 Advanced Interview Questions

    IQ7.How do you specify an integration with another system?
    Define trigger, data, errors and retries, ownership, testing in a sandbox, and monitoring, and agree the contract early with the other team.
    IQ8.How do you decide when to pay down technical debt?
    Make impact visible (incidents, slowdown), tie it to upcoming work, allocate a steady share of capacity, and review with measures such as cycle time and defects.
    IQ9.How do you work with an architect?
    Share business context (scale, change rate, compliance), ask for options and consequences, decide with them, and record the decision with reasons.

    🏆 Expert Interview Questions

    IQ10.How do you respond to "that will take three weeks"?
    Ask what drives it, explore a smaller slice, check for dependencies or unknowns, and agree on a plan; never pressure for a smaller number.
    IQ11.How do you ensure a feature is production ready?
    Check acceptance and DoD, tests, security and accessibility, monitoring, runbook and rollback, support readiness and a staged rollout plan.
    IQ12.Tell me about a time a vague requirement went well.
    Describe how you asked about goals and users, ran a workshop or prototype, sliced a thin first version, specified criteria with developers, and delivered value early.
