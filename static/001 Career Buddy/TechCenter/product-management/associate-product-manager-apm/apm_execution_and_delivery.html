    Module 01

    Execution & Delivery: The APM Role

    What an APM does to turn ideas into shipped work — clear user stories, functional requirements, bug triage and sprint participation — and how the pieces fit together.

    🏛️ Course Overview: What the Role Is

    Execution & Delivery is where an Associate Product Manager (APM) earns trust. Discovery decides what is worth building; delivery makes sure the team builds it correctly, on time and with shared understanding. Day to day you write user stories and requirements engineers can build from, triage bugs so the right ones get fixed first, and take part in the sprint rituals that keep the team moving.

    📝
    User stories
    Turn a user need into a small, testable piece of work with clear acceptance criteria.
    📐
    Functional requirements
    State exactly what the product must do — inputs, rules, outputs and edge cases.
    🐞
    Bug triage
    Reproduce, classify and prioritise defects by severity, priority and user impact.
    🔁
    Sprint participation
    Join refinement, planning, stand-ups, reviews and retros — and keep the sprint goal clear.
    💡
    A useful mental model: the APM is the team's clarity engine. If engineers, designers and testers can all explain the same story in the same words, delivery is smooth. Most delays come from unclear requirements, not slow coding.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    By the end of this course you will be able to:

    1. Describe where execution and delivery fit in the product lifecycle and what an APM owns in each sprint.
    2. Write user stories in the standard format, check them against INVEST and split large stories into small ones.
    3. Write functional requirements and acceptance criteria that are specific, testable and cover edge cases.
    4. Triage a bug: reproduce it, set severity and priority, assign an owner and decide when it blocks a release.
    5. Participate effectively in refinement, planning, stand-up, review and retrospective meetings.
    6. Use Jira, documentation and communication tools to keep work visible and decisions recorded.
    7. Handle realistic delivery situations: scope creep, a late critical bug, an unclear requirement, a missed sprint goal.

    🗺️ Where the APM Sits in Delivery

    Figure 1.1 · The APM connects every delivery stakeholder

    Before the sprint you refine stories with engineers. During the sprint you clarify questions, accept finished work and triage new bugs. After the sprint you review outcomes and feed lessons into the next one. You do not manage people — you remove ambiguity.

    Functional requirementA statement of what the system must doRemoves guesswork for engineers and testers
    EpicA large body of work split into many storiesKeeps big goals connected to small tasks

    Module 02

    Writing Clear User Stories

    From the basic "As a… I want… so that…" format to INVEST checks, story splitting, story mapping and epics that stay connected to user value.

    📝 Basic: What a User Story Is

    A user story is a short note describing something a user wants to do and why. It is a promise for a conversation, not a full specification: the details come from talking, sketching and the acceptance criteria attached to it.

    textTitle: Save delivery address
Story: As a returning shopper, I want to save my delivery address
       so that I can check out faster next time.
Value: Shorter checkout; fewer abandoned carts.
Notes: Up to 5 addresses; one marked as default.

    ✅ Good story

    The story names a real user, a clear action and a benefit; engineers know why it matters and can suggest better options.

    ❌ Weak story

    The story is a task in disguise ("Add address table") with no user or reason; the team builds it without understanding the value.

    🧱 Basic: Anatomy of a Good Story

    PartQuestion it answersExample
    Who (As a…)Which user or role?Returning shopper
    What (I want…)What action or capability?Save my delivery address
    Why (so that…)What benefit or outcome?Check out faster next time
    Acceptance criteriaHow do we know it is done?Saved address is pre-filled at checkout
    Notes / linksDesigns, rules, dependenciesFigma frame; address limit is 5
    Figure 2.1 · From epic to tasks
    💡
    Write the "so that" first. If you cannot state the benefit, the story may not be worth building yet.

    ✅ Intermediate: The INVEST Checklist

    LetterMeaningQuick test
    I · IndependentCan be built without waiting on another storyDoes it list hidden dependencies?
    N · NegotiableDetails can be discussedIs the how left to the team?
    V · ValuableA user or the business gains somethingCan you name the benefit?
    E · EstimableThe team can size itDo engineers understand it?
    S · SmallFits comfortably in one sprintCan it finish in 2–3 days?
    T · TestableClear pass or failAre criteria measurable?
    gherkinFeature: Save delivery address
  Scenario: Save a new address at checkout
    Given I am logged in with no saved addresses
    When I enter a valid address and tick "Save this address"
    Then the address appears in my saved addresses list
    And it is pre-filled on my next checkout

  Scenario: Address limit reached
    Given I already have 5 saved addresses
    When I try to save a sixth address
    Then I see "You can save up to 5 addresses"
    And no new address is saved

    ✂️ Intermediate: Splitting Large Stories

    Large stories hide risk. Split them into thin vertical slices that each deliver something a user can notice, rather than horizontal layers (database, then API, then screen).

    Figure 2.2 · Splitting by user value
    Splitting patternExample
    By workflow stepAdd item → apply coupon → pay
    By rule or variationDomestic addresses first; international later
    By user roleCustomer view first; admin view later
    By happy path vs edge casesCore flow now; error recovery next
    By data or platformAndroid first; iOS next sprint

    🗺️ Advanced: Story Mapping and Epics

    A story map lays user activities left to right in the order a user does them, with stories stacked below by priority. It shows the whole journey and makes it easy to cut a thin first release.

    Figure 2.3 · User activities across the top
    ActivityBrowseChoosePayTrack
    Release 1 (must)Search by nameAdd to cartPay by cardOrder status page
    Release 2 (should)FiltersSave for laterWalletLive map
    Release 3 (could)RecommendationsCompareSplit paymentDelivery alerts
    📌
    Draw the walking skeleton first: the thinnest complete path from start to finish. It proves the whole flow works before polishing any one step.

    🏆 Expert: Dependencies, Spikes and Technical Stories

    Experts handle the stories that do not look like user stories but still matter.

    TypeWhen to useHow to write it
    SpikeUnknown technical or design questionTime-boxed investigation with a clear question and expected output
    Technical storyRefactor or infrastructure that enables user valueLink it to the user story it supports and the risk it reduces
    Bug storyDefect to fixSteps, expected vs actual, severity, acceptance criteria
    DependencyNeeds another team or story firstName owner and date; plan around it
    • Definition of Ready: only pull a story into a sprint when it is clear, sized, has criteria and has no blocking unknowns.
    • Vertical, not horizontal: prefer stories that cross front end, back end and data so users see value each sprint.

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    Name a real user; state the benefit; keep stories small; write acceptance criteria with the team; include error cases; keep the "how" open; review stories with engineers before planning.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Writing tasks as stories; vague adjectives; giant stories that span sprints; adding requirements after the sprint starts; writing alone and handing it over.

    Module 03

    Functional Requirements

    How to state exactly what the product must do: functional vs non-functional requirements, rules and edge cases, acceptance criteria, requirement documents and traceability.

    📐 Basic: What Functional Requirements Are

    A functional requirement describes what the system must do: its behaviour, rules and outputs. A non-functional requirement describes how well it does it — speed, security, availability, accessibility.

    ⚙️
    Behaviour
    What the system does when a user acts.
    📏
    Rules
    Limits, validations and business logic.
    📤
    Outputs
    Messages, files and screens the user receives.
    🧪
    Testable
    Every statement can pass or fail.
    TypeQuestionExample
    FunctionalWhat must it do?The system shall email a receipt after payment succeeds
    Non-functionalHow well?Receipts are sent within 60 seconds for 99% of orders
    ConstraintWhat limits us?Must support payment providers licensed in India
    💡
    Good requirements are specific, testable, unambiguous and necessary. Test each one with: "How would a tester prove this passes or fails?"

    ✍️ Basic: Writing Requirement Statements

    Use one clear pattern and one idea per statement.

    textFR-12: When a customer places an order, the system shall send a confirmation
       email within 60 seconds containing the order number, items, total and
       estimated delivery date.
FR-13: If the email fails to send, the system shall retry up to 3 times
       and log the failure for support.
    Weak wordingBetter wording
    The system should be fastPages load within 2 seconds for 95% of requests
    Show an error if neededShow "Card declined. Try another card." when the bank rejects payment
    Users can manage their profileUsers can edit name, phone and email; email changes require OTP verification
    Support many usersSupport 5,000 concurrent users without errors above 1%

    🧩 Intermediate: Business Rules and Edge Cases

    Most bugs live in the cases nobody wrote down. For every feature ask: what if it is empty, too long, too many, too late, duplicated, offline or unauthorised?

    Figure 3.1 · Edge case checklist
    FeatureEdge cases to specify
    Apply couponExpired, used already, minimum amount not met, two coupons together
    Upload photoToo large, wrong format, upload interrupted, no storage permission
    Cancel orderAlready shipped, partially refunded, double-tapped cancel

    ✅ Intermediate: Acceptance Criteria That Can Be Tested

    Acceptance criteria turn requirements into pass/fail checks. Two common formats are Given / When / Then and a simple checklist.

    gherkinFeature: Apply coupon
  Scenario: Valid coupon reduces the total
    Given my cart total is 1000
    When I apply coupon "SAVE10"
    Then the total shows 900
    And "SAVE10 applied" is displayed

  Scenario: Expired coupon
    Given coupon "OLD20" expired yesterday
    When I apply "OLD20"
    Then I see "This coupon has expired"
    And my total does not change
    📌
    Include at least one negative scenario per story. Testers and engineers will ask about errors anyway — answer before they have to.

    📄 Advanced: Requirement Documents and Traceability

    For larger features a short requirements section in the PRD keeps everything in one place. Traceability links each requirement to its story, test and release.

    Figure 3.2 · Requirement traceability
    IDRequirementStoryTestStatus
    FR-12Send confirmation email within 60 sST-101TC-41Done
    FR-13Retry failed email 3 timesST-102TC-42In sprint
    FR-14Include invoice PDFST-107TC-47Backlog

    🏆 Expert: Ambiguity, Conflicts and Change Control

    Experts manage requirements as living, negotiated agreements.

    SituationExpert response
    Two requirements conflictTrace both to goals; decide with the PM; update both and tell the team
    Requirement is unclear mid-sprintClarify within a day; record the answer in the story; avoid verbal-only decisions
    New request arrives mid-sprintAssess impact; swap with equal work or move to next sprint; never silently add scope
    Stakeholder asks for a solutionAsk for the underlying need; rewrite as a requirement with a measurable outcome
    textChange request CR-07
Request: Add invoice PDF to confirmation email (Sales)
Impact: +3 days engineering; delays FR-14 by one sprint
Options: (A) include now, drop wallet story  (B) next sprint  (C) link to invoice page
Decision: Option C now, A next sprint (agreed with PM, 2026-10-04)

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    One idea per requirement; measurable wording; examples and edge cases; unique IDs; link to stories and tests; record every change and decision.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Vague adjectives; mixing several rules in one sentence; hidden assumptions; changing requirements verbally; skipping error and empty-state cases.

    Module 04

    Bug Triage

    How an APM handles defects: writing and reading bug reports, reproducing issues, severity vs priority, the triage meeting, SLAs and release-blocking decisions.

    🐞 Basic: What a Bug Is and What Triage Does

    A bug is a difference between what the product should do and what it does. Triage means sorting incoming bugs quickly and fairly so the team fixes the right ones first — like a hospital emergency room deciding who needs care now.

    Figure 4.1 · The triage flow
    💡
    Triage is about decisions, not debugging. You are not fixing the bug; you are deciding how serious it is, who owns it and when it should be fixed.

    📋 Basic: A Good Bug Report

    textTitle: Order total shows 0 after applying coupon on Android
Environment: Android 14, app v5.2.1, mobile data
Steps to reproduce:
  1. Add any item to the cart
  2. Apply coupon SAVE10
  3. Open the cart summary
Expected: Total shows discounted price (900)
Actual: Total shows 0
Frequency: 10 of 10 attempts
Evidence: screen recording, request ID 7f2a
Impact: Customers cannot complete checkout
    FieldWhy it matters
    Clear titleLets the team spot duplicates quickly
    Steps to reproduceEngineers cannot fix what they cannot see
    Expected vs actualDefines the gap
    Environment and versionMany bugs only appear on certain devices or versions
    EvidenceScreenshots, logs or video save a conversation

    ⚖️ Intermediate: Severity vs Priority

    Severity is how bad the impact is. Priority is how soon it should be fixed. They are related but not the same.

    SeverityMeaningExample
    S1 · CriticalSystem down, data loss, security issue or no workaroundPayments fail for all users
    S2 · MajorKey feature broken; workaround existsCoupons fail on Android only
    S3 · MinorSmall issue; most users unaffectedWrong icon on settings page
    S4 · TrivialCosmeticTypo in a rarely seen message
    PriorityMeaningTypical response
    P0Fix nowDrop everything; hot-fix today
    P1Fix this sprintPulled into current sprint
    P2Plan soonNext sprint or two
    P3BacklogFix when convenient
    📌
    A typo on the home page of a flagship launch can be low severity but high priority. A crash in a rarely used admin screen can be high severity but lower priority.

    🧪 Intermediate: Reproducing and Gathering Information

    QuestionWhy ask
    Can I reproduce it? How often?Always vs intermittent changes urgency
    Which users, devices and versions are affected?Sizes the impact
    Since when? What changed?Points to a release or config change
    Is there a workaround?Lowers severity if users can continue
    Is data lost or exposed?May make it critical regardless of count
    sql-- How many users hit the error in the last 24 hours?
SELECT app_version, device_os, COUNT(DISTINCT user_id) AS users_affected
FROM error_events
WHERE error_code = 'COUPON_TOTAL_ZERO'
  AND created_at > now() - interval '24 hours'
GROUP BY app_version, device_os
ORDER BY users_affected DESC;

    🚦 Advanced: Running the Triage Meeting

    A short, regular meeting (15–30 minutes, 2–3 times a week) keeps the queue healthy.

    Figure 4.2 · Triage meeting structure
    RoleResponsibility
    APM / PMImpact on users and business; priority call
    Engineering leadCause, effort and risk of fix
    QAReproduction, test coverage, regression risk
    SupportCustomer volume and complaints

    🏆 Expert: SLAs, Release Blockers and Root Causes

    Experts connect triage to release decisions and prevention.

    SeverityTypical response SLATypical fix SLA
    S1 Critical15 minutesHot-fix within 24 hours
    S2 Major4 hoursWithin the current sprint
    S3 Minor1 business dayWithin 2–3 sprints
    S4 Trivial1 weekBacklog
    Release blocker testQuestion
    Data or money at risk?Loss, corruption, wrong charges or exposure
    Core journey broken?Sign-up, checkout or login unusable
    No safe workaround?Users cannot proceed
    Cannot be turned off?No feature flag or rollback
    • After critical bugs run a blameless root-cause review: why was it introduced, why was it missed, how do we prevent it?
    • Track escape rate (bugs found in production vs before release) to improve testing.

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    Reproduce before classifying; use clear severity definitions; size impact with data; assign an owner and date to every bug; review the queue on a schedule; close the loop with reporters.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Treating every bug as urgent; leaving bugs unowned; debating severity by volume of the reporter; ignoring duplicates; fixing symptoms without root cause.

    Module 05

    Sprint Participation

    How an APM contributes to every sprint ceremony: refinement, planning, stand-up, review and retrospective — plus sprint goals, Definition of Ready and Done, and handling mid-sprint change.

    🔁 Basic: What a Sprint Is

    A sprint is a fixed time box — usually one or two weeks — in which a team plans, builds and reviews a small piece of working product. Short cycles mean quick feedback and fewer big surprises.

    Figure 5.1 · The sprint cycle
    🎯
    Sprint goal
    One sentence describing what the sprint should achieve for users.
    📋
    Sprint backlog
    The stories the team commits to finish.
    📈
    Increment
    Working, tested product delivered at the end.
    🔍
    Transparency
    Everyone can see status without asking.

    🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Basic: Your Role in Each Ceremony

    CeremonyTypical lengthWhat you bringWhat you avoid
    Backlog refinement1 hr/weekClear stories, examples, answers to questionsSurprising the team with brand-new unclear work
    Sprint planning1–2 hrsPriorities and the sprint goalOverloading the sprint
    Daily stand-up15 minBlockers you can clear, decisions madeStatus essays or problem-solving in the meeting
    Sprint review45 minOutcome vs goal; stakeholder feedbackDemoing unfinished work as done
    Retrospective45 minHonest reflection and one concrete changeBlaming individuals

    ✅ Intermediate: Definition of Ready and Definition of Done

    These two checklists protect the team from unclear starts and unclear finishes.

    Definition of Ready (start)Definition of Done (finish)
    Story has a user, benefit and acceptance criteriaCode reviewed and merged
    Designs or examples attached where neededAcceptance criteria tested and passing
    Dependencies identifiedNo open critical or major bugs for the story
    Team has estimated itAnalytics events and documentation updated
    Fits in one sprintProduct owner or APM has accepted it
    💡
    If a story is not ready, say so in refinement. A story that enters the sprint half-clear becomes a mid-sprint argument.

    🎯 Intermediate: Setting a Sprint Goal and Planning Capacity

    A sprint goal gives the team a purpose beyond a list of tickets. It helps decide what to cut when things slip.

    textSprint 14 goal: Returning shoppers can check out in under 3 taps with a saved address.
Committed stories: Save address, Pre-fill address at checkout, Edit/delete address
Stretch: Address limit message
Capacity: 5 engineers x 9 days = 45 days; minus leave/holidays 6; target load 39 points
Risks: Address validation API dependency (owner: Platform team)
    Planning conceptMeaning
    Story pointsRelative size of work — complexity, effort and uncertainty
    VelocityAverage points the team completes per sprint; use for forecasting, not for judging people
    CapacityAvailable working time after holidays, meetings and support duties

    ⚡ Advanced: Handling Mid-Sprint Change and Blockers

    SituationGood response
    Urgent new requestAssess impact with the PM; swap equal work out or defer; tell the team why
    Story is unclear during buildClarify the same day; update the ticket; do not leave answers in chat only
    Dependency is lateEscalate early; re-plan or find a workaround; adjust the goal if needed
    Critical bug foundTriage; decide whether it replaces planned work; communicate the trade-off
    Team will miss the goalSay it early; cut scope, not quality; record what was learned

    Stand-ups become longProblem-solving in the meetingMove to "parking lot" afterwards
    Same retro actions repeatNo follow-throughLimit to 1–3 actions with owners and review them
    • Track a few simple measures: sprint goal hit rate, carry-over, bugs escaped and cycle time per story.
    • Protect the team from interruptions; be the single, clear channel for priorities.

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    Refine stories before planning; set a clear sprint goal; keep stand-ups short; accept work against criteria; make scope changes visible; run retros with one concrete improvement.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Bringing unclear stories to planning; adding work silently; using velocity to compare teams; demoing unfinished work; skipping retros when busy.

    📏 Defect Metrics and Learning from Escapes

    MetricMeaningUse
    Open bugs by severityBacklog healthSpot growing risk
    Escape rateBugs found in production vs before releaseJudge testing strength
    Mean time to fixAverage time from report to fixCheck SLA performance
    Reopen rateBugs reopened after being closedSpot weak fixes or unclear criteria
    sql-- Weekly bugs found in production vs total
SELECT date_trunc('week', created_at) AS week,
       COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE found_in = 'production') AS escaped,
       COUNT(*) AS total,
       ROUND(COUNT(*) FILTER (WHERE found_in = 'production') * 100.0 / NULLIF(COUNT(*), 0), 1) AS escape_pct
FROM bugs
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;

    Real-World Scenarios & Troubleshooting

    Case studies from life as an APM in delivery — rewriting a vague requirement, triaging a late critical bug, handling scope creep and recovering a missed sprint goal.

    📝 Case Study: Rewriting a Vague Request

    Request from Sales: "Make the report export better." Engineers ask what that means.

    Figure 8.1 · From vague request to clear story
    BeforeAfter
    Make export betterAs a finance manager, I want to export reports as CSV with all filters applied so that I can reconcile in Excel
    No criteriaGiven a filtered report of 10,000 rows, When I click Export CSV, Then a file downloads within 30 seconds with the same rows and columns shown
    ✅
    Outcome: the story was sized in one refinement meeting, built in two days and accepted without rework because everyone understood the same words.

    🔥 Troubleshooting: A Critical Bug Two Days Before Release

    Symptom: QA finds that some orders are charged twice when users double-tap Pay. Release is in two days.

    StepCheckFinding
    1Reproduce and countReproduces 6 of 10 times on slow networks
    2SeverityS1: customers charged twice (money at risk)
    3Workaround?None for customers; refunds possible but harmful to trust
    4CausePay button not disabled; no idempotency key
    5DecisionRelease blocker; fix, test, then ship
    💡
    Action: inform the PM and stakeholders immediately with impact and options (delay a day, ship without the feature via flag). Add the missing acceptance criterion and a regression test.

    🤝 Troubleshooting: Scope Creep Mid-Sprint

    1. Write the request as a user problem and ask who asked and why now.
    2. Estimate the effort and risk with engineers.
    3. Show the trade-off: what moves out to make room.
    4. Decide with the PM; if urgent, swap equal-sized work.
    5. Update the sprint goal and board; tell the team and stakeholders.
    ⚠️
    Never say "yes" to protect a relationship and absorb the cost silently. The cost appears later as missed goals and bugs.

    📉 Troubleshooting: The Team Missed the Sprint Goal

    • Check how the goal was set — was it clear and realistic for capacity?
    • Check scope changes, blockers and late dependencies.
    • Review story sizes — were any too large to finish?
    • Look for unclear stories that needed mid-sprint clarification.
    • Agree one concrete change in the retro and review it next sprint.

    ⚖️ Trade-off Scenario: Quality vs Deadline

    Marketing has a fixed launch date, but regression finds two major bugs. As APM you present options: (A) delay two days, (B) ship behind a feature flag to 10% of users with monitoring, (C) ship and hope. You recommend B, document the risk and rollback plan, and agree it with the PM and engineering lead — transparent, reversible and measured.

    Module 11

    Interview Preparation

    Questions asked in APM interviews about execution and delivery, grouped by level. Draft your answer, then compare with the model answer.

    🌱 Beginner Interview Questions

    IQ1.What is a user story and why is it written that way?
    A short description of a user need in the form "As a [user], I want [action] so that [benefit]". It keeps focus on the user and the value instead of the implementation, and it starts a conversation with the team. Acceptance criteria make it testable.
    IQ2.What is the difference between functional and non-functional requirements?
    Functional requirements say what the system must do (send a receipt after payment). Non-functional requirements say how well it must do it (within 60 seconds, secure, accessible). Both must be specific and testable.
    IQ3.What is the difference between severity and priority?
    Severity is the impact of a bug on users or the system. Priority is how soon the team should fix it, considering impact, number of users, workarounds and business timing. A low-severity bug can be high priority and vice versa.

    🛠️ Intermediate Interview Questions

    IQ4.How do you make sure a story is ready for the sprint?
    Check the Definition of Ready: clear user and benefit, acceptance criteria with edge cases, designs attached if needed, dependencies known, sized by the team and small enough for one sprint. Review it with engineers in refinement before planning.
    IQ5.Walk me through how you triage a new bug.
    Reproduce it and gather environment and evidence, size the impact with data, set severity, decide priority with the team, check for duplicates and workarounds, assign an owner and target date, and update the reporter. Escalate immediately if money, data or security is at risk.
    IQ6.How do you write good acceptance criteria?
    Use Given / When / Then or a checklist; make each criterion measurable; cover happy path, error cases and edge cases; avoid vague words; review with engineers and testers; keep them attached to the story as the shared definition of done.

    🚀 Advanced Interview Questions

    IQ7.A stakeholder asks for a new feature mid-sprint. What do you do?
    Understand the need and urgency, estimate impact with engineering, show the trade-off (what moves out), decide with the PM, keep the sprint goal clear, record the decision and communicate it to the team and the stakeholder. Never absorb it silently.
    IQ8.How would you split a very large story?
    Find the thinnest vertical slice that delivers user value end to end, then add slices by workflow step, rule variation, user role or platform. Each slice should be testable and fit in a sprint. Defer the nice-to-have variations.
    IQ9.Two critical bugs arrive on the same day. How do you decide?
    Compare user impact, money or data risk, availability of workarounds, number of users affected and release timing. Fix the one with the larger harm first, assign both owners, communicate status and next update time to stakeholders, and consider a feature flag or rollback.

    🏆 Expert Interview Questions

    IQ10.Your team keeps carrying stories over to the next sprint. What do you investigate?
    Look at commitment vs capacity, story size, unclear requirements, late dependencies, mid-sprint scope changes and bugs. Use data on carry-over and cycle time, discuss it in the retro, and try one or two changes such as stricter Definition of Ready, smaller stories and a lower commitment.
    IQ11.How do you decide whether a bug blocks a release?
    Ask whether money, data or security is at risk, whether a core journey is broken, whether users have a safe workaround and whether the issue can be turned off with a flag. If yes to risk and no to workaround, it blocks. Document the decision and mitigation.
    IQ12.How do you handle disagreement with an engineer about a requirement?
    Seek to understand their concern, return to the user problem and acceptance criteria, bring data or a quick prototype, discuss options with trade-offs, decide with the PM if needed, and record the decision in the ticket. Keep it about the product, not people.
