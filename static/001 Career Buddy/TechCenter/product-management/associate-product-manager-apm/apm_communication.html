    Module 01

    Communication for Product Managers

    Why communication is the APM's main tool, the three skills in this course — coordinating engineering and design, presenting, and listening — and how they fit together.

    🏛️ Course Overview: What the Role Needs

    An Associate Product Manager has influence without authority. You do not manage the engineers or designers who build the product, so your results depend on how clearly you listen, explain, align and present. Communication here means getting the right information to the right people, in the right form, early enough to act on it.

    🤝
    Cross-functional coordination
    Keep engineering and design aligned on the problem, scope and timing.
    🎤
    Presentation design
    Turn analysis and plans into clear, persuasive slides and stories.
    👂
    Active listening
    Understand what people mean, including what they do not say.
    ✍️
    Written clarity
    Specs, updates and decisions people can act on without a meeting.
    💡
    A useful rule: if it matters, say it twice in two forms — once live, once in writing. The meeting creates understanding; the document creates memory.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    By the end of this course you will be able to:

    1. Use active listening techniques to uncover real needs, concerns and constraints.
    2. Coordinate work between engineering and design from discovery through release.
    3. Run efficient meetings, hand-offs and design and technical reviews.
    4. Structure a presentation around one message, an audience and a decision.
    5. Design clear slides, charts and demos that people remember.
    6. Write concise updates, briefs and decision records.
    7. Handle disagreement, bad news and misalignment without damaging trust.

    Module 02

    Active Listening

    Listening to understand: techniques, questions, reading concerns, taking notes, and using listening in user interviews, stand-ups and difficult conversations.

    👂 Basic: What Active Listening Is

    Active listening means giving full attention, understanding the meaning and checking that you understood. It is different from waiting for your turn to speak or from listening only for facts you want.

    Figure 2.1 · Active listening cycle
    💡
    Aim to talk about 30% and listen 70% in discovery conversations. The best question is often the second one.

    🧱 Basic: Core Techniques

    TechniqueWhat to doExample
    PauseWait two seconds before replyingLet the person finish the thought
    ParaphraseRestate in your own words"So the main blocker is the unclear API timing?"
    Label feelingName the emotion you notice"It sounds frustrating that scope changed again."
    Open questionAsk what, how, tell me more"What happened the last time you tried this?"
    SummariseClose with the key points and next steps"We agreed A, B and I will send notes today."
    SilenceAllow space for more detailSay nothing for a moment after a short answer
    📌
    Avoid solutions too early. Jumping to a fix tells the person you stopped listening.

    ❓ Intermediate: Asking Better Questions

    ✅ Good questions

    Open: "How do you do this today?"; Specific past event: "Tell me about the last time"; Follow-up: "Why was that important?"; Neutral wording; One question at a time.

    ❌ Weak questions

    Leading: "Don't you think the new design is better?"; Closed only: "Is it fine?"; Multiple questions at once; Assuming the answer; Interrupting.

    🔬 Intermediate: Listening in User Interviews

    • Prepare 5–7 open questions and a goal; do not read them like a script.
    • Ask about behaviour (what they did) rather than opinions about the future.
    • Notice emotion, hesitation and workarounds.
    • Capture exact quotes; separate observation from your interpretation.
    • End with "Is there anything I did not ask that I should have?"
    textINTERVIEW NOTES
Participant: Operations manager, 50-person company
Goal: Understand how approvals are done today
Observed: Uses spreadsheet + chat to chase approvals
Quote: "I never know who is holding things up."
Interpretation (mine): Needs visibility of approval status
Follow-up: Ask how often approvals are late

    🗣️ Intermediate: Listening to Engineers and Designers

    SignalWhat it may meanWhat to do
    "That is hard."High effort, risk or unclear scopeAsk what makes it hard and what would make it easier
    "It depends."Missing information or a trade-offAsk what it depends on
    Silence in a meetingDisagreement or confusionAsk quietly one-to-one
    "We always do it this way."A constraint or habitAsk for the reason behind it
    A designer defends a screen stronglyAttachment to a decision or a real usability concernAsk which user problem it solves

    🧠 Advanced: Listening Under Pressure and Disagreement

    Figure 2.2 · Handling a heated conversation

    When someone is upset, do not defend yet. Acknowledge the concern, ask for the facts, repeat what you heard and only then explain your view. People accept a different decision more easily when they feel heard.

    ✅
    Script: "Let me check I understand. You are worried that cutting this step will increase support tickets. Is that right? What would you want us to watch after launch?"

    🏆 Expert: Hearing What Is Not Said

    SkillExpert practice
    Read contextConsider workload, past decisions and politics behind a statement
    Separate position from interestA demand ("add this now") hides a need ("I must show progress")
    Track commitmentsWrite down what each person said they would do
    Listen across channelsFollow chat, tickets and meeting notes for signals
    Reflect on biasCheck whether you listen less to people who disagree

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    Pause before replying; paraphrase key points; ask open questions; note commitments; follow up in writing; listen for needs behind demands.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Interrupting; planning your reply while they talk; solving too early; checking your phone; asking leading questions; arguing facts before feelings.

    Module 03

    Coordinating with Engineering

    Working with engineers: sharing context, scoping, estimates, dependencies, technical conversations, hand-offs and keeping everyone informed through the build.

    🤝 Basic: How Engineers Like to Be Worked With

    Engineers build under constraints you cannot always see. They do their best work when they understand the user problem, the goal and the limits, and have room to propose the solution.

    Engineers valueAPM behaviour
    Clear problem and goalExplain why, not just what
    Stable scopeAvoid mid-sprint changes without trade-offs
    Focus timeBatch questions; respect deep work
    Honest estimatesNever press for a number you then treat as a promise
    Respect for craftAsk about trade-offs before asking for speed
    Figure 3.2 · Habits for working with engineers

    📄 Basic: The One-Page Brief

    textFEATURE BRIEF
Problem: Shoppers abandon checkout when asked to create an account (38% drop).
Goal: Reduce checkout abandonment by 10% in Q4.
Users: First-time shoppers on mobile.
Scope (in): Guest checkout with email only.
Scope (out): Loyalty points, saved cards.
Success metric: Checkout conversion; guardrail: payment errors.
Design: Figma link
Open questions: Email verification needed?
Owner: APM; Engineering lead: Sam; Design: Priya
Date: Kick-off Oct 12; target release Nov 4
    💡
    Share the brief before the kick-off so the meeting is spent on questions, not reading.

    🚀 Intermediate: Kick-off, Refinement and Hand-off

    Figure 3.1 · Working with engineering through delivery
    MomentYour roleOutput
    Kick-offExplain problem and goal; invite concernsRisks list and open questions
    RefinementClarify stories with acceptance criteriaReady stories with estimates
    Mid-buildAnswer questions quickly; protect scopeDecisions in the ticket
    ReviewCheck against criteriaAccepted work or clear rework list
    ReleaseCoordinate comms and supportRelease note and monitoring plan

    🧮 Intermediate: Estimates, Dependencies and Trade-offs

    SituationDoAvoid
    Engineers give a large estimateAsk what drives it; explore a smaller first sliceSay "it should be easy"
    Estimate changesAsk what was learned; update the planBlame
    Dependency on another teamName it early; agree dates in writingHope it works out
    Scope must shrinkOffer options with trade-offsQuietly cut quality
    ⚠️
    An estimate is a forecast with uncertainty, not a commitment. Treating it as a promise teaches engineers to pad estimates.

    🛠️ Advanced: Technical Conversations Without Being Technical

    • Ask "What are the options and what do they cost?" rather than prescribing a solution.
    • Learn the vocabulary: API, database, latency, tech debt, rollback, feature flag.
    • Ask for a plain-language summary and an analogy.
    • Understand trade-offs: speed vs quality vs scope vs risk.
    • Write down decisions and the reason, so they can be revisited.
    textDECISION RECORD
Decision: Use email-only guest checkout in v1; defer saved cards.
Options: (A) full guest + saved cards 6 weeks, (B) email-only 2 weeks.
Reason: B delivers 80% of the value in a third of the time.
Risks: Support asks for saved cards; revisit after 4 weeks of data.
Decided by: PM, Eng lead, Design lead on Oct 14

    🏆 Expert: Handling Incidents, Delays and Pushback

    SituationExpert response
    Release at riskTell stakeholders early with options: cut scope, move date, add help
    Production incidentSingle channel, clear owner, regular updates; blameless review later
    Engineer disagrees with a requirementReturn to the user problem; test with data or a prototype; decide with the PM
    Repeated scope changesMake cost visible; agree a change process
    Tech debt blocks featuresQuantify the cost; negotiate a planned share of capacity

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    Share context early; write briefs and decisions down; ask about options; respect focus time; update stakeholders before they ask; thank engineers publicly.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Dictating solutions; changing scope silently; interrupting for status; treating estimates as promises; surprising engineers in front of stakeholders.

    Module 04

    Coordinating with Design

    Working with designers and researchers: sharing the problem, design critique, feasibility, hand-off, design systems and keeping design and engineering in sync.

    🎨 Basic: How Designers Like to Be Worked With

    Designers solve user problems through structure, flow and visual detail. They need the problem, the user and the constraints — not a finished screen drawn by the PM.

    Designers valueAPM behaviour
    A clear problem and userShare research, data and goals
    Time to exploreAsk for options, not one answer
    Feedback on goalsGive feedback as user and business impact, not taste
    Early involvementBring design in at discovery, not after the spec
    Credit and respectPresent design work with the designer's reasoning
    💡
    Replace "I don't like it" with "I'm worried users won't notice the next step. What if we tested it?"
    🎯
    Share the problem
    Research, data and goals before solutions.
    🗨️
    Goal-based feedback
    Tie comments to users, not taste.
    🧪
    Early feasibility
    Check with engineering while exploring.
    📦
    Complete hand-off
    All states, components and copy.

    🧩 Basic: Writing a Design Brief

    textDESIGN BRIEF
Problem: New users do not find the invite-teammates action (12% use it).
User: Team lead in a first-week account.
Goal: Raise invites sent in week 1 from 12% to 25%.
What we know: Research notes, funnel data (links)
Constraints: Existing nav, two-week design window, web and mobile
Must include: Clear empty state, success confirmation
Out of scope: Redesign of the settings area
Deadline: First options by Oct 20

    🔄 Intermediate: The Design–Engineering–Product Loop

    Figure 4.1 · Design and engineering in the product loop
    📌
    Bring an engineer into design exploration early. A 15-minute feasibility check avoids weeks of rework.

    🗨️ Intermediate: Running a Design Critique

    StepWhat happensRule
    1. FrameDesigner states the goal and userFeedback is about the goal
    2. ObserveEveryone says what they see and understandNo solutions yet
    3. QuestionAsk why choices were madeCuriosity, not challenge
    4. SuggestOffer ideas tied to the user problem"What if…"
    5. DecideDesigner owns the next iterationWrite down actions

    ✅ Best practices

    Tie comments to user goals; ask questions; point to evidence; suggest options; let the designer decide on form.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Comments on personal taste; redesigning in the meeting; many people giving conflicting orders; feedback only at the end.

    📦 Advanced: Design Hand-off and Consistency

    Figure 4.2 · Layers of a good design hand-off
    Hand-off checkWhy
    All states shown: empty, loading, error, successMost bugs come from missing states
    Uses the design systemFaster build and consistent look
    Copy and labels agreedAvoids late wording changes
    Accessibility notesContrast, labels, keyboard access
    Analytics events listedMeasurement from day one

    ⚖️ Advanced: When Design and Engineering Disagree

    1. Restate both positions and the user goal they serve.
    2. Ask each side what they are protecting (quality, timing, risk, consistency).
    3. Find options: simplify, stage, prototype or test.
    4. Use data or a quick test, if possible.
    5. Decide with the PM and record the reason; return to the decision after launch with data.
    ✅
    Example: Design wants an animated onboarding; engineering says it costs two weeks. Option: ship a static version now, measure activation, and add animation only if the data supports it.

    🏆 Expert: Design Systems, Research and Accessibility

    TopicExpert practice
    Design systemReuse components; request changes through the system owner
    Research opsShare research findings in a searchable place; invite engineers to sessions
    AccessibilityTreat it as a requirement in acceptance criteria
    ExperimentationAgree on variants, metrics and duration with design and data
    Design debtTrack inconsistencies and schedule fixes

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    Involve design early; share data and research; give goal-based feedback; check feasibility together; track design states; celebrate design contribution.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Handing over a finished wireframe; subjective feedback; late changes; skipping error states; bypassing the designer with engineers.

    Module 05

    Presentation Design

    Designing presentations that persuade: audience, one message, story structure, slide design, charts, delivery and handling questions.

    🎯 Basic: Start with the Audience and the Ask

    A presentation is not a document read aloud. It exists to help a specific audience understand something and make a decision. Before opening slides, answer three questions.

    👥
    Who is the audience?
    Their role, knowledge and what they care about.
    💬
    What is the one message?
    If they remember one sentence, which is it?
    ✅
    What is the ask?
    Decision, feedback, support or awareness only.
    ⏱️
    How much time?
    Plan 1–2 minutes per slide and leave time for questions.
    💡
    Write the ask in one sentence first: "I need approval to launch guest checkout to 20% of users on Oct 28." Everything else supports it.

    🧭 Basic: Story Structure

    Figure 5.1 · A decision-ready presentation structure
    AudienceLead withKeep short
    ExecutivesRecommendation and impactBackground and detail
    EngineeringProblem, constraints, scopeMarket story
    DesignUser problem and evidenceBusiness jargon
    Customers or partnersTheir benefitInternal process

    🖼️ Intermediate: Slide Design Principles

    PrincipleHowExample
    One message per slideUse an action title (a full sentence)"Guest checkout could recover 4% of lost orders"
    Less textKeep to a headline and one visualMove detail to notes or an appendix
    Visual hierarchySize, weight and position show importanceKey number large, context small
    ConsistencySame fonts, colours and alignmentUse a template
    Contrast and sizeReadable from the back of a roomBody text 18pt or more
    White spaceLeave room around objectsDo not fill every corner

    ✅ Good slides

    Action titles; one idea per slide; large readable text; simple visuals; consistent layout; appendix for detail.

    ❌ Common slide mistakes

    Titles like "Overview"; paragraphs of text; 6 colours; tiny charts; animations everywhere; reading slides aloud.

    📊 Intermediate: Presenting Data and Charts

    QuestionBest chartAvoid
    Change over timeLine chartPie chart
    Compare categoriesBar chart (sorted)3D charts
    Parts of a whole (few)Stacked bar or simple donutMore than 5 slices
    Funnel drop-offHorizontal funnel or barsTable of raw numbers
    RelationshipScatter plotDual axes without labels
    Figure 5.2 · Anatomy of a data slide

    🎤 Advanced: Delivery and Handling Questions

    • Open with the point and the ask, then explain.
    • Speak to people, not to the slide; pause after key points.
    • Practise out loud and time it; prepare a 3-minute and a 10-minute version.
    • Repeat each question, answer briefly, and check you answered it.
    • If you do not know, say so and promise a follow-up date.
    Hard momentResponse
    A hostile questionAcknowledge, answer with facts, move on
    Off-topic detour"Good point, let us take it after so we keep time"
    A senior challenges your dataShow the definition and source; offer to re-check
    Technical failureContinue verbally from your outline

    🏆 Expert: Presenting Bad News and Persuading

    Figure 5.3 · Presenting bad news
    ⚠️
    Never hide a risk in an appendix. Senior audiences trust presenters who name the risk before they are asked.

    ✅ Best Practices & Common Mistakes

    ✅ Best practices

    Define the ask; one message per slide; rehearse; show evidence; send a short pre-read; follow up with decisions in writing.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Starting with background; dense slides; no clear ask; reading slides; skipping rehearsal; no follow-up.

    Module 11

    Interview Preparation

    Questions asked in APM interviews about communication, cross-functional work, presentations and listening, grouped by level. Draft your answer, then compare with the model answer.

    🌱 Beginner Interview Questions

    IQ1.Why is communication so important for an APM?
    An APM leads through influence, not authority. Engineers, designers and stakeholders must understand the goal, scope and trade-offs; good communication creates alignment and prevents rework.
    IQ2.What is active listening?
    Paying full attention, understanding the meaning and feelings, reflecting back what you heard and asking open questions to confirm. It prevents misunderstanding and builds trust.
    IQ3.How do you keep engineering and design aligned?
    Share a clear brief, involve both early, hold regular check-ins, record decisions in a shared place, and review work against the goal before build and release.

    🛠️ Intermediate Interview Questions

    IQ4.How do you structure a presentation for executives?
    Start with the recommendation and the ask, then give context, evidence, options and risks. One message per slide with action titles, simple charts and details in an appendix.
    IQ5.How do you give feedback to a designer?
    Tie feedback to the user and the goal, describe what you observe and your concern, ask questions and suggest options, and leave the form to the designer. Avoid taste-based comments.
    IQ6.How do you handle a new request from a senior stakeholder mid-sprint?
    Clarify the need and deadline, get impact from engineering, present options and trade-offs, decide with the PM, then communicate the change to everyone affected.

    🚀 Advanced Interview Questions

    IQ7.Tell me about a time you disagreed with an engineer.
    Use a structure: situation, their concern, how you listened and clarified the user goal, options and data used, the decision, and the result and lesson. Show respect and a focus on the product, not on winning.
    IQ8.How do you present data that contradicts what leadership believes?
    Check the data and definitions first, lead with the finding and its implication, show the evidence simply, acknowledge limits, and propose a next step such as a test. Stay factual and calm.
    IQ9.How do you run a meeting that actually decides something?
    Define the decision and invite only needed people, send a pre-read, timebox topics, capture decisions, owners and dates, and send notes within a day.

    🏆 Expert Interview Questions

    IQ10.The launch is going to slip. Who do you tell, and how?
    Tell the sponsor and affected stakeholders early with the headline, impact, cause, options and a recommended plan. Give an update schedule. Early, honest news gives people time to act.
    IQ11.How do you communicate with teams in different time zones?
    Use written async updates and decision records, rotate meeting times fairly, record key meetings, set clear response expectations and make decisions visible in one place.
    IQ12.How would you improve communication in a team that often misses requirements?
    Diagnose with a retro and examples, introduce a one-page brief, acceptance criteria and a hand-off checklist, hold a short mid-build review and track rework. Measure and adjust after a few sprints.
