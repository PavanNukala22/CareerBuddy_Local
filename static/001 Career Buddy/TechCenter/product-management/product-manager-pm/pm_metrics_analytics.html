    Module 01

    Metrics & Analytics for Product Managers

    How PMs choose and track the right metrics: North Star metrics, A/B test design, cohort retention analysis and turning numbers into decisions.

    🏛️ Course Overview: What the Role Needs

    Metrics and analytics let a Product Manager (PM) steer by evidence instead of opinion. You choose what to measure, design tests that give reliable answers and read retention and behaviour to know whether the product truly works.

    ⭐
    North Star metric
    One metric that captures the value users get.
    🧪
    A/B testing
    Design experiments that give trustworthy answers.
    📆
    Cohort retention
    See whether users come back over time.
    📊
    Decision-making
    Turn data into clear product decisions.
    💡
    A metric is useful only if a decision would change depending on its value. If nothing would change, stop tracking it.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    By the end of this course you will be able to:

    1. Define a North Star metric and the input metrics that drive it.
    2. Choose metrics using frameworks such as AARRR and HEART, with guardrails.
    3. Design an A/B test: hypothesis, metric, sample size, duration and analysis plan.
    4. Read results with confidence intervals and avoid common statistical traps.
    5. Build and interpret cohort retention tables and curves.
    6. Use SQL and dashboards for basic analysis and instrumentation.
    7. Communicate results and recommendations clearly.

    Module 02

    North Star Metric Tracking

    Choosing a North Star, breaking it into input metrics, metric frameworks (AARRR, HEART), guardrails and a metric tree.

    ⭐ Basic: What a North Star Metric Is

    A North Star metric is the single number that best captures the core value users receive and predicts long-term business success. It guides decisions and keeps teams aligned.

    ProductExample North StarWhy
    Streaming serviceHours watched per subscriber per weekShows value and predicts retention
    MarketplaceSuccessful transactions per weekBoth sides get value
    Collaboration toolWeekly teams with 3+ active membersShows team adoption
    Language appLearners completing 3+ lessons a weekHabit and progress
    💡
    Good North Star tests: reflects user value, leads to revenue, can be influenced by the team and is measurable weekly.

    🌲 Intermediate: Input Metrics and the Metric Tree

    Figure 2.1 · A metric tree from North Star to team actions
    📌
    Teams own input metrics they can move in a quarter. The North Star is the shared outcome they all contribute to.

    🧭 Intermediate: AARRR and HEART Frameworks

    FrameworkDimensionsUse for
    AARRR (Pirate metrics)Acquisition, Activation, Retention, Revenue, ReferralGrowth funnel across the lifecycle
    HEARTHappiness, Engagement, Adoption, Retention, Task successProduct experience quality
    Figure 2.2 · The AARRR funnel
    📣
    Acquisition
    Visits, signups, cost per signup.
    ⚡
    Activation
    % reaching the first value moment.
    🔁
    Retention
    Week-N return rate.
    💰
    Revenue
    Conversion, ARPU, LTV.

    🛡️ Advanced: Guardrails, Vanity Metrics and Metric Dictionaries

    ✅ Best practices

    Define each metric precisely; add guardrails (errors, support tickets, retention); review metrics quarterly; write a shared metric dictionary; segment before concluding.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Vanity metrics (downloads, page views); changing definitions silently; one metric without guardrails; metrics no one owns; chasing a number at user expense.

    Module 03

    A/B Testing Design

    Designing experiments that give reliable answers: hypotheses, metrics, randomisation, sample size, duration, analysis and decisions.

    🧪 Basic: What an A/B Test Is

    An A/B test randomly splits users into control (current version) and variant (new version) and compares a metric. Because users are assigned randomly, differences can be attributed to the change.

    Figure 3.1 · A/B test lifecycle

    📝 Basic: Hypothesis and Metrics

    textHYPOTHESIS
We believe that showing delivery-time estimates on product pages will increase checkout starts.
Primary metric: checkout start rate (baseline 18%)
Guardrails: order cancellation rate, page load time
Minimum detectable effect: +1.5 points (relative +8%)
Population: new visitors on mobile
Decision rule: ship if primary metric improves and no guardrail worsens beyond tolerance

    📐 Intermediate: Sample Size, Duration and Randomisation

    Design choiceGuidance
    Unit of randomisationUsually user, not session; keep consistent assignment
    Sample sizeFrom baseline rate, minimum detectable effect, power (80%), significance (5%)
    DurationAt least one or two full weekly cycles; avoid stopping early
    Traffic split50/50 is most efficient; use less for risky changes
    Sample ratio checkVerify the split matches the plan (SRM)
    Figure 3.2 · Layers of a reliable test
    ⚠️
    Do not stop a test when it first looks significant. Peeking inflates false positives. Fix the duration or sample size in advance.

    📊 Intermediate: Reading Results

    TermMeaningExample
    LiftDifference between variant and control+1.2 points (6.7% relative)
    Confidence intervalPlausible range for the true effect+0.3 to +2.1 points
    p-valueChance of a result this extreme if there were no real effectp = 0.02
    PowerChance of detecting a real effect of the planned size80%
    Practical significanceIs the effect big enough to matter?+0.1 points may not be worth shipping
    Figure 3.3 · Reading experiment results

    🏆 Advanced: Pitfalls and Special Designs

    PitfallSafeguard
    PeekingFixed horizon or sequential methods
    Many metrics and segmentsPre-register one primary metric
    Novelty effectRun long enough; look at later weeks
    Network effectsRandomise by group or region
    Low trafficTest bigger changes; use fewer variants
    Interaction between testsCoordinate experiment calendar

    Module 04

    Cohort Retention Analysis

    Building and reading cohort tables and retention curves, finding why users leave, and linking retention to product changes.

    📆 Basic: Cohorts and Retention

    A cohort is a group of users who started in the same period (for example, signups in January). Retention is the share of that cohort still active N days or weeks later.

    CohortWeek 0Week 1Week 2Week 4
    Jan signups (4,000)100%41%30%22%
    Feb signups (4,400)100%44%33%25%
    Mar signups (5,000)100%49%38%31%
    💡
    Read down a column to compare cohorts, and across a row to see one cohort age.

    📈 Intermediate: Reading the Retention Curve

    Figure 4.1 · An example retention curve
    PatternMeaningAction
    Curve flattens above zeroA retained core existsGrow acquisition and activation
    Curve keeps falling to zeroNo lasting valueFix core value before growth
    Steep day-1 dropWeak onboardingImprove first-run experience
    Newer cohorts higherProduct improvements workVerify cause and double down

    🧮 Intermediate: Calculating Retention in SQL

    sql-- Weekly retention by signup cohort
WITH cohorts AS (
  SELECT user_id, date_trunc('week', signup_at) AS cohort_week
  FROM users
),
activity AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT user_id, date_trunc('week', event_time) AS active_week
  FROM events
)
SELECT c.cohort_week,
       (a.active_week::date - c.cohort_week::date) / 7 AS week_n,
       COUNT(DISTINCT a.user_id) AS active_users
FROM cohorts c
JOIN activity a ON a.user_id = c.user_id
GROUP BY 1, 2
ORDER BY 1, 2;
    Figure 4.2 · Building a retention table

    🏆 Advanced: Segmenting and Diagnosing Retention

    CutQuestion
    Acquisition channelDo paid users leave faster than organic?
    Platform / versionDid a release hurt retention?
    First actionsWhich first-week behaviour predicts retention (the "aha moment")?
    Plan or segmentDo teams retain better than individuals?
    Figure 4.3 · Common drivers of retention
    ✅

    Module 08

    Real-World Scenarios & Troubleshooting

    Case studies: a metric dip, a win that disappears, conflicting dashboards and a test with too little traffic.

    📉 Case Study: Retention Dropped After a Release

    Week-4 retention fell from 25% to 19% for the latest cohort.

    Figure 8.1 · Diagnosing a retention drop
    ✅
    Finding: a new paid-ad channel brought low-intent users; organic retention was unchanged. The team adjusted targeting and reported retention by channel.

    🎢 Troubleshooting: The Win That Disappeared

    1. Check for novelty effect and seasonality.
    2. Verify the test ended at the planned time with no SRM.
    3. Check segments and interactions with other launches.
    4. Run a holdout or repeat the test.
    Figure 8.2 · Investigating a vanished win

    ⚖️ Troubleshooting: Conflicting Dashboards

    StepAction
    1Compare definitions, filters and time zones
    2Reproduce each number
    3Explain the gap
    4Standardise in the metric dictionary

    🚦 Trade-off Scenario: Low Traffic

    With few users, tests take months. Test bigger changes, use a more sensitive metric (such as click-through rather than revenue), run sequential or Bayesian methods with care, and combine with qualitative research. Do not declare a win from a tiny sample.

    Module 11

    Interview Preparation

    Questions asked in PM interviews about metrics and analytics, grouped by level. Draft your answer, then compare with the model answer.

    🌱 Beginner Interview Questions

    IQ1.What is a North Star metric?
    A single metric capturing the core value users get and predicting long-term success, such as weekly active teams. It aligns teams, supported by input metrics and guardrails.
    IQ2.What is the difference between a metric and a guardrail?
    A target metric is what you want to improve. A guardrail is a metric that must not get worse, such as error rate or retention, to prevent harm while optimising.
    IQ3.What is retention and why does it matter?
    The share of users who return after a period. It shows whether the product delivers lasting value and is a leading sign of product-market fit and sustainable growth.

    🛠️ Intermediate Interview Questions

    IQ4.How do you set up an A/B test?
    Write a hypothesis, choose a primary metric and guardrails, define the population and randomisation unit, compute sample size and duration, run without peeking, analyse effect and interval, then decide.
    IQ5.What does statistical significance mean?
    A result unlikely to occur by chance if there were no real effect, judged against a pre-set threshold such as 5%. It does not measure the size or importance of the effect.
    IQ6.How do you read a cohort retention table?
    Rows are cohorts, columns are time since start. Read across to see one cohort age and down to compare cohorts. Look for where the curve flattens and whether newer cohorts improve.

    🚀 Advanced Interview Questions

    IQ7.How would you choose a North Star for a marketplace?
    Pick a measure of successful transactions that reflects value to both sides, such as weekly completed orders with positive ratings, then define driver and input metrics and guardrails like cancellation rate.
    IQ8.Your test is significant but the lift is tiny. What do you do?
    Consider practical significance and cost: is the effect big enough to justify complexity and risk? Check segments and guardrails, estimate business impact and decide whether to ship, iterate or stop.
    IQ9.How do you investigate a sudden metric drop?
    Verify tracking and definitions, compare with a second source, segment by platform, version, channel and region, align with releases or incidents, form hypotheses and test them.

    🏆 Expert Interview Questions

    IQ10.What are common experimentation mistakes?
    Peeking, no pre-defined metric, too small a sample, ignoring guardrails, many segment hunts, novelty effects, sample ratio mismatch and running overlapping tests carelessly.
    IQ11.How do you decide what to measure for a new feature?
    Start from the user outcome and business goal, define success and guardrail metrics, add tracking in the requirements, set a target and review date, and ensure data can be reconciled.
    IQ12.How do you build an experimentation culture?
    Make testing easy with templates and tooling, share results including failures, set standards for design and review, link experiments to strategy, and celebrate learning rather than wins only.
