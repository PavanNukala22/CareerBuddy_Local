    Module 01

    Product Execution for Product Managers

    How a PM takes an idea from problem to launch: lifecycle management, PRDs, MVP scoping, acceptance criteria and launch coordination.

    🏛️ Course Overview: What the Role Needs

    Execution is where strategy becomes real. A Product Manager (PM) owns the path from a validated problem to a launched solution: clear requirements, a sensible scope, a ready team and a launch that works.

    🔄
    Product lifecycle
    Guide work from idea through launch, growth and sunset.
    📄
    PRD creation
    Write requirements engineers and designers can act on.
    📦
    MVP scoping
    Cut to the smallest release that tests the core value.
    🚀
    Launch coordination
    Align engineering, marketing, support and sales on go-live.
    💡
    Execution is clarity plus follow-through: the right scope, written clearly, delivered on time, launched with everyone ready.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    By the end of this course you will be able to:

    1. Describe each stage of the product lifecycle and the PM's role in it.
    2. Write a clear PRD with problem, goals, scope, requirements and metrics.
    3. Scope an MVP that tests the riskiest assumption with minimum effort.
    4. Write acceptance criteria and a definition of done that testers can verify.
    5. Plan and coordinate a launch across all teams, with readiness checks and rollback.
    6. Track delivery, manage risks and communicate status.
    7. Run a post-launch review and feed learning back into the roadmap.

    Module 03

    Writing Product Requirements Documents (PRDs)

    What a PRD is, what goes in it, how to write it clearly, how to keep it alive and how to review it with engineering and design.

    📄 Basic: What a PRD Is For

    A PRD explains what we are building, for whom and why, with enough detail that engineering, design, QA and go-to-market can do their jobs. It is a tool for shared understanding, not paperwork.

    Figure 3.1 · Layers of a PRD

    🧱 Basic: PRD Sections

    SectionContentTip
    SummaryOne paragraph: problem, solution, outcomeWrite last, place first
    Problem & usersWho has the problem and evidenceLink to research
    Goals & metricsSuccess metric, target, guardrailsOne primary metric
    ScopeIn, out, laterState exclusions
    RequirementsNumbered, testableOne idea each
    User flows & designsLinks to prototypesShow states
    Dependencies & risksTeams, systems, legalOwner and date
    Launch planRollout and commsLink to checklist
    Open questionsUnresolved itemsOwner and due date

    ✍️ Intermediate: A Short Example

    textPRD: Guest Checkout
Summary: Let first-time shoppers buy without creating an account, to cut checkout abandonment by 10%.
Problem: 38% of mobile shoppers abandon when asked to register (analytics; 8 interviews).
Goal: Checkout conversion 52% → 57% in 8 weeks. Guardrail: payment error rate < 1%.
In scope: email-only guest checkout, order confirmation email.
Out of scope: saved cards, loyalty points.
Requirements:
 R1 A shopper can check out with an email and shipping address.
 R2 The system sends a confirmation within 60 seconds.
 R3 A guest can create an account after purchase.
Risks: Fraud on guest orders — owner: Risk team; mitigation: 3-D Secure.
Open: Email verification? (Eng, due Oct 14)
    Figure 3.2 · PRD review workflow

    📏 Intermediate: Writing Good Requirements

    ✅ Best practices

    Specific and testable: "Page loads in under 2 seconds for 95% of requests"; one idea each; plain language; numbered; linked to a user need.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Vague: "fast and intuitive"; many ideas per line; design instructions disguised as requirements; undefined terms; missing error cases.

    🏆 Advanced: Keeping the PRD Alive

    • Keep one source of truth; link tickets and designs to it.
    • Maintain a short change log with dates and reasons.
    • Move completed decisions out of "open questions".
    • Review the PRD in sprint planning and in the launch review.
    • Archive with outcomes so future teams can learn.

    Module 04

    MVP Scoping

    Defining the smallest release that tests the core value: finding the riskiest assumption, cutting scope, choosing MVP types and setting success criteria.

    📦 Basic: What an MVP Is and Is Not

    An MVP is the smallest version of a product that lets you learn whether the core idea creates value. It is not a low-quality product or the first version of every feature.

    ✅ A real MVP

    Tests one core assumption; usable end to end for a narrow group; has a measurable learning goal; shipped quickly.

    ❌ A fake MVP

    Half-built features; many assumptions at once; no success criteria; "MVP" used to excuse poor quality; never iterated.

    ✂️ Intermediate: How to Scope an MVP

    Figure 4.1 · MVP scoping process
    MVP typeDescriptionExample
    ConciergeManual service behind the scenesStaff match tutors by hand
    Wizard of OzLooks automated, human-runChat that people answer
    Single-featureOne core feature builtOnly booking, no payments
    PiecemealExisting tools combinedForm + spreadsheet + email
    Landing pageDemand testWaitlist with pricing

    🎯 Intermediate: MoSCoW and the Cut Line

    Figure 4.2 · Elements of an MVP
    textMVP SCOPE — Tutor Marketplace
Assumption: Parents will book a tutor online without a free trial.
Must: search by subject, profile, book a slot, pay
Should: reviews (3 seeded), reminders
Could: chat, calendar sync
Won't: group classes, subscriptions, mobile app
Success: 40 bookings in 6 weeks from 500 visits; repeat rate ≥ 25%

    ⚙️ Advanced: Trade-offs, Quality and Next Steps

    QuestionGuideline
    What cannot be cut?Anything that breaks trust, safety, legal or the core journey
    What is OK to do manually?Low-volume tasks you can automate later
    How to avoid MVP bloat?Every feature must link to the learning goal
    After the MVP?Review results; iterate, pivot or stop
    🧪
    Learn
    Define the learning goal first.
    ✂️
    Cut
    Remove everything not tied to it.
    🛡️
    Protect
    Keep trust and safety intact.
    🔁
    Iterate
    Plan the next step from results.

    Module 05

    Acceptance Criteria & Definition of Done

    Writing acceptance criteria that testers and engineers can verify, user stories, edge cases, definition of done and acceptance in practice.

    ✅ Basic: User Stories and Acceptance Criteria

    A user story says who wants what and why. Acceptance criteria say exactly when the story is done and correct.

    gherkinStory: As a shopper, I want to check out as a guest so that I can buy without an account.

Scenario: Guest completes checkout
  Given I have items in my cart and I am not signed in
  When I enter a valid email, address and payment
  Then my order is placed
  And I receive a confirmation email within 60 seconds

Scenario: Invalid email
  Given I am on the guest checkout form
  When I enter "abc" as the email
  Then I see "Enter a valid email" and cannot continue
    Figure 5.1 · From story to acceptance

    🔍 Intermediate: Covering Edge Cases

    CategoryAskExample
    Happy pathWhat is the normal flow?Valid card, order placed
    ErrorsWhat if input is wrong?Declined card message
    Empty / boundaryWhat if none, max or min?Cart with 0 or 100 items
    PermissionsWho may do it?Guest vs signed-in
    FailureWhat if a service is down?Payment provider timeout
    Non-functionalSpeed, security, accessibilityWorks with screen readers

    ✅ Best practices

    One behaviour per scenario; measurable results; written in user language; negative cases; reviewed by QA.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Vague terms like "user-friendly"; technical solutions; scenarios that depend on each other; only happy paths.

    📋 Advanced: Definition of Done vs Acceptance Criteria

    ItemScopeExample
    Acceptance criteriaSpecific to one storyGuest checkout sends a confirmation in 60 seconds
    Definition of DoneApplies to every storyCode reviewed, tests pass, analytics events added, docs updated, accessibility checked

    Real-World Scenarios & Troubleshooting

    Case studies from daily PM execution: scope creep, a slipping launch, an unclear PRD and a failed rollout.

    📈 Case Study: Scope Creep Before Launch

    Three weeks before launch, sales and design each add requests. The release would slip by a month.

    Figure 8.1 · Handling late scope
    ✅
    Outcome: two requests deferred to v1.1, one swapped for a lower-value story; launch date held; requesters got dates in writing.

    ⏳ Troubleshooting: The Launch Is Slipping

    1. Find the cause: scope, dependency, quality or capacity.
    2. Generate options: cut scope, add help, move date, stage release.
    3. Quantify trade-offs; pick a recommendation.
    4. Tell stakeholders early with the plan and next checkpoint.
    Figure 8.2 · Responding to a slipping launch

    📄 Troubleshooting: An Unclear PRD

    SymptomFix
    Engineers keep asking the same questionsAdd the answers and edge cases to the PRD
    QA cannot testRewrite requirements with acceptance criteria
    Stakeholders disagree on scopeAdd explicit in/out lists and get sign-off
    Document is 20 pagesCut to one page plus linked details

    ⚠️ Trade-off Scenario: Speed vs Quality

    Leadership wants a feature in two weeks. Define what must be right (core journey, security, data), what may be rough (secondary screens), ship behind a flag to a small group, and schedule cleanup so speed does not become permanent debt.

    Module 11

    Interview Preparation

    Questions asked in PM interviews about execution, grouped by level. Draft your answer, then compare with the model answer.

    🌱 Beginner Interview Questions

    IQ1.What is a PRD and when do you write one?
    A PRD documents the problem, users, goals, scope and requirements for a feature so teams share understanding. I write one when the work involves several teams or enough complexity that a short, shared document will save rework — kept brief and updated.
    IQ2.What is an MVP?
    The smallest release that tests the core assumption and gives real learning. It is built for a narrow group with clear success criteria — not a low-quality product.
    IQ3.What are acceptance criteria?
    Specific conditions a story must meet to be accepted, usually as Given/When/Then scenarios including edge cases. They make requirements testable and align engineering and QA.

    🛠️ Intermediate Interview Questions

    IQ4.How do you decide what goes into an MVP?
    Identify the riskiest assumption and the core user journey, list features, apply MoSCoW against the learning goal, cut everything else, and set success criteria before building.
    IQ5.How do you keep a PRD useful?
    Keep it short, link designs and tickets, maintain a change log, put open questions with owners, review it in planning, and update it after decisions.
    IQ6.What is in a launch checklist?
    Product and QA readiness, monitoring and rollback, support enablement, marketing and sales materials, legal approvals, a staged rollout plan, owners, and a go/no-go decision.

    🚀 Advanced Interview Questions

    IQ7.How do you handle scope creep close to launch?
    Freeze baseline scope, assess each request with engineering, show options and trade-offs (swap, defer, cut), decide with owners, record in the change log and communicate immediately.
    IQ8.Describe a launch that went wrong and what you learned.
    Explain the situation, impact, how you responded (rollback, communication), the root cause and the process changes you made, such as new monitoring or checklist items. Show ownership without blame.
    IQ9.How do you decide whether to launch with a known bug?
    Assess severity and users affected, check for a workaround, whether it can be flagged off, and the cost of delay. Money, data or security risk blocks launch; minor issues ship with a fix date and monitoring.

    🏆 Expert Interview Questions

    IQ10.How do you balance speed and quality?
    Protect the core journey, security and data integrity; accept roughness in secondary areas; ship behind a flag to a small group; schedule cleanup and track debt visibly.
    IQ11.How do you manage several launches at once?
    Keep one view with stage, owner, risks and dates; use consistent checklists and rituals; delegate tracking to leads; and protect time for decisions that only I can make.
    IQ12.How do you know a launch succeeded?
    Compare outcome metrics with the target set in the PRD, check guardrails, quality and support load, gather user feedback, and hold a review within a few weeks to decide next steps.
