    Module 01

    Strategy & Discovery for Product Managers

    What discovery is, how strategy connects to it, and how opportunity solution trees, user interviews, market research and prioritization fit into one repeatable practice.

    🏛️ Course Overview: What the Role Needs

    Strategy and discovery decide what a product team should work on and why. A Product Manager (PM) owns the outcome, so you must find the real user problems, understand the market and choose a small number of high-impact bets — and be able to explain the choice.

    🌳
    Opportunity mapping
    Link outcomes to opportunities, solutions and experiments.
    🎙️
    User interviews
    Learn real behaviour, needs and context.
    🔭
    Market research
    Understand competitors, segments and positioning.
    ⚖️
    Prioritization
    Choose what to build with RICE, MoSCoW and judgement.
    💡
    Discovery answers "are we building the right thing?" and delivery answers "are we building it right?". Strong PMs run both continuously, not one after the other.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    By the end of this course you will be able to:

    1. Explain the difference between outcomes, outputs, opportunities and solutions.
    2. Build and maintain an opportunity solution tree for a product outcome.
    3. Plan, run and synthesise user interviews that reveal behaviour, not opinions.
    4. Research competitors and markets and turn findings into positioning and bets.
    5. Score and rank features using RICE and MoSCoW, and know when each fails.
    6. Design cheap experiments to test assumptions before building.
    7. Communicate a discovery-backed strategy and defend trade-offs.

    Module 02

    Opportunity Solution Tree Mapping

    Mapping a desired outcome to user opportunities, solutions and experiments, so every idea connects to a goal and a test.

    🌳 Basic: What an Opportunity Solution Tree Is

    An opportunity solution tree (OST) is a visual map that links one desired outcome to the opportunities (user needs, pains, desires) that could drive it, the solutions that address each opportunity and the experiments that test those solutions.

    Figure 2.1 · Layers of an opportunity solution tree
    💡
    One tree, one outcome. If a tree has three unrelated outcomes, split it into three trees.

    🧱 Basic: Outcome vs Output

    Output (thing shipped)Outcome (change caused)
    Launch a referral pageIncrease new signups from referrals by 20%
    Add dark modeIncrease evening session length
    Build a dashboardReduce time managers spend on weekly reports by 30%
    📈
    Activation
    More new users reach first value.
    🔁
    Retention
    More users come back.
    💰
    Monetisation
    More users upgrade or buy.
    🤝
    Referral
    More users invite others.
    📌
    Choose an outcome your team can influence, such as activation or retention, rather than a company-wide result such as revenue.

    🛠️ Intermediate: Building the Tree Step by Step

    Figure 2.2 · Building an opportunity solution tree
    StepRuleExample
    OpportunitiesPhrase as a user need, not a solution"I can't tell which tasks are urgent"
    StructureBreak big opportunities into smaller onesOverload → prioritising → deadlines
    SolutionsGenerate at least three per opportunitySmart sort, daily digest, colour labels
    CompareChoose one opportunity to focus on firstLargest, most reachable, strategic

    🧪 Intermediate: Assumption Mapping and Experiments

    Assumption typeQuestionExample test
    DesirabilityDo users want it?Interviews, fake-door button
    UsabilityCan they use it?Prototype test with 5 users
    FeasibilityCan we build it?Engineering spike
    ViabilityDoes it work for the business?Pricing test, unit economics
    💡
    Test the riskiest assumption first — the one that would kill the idea if false.

    🏆 Advanced: Maintaining the Tree and Common Pitfalls

    ✅ Best practices

    Update weekly after interviews; keep opportunities as user needs; compare several solutions; link every experiment to an assumption; share the tree with the team.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Writing solutions as opportunities; one solution per opportunity; building the tree once and never updating; trees with no experiments; trees only the PM understands.

    Module 03

    Conducting User Interviews

    Planning, running and synthesising user interviews: recruiting, questions, story-based interviewing, notes, synthesis and sharing insights.

    🎙️ Basic: Why and How to Interview

    Interviews reveal why people behave as they do. Customers are poor at predicting the future but good at telling stories about the past. Ask about what they did, not what they would do.

    Figure 3.1 · The user interview process
    💡
    Replace "Would you use this?" with "Tell me about the last time you did this."

    ❓ Basic: Writing Good Questions

    ✅ Good questions

    "Tell me about the last time you booked travel."; "What happened next?"; "What was hard about that?"; "How do you do it today?"; "Why was that important?"

    ❌ Poor questions

    "Would you pay for this?"; "Don't you think this is easier?"; "Do you like the design?"; "How often will you use it?"; Asking two questions at once.

    textINTERVIEW GUIDE (30 min)
Intro (3): purpose, recording consent, no right answers
Context (5): role and typical week
Story (15): last time you [did the task] — what triggered it, steps, tools, problems
Workarounds (5): what do you do when it fails?
Wrap (2): anything I missed? Who else should I talk to?

    👥 Intermediate: Recruiting and Running Sessions

    TopicPractice
    WhoRecruit people who recently did the task, including non-users and churned users
    How manyStart with 5–8 per segment; stop when patterns repeat
    SetupQuiet place or video call; record with consent; a second person takes notes
    BehaviourListen 80%; use silence; follow the story; avoid pitching
    EthicsConsent, privacy, fair incentive, no deception
    Figure 3.2 · Habits of a good interviewer

    🧠 Intermediate: Synthesis

    StepWhat to do
    SnapshotOne page per interview: who, context, key quotes, opportunities
    ClusterGroup similar needs and pains across interviews
    NameWrite each opportunity as a user need
    CountNote how many people mentioned it, with strength of feeling
    SharePresent themes with quotes and add to the tree
    textINSIGHT
Users who run weekly reports keep a private spreadsheet because the tool's export loses formatting (6 of 8 interviews).
Quote: "I rebuild it by hand every Friday."
Opportunity: Export reports in a ready-to-share format
Confidence: High (repeated, recent, costly)

    🏆 Advanced: Interviewing in Special Situations

    SituationApproach
    B2B with many stakeholdersInterview user, buyer and admin separately
    Sensitive topicsBuild trust; offer to skip; protect identity
    Early idea with no productExplore the problem space, not the solution
    Existing customersBeware of politeness and bias; ask for real examples
    RemoteTest tech; shorter sessions; watch screens with permission

    Module 04

    Competitive & Market Research

    Understanding the market: competitor analysis, segments, positioning, trends, sizing and turning findings into strategy.

    🔭 Basic: Why Research the Market

    Product choices happen inside a market. Market research shows who else solves the problem, how customers choose, and where you can be different and better. It protects you from copying competitors blindly or missing a threat.

    🏁
    Direct competitors
    Same customer, same problem.
    🔀
    Indirect competitors
    Different solution to the same problem.
    🔄
    Substitutes
    Spreadsheets, manual work or doing nothing.
    🌱
    New entrants
    Startups and big platforms moving in.

    📊 Intermediate: Competitor Analysis

    DimensionWhat to look atSource
    Target customerWho they serve and message toWebsite, ads, case studies
    ProductKey features and gapsTrials, demos, release notes
    PricingModel, tiers, discountsPricing page, sales calls
    PositioningTheir claim and proofHomepage, reviews
    Customer voiceReviews, complaints, praiseApp stores, G2, forums
    Strengths and weaknessesWhere they win or loseInterviews, win/loss data
    Figure 4.1 · Competitor research process

    🧭 Intermediate: Positioning and Segmentation

    textPOSITIONING STATEMENT
For [small clinic managers] who [lose revenue to missed appointments],
[ClinicPing] is a [reminder platform] that [cuts no-shows by 30%].
Unlike [generic calendar tools], we [integrate with clinic systems and send smart reminders].
    Segmentation basisExample
    Need or jobFast booking vs managing complex schedules
    Company sizeSolo practice vs hospital group
    BehaviourHeavy vs occasional users
    GeographyRegulation and language differences

    📐 Advanced: Market Sizing and Trends

    MeasureMeaningHow
    TAMTotal market demandAll potential customers × price
    SAMPart you could serveFilter by segment and geography
    SOMPart you can win near termRealistic share from channels and capacity
    Figure 4.2 · TAM, SAM and SOM
    ⚠️
    Size a market bottom-up (customers × price × adoption) and sanity-check with top-down. Beware of "if we get 1% of a huge market".

    🏆 Expert: From Research to Strategy

    QuestionStrategic answer
    Where do we play?Choose target segments and problems
    How do we win?Differentiate on value, cost or experience
    What do we not do?Name explicit trade-offs
    How will we know?Define outcome metrics and checkpoints
    ✅
    A strong strategy is a set of choices, including what you will not do. If nothing is excluded, it is a wish list.

    Module 05

    Feature Prioritization: RICE & MoSCoW

    Deciding what to build: RICE scoring, MoSCoW scoping, other frameworks, bias, and how to explain and defend prioritization decisions.

    ⚖️ Basic: Why Prioritize

    Teams always have more ideas than capacity. Prioritization makes the trade-off explicit so decisions are consistent, explainable and linked to outcomes.

    Figure 5.1 · Prioritization process
    ⚠️
    A framework informs a decision; it does not make it. If the ranking surprises you, investigate the inputs.

    🧮 Intermediate: RICE Scoring

    FactorMeaningExample scale
    ReachUsers affected per periodNumber per quarter
    ImpactEffect on each user3 massive, 2 high, 1 medium, 0.5 low, 0.25 minimal
    ConfidenceHow sure you are100% high, 80% medium, 50% low
    EffortPerson-months of workEstimate with engineering
    textRICE = (Reach × Impact × Confidence) ÷ Effort

Idea A: Guest checkout   (8000 × 2 × 80%) ÷ 2   = 6,400
Idea B: Dark mode        (5000 × 0.5 × 100%) ÷ 1 = 2,500
Idea C: Referral program (3000 × 2 × 50%) ÷ 3   = 1,000
=> Do A first; C needs more evidence before it deserves effort
    Figure 5.2 · What goes into a RICE score

    📦 Intermediate: MoSCoW Scoping

    CategoryMeaningRule of thumb
    Must haveRelease fails without itNot more than about 60% of effort
    Should haveImportant but not vitalPainful to leave out
    Could haveNice to haveFirst to cut
    Won't have (now)Agreed out of scope this timeWritten down to avoid scope creep
    Figure 5.3 · Using MoSCoW to scope an MVP
    💡
    Use RICE to rank across initiatives and MoSCoW to scope inside a release.

    🔀 Advanced: Other Frameworks and Bias

    FrameworkBest for
    Value vs Effort matrixQuick visual workshop
    Kano modelSeparating basics, performance and delighters
    Weighted scoringSeveral business criteria
    Cost of delayTime-sensitive work
    Opportunity scoringImportance vs satisfaction gaps

    ✅ Best practices

    Use data for reach; calibrate impact with examples; lower confidence for guesses; review with engineering; re-score when evidence changes.

    ❌ Common mistakes

    Inflating impact for a favourite; ignoring confidence; effort from only one person; HiPPO decisions; never revisiting scores.

    🏆 Expert: Defending and Communicating Priorities

    • Show the criteria and scoring, not just the ranking.
    • Explain what is not being done and why.
    • Offer a path: "If this evidence appears, we will reconsider."
    • Keep a visible backlog with status and reasons.
    • Hold a regular priority review with stakeholders.
    textMESSAGE TO A STAKEHOLDER
"Your request (bulk export) scores 900 RICE vs 6,400 for guest checkout, mainly because reach is 300 users vs 8,000.
We will not build it this quarter. If three more enterprise customers ask or you can show revenue at risk, we will re-score next review on Nov 15."

    TestWhat it checksExample
    Fake doorDesireA button for a feature that is not built; count clicks
    Prototype testUsabilityFive users complete a task in a Figma prototype
    ConciergeValueDeliver the service manually to a few customers
    Wizard of OzValue & usabilityLooks automated; a human does the work
    Landing pageDemandSign-up page; count conversions
    SurveyPrevalenceHow many users have this problem?
    Figure 7.1 · Running a discovery experiment

    🎯 Writing Hypotheses and Success Criteria

    textHYPOTHESIS
We believe that adding a "save for later" button will increase return visits.
We will test it by showing the button to 10% of users and counting clicks.
We will know we are right if at least 8% of those users click it within 7 days.
If not, we will drop it and explore price alerts instead.

    Module 08

    Real-World Scenarios & Troubleshooting

    Case studies from daily PM work: a stakeholder-driven roadmap, interviews that contradict data, a crowded market and a priority argument.

    🗣️ Case Study: The CEO Wants a Feature

    Situation: the CEO asks for an AI assistant because a competitor launched one. You have no evidence customers want it.

    Figure 8.1 · Responding to a top-down request
    ✅
    Result: a 2-week fake-door and 6 interviews showed demand for one narrow use case; the team built that slice first and avoided a 4-month general assistant.

    📉 Troubleshooting: Interviews Contradict the Data

    1. Check sample bias: who did you interview? who is in the data?
    2. Separate what people say from what they do.
    3. Segment the analytics to find the group your interviews match.
    4. Run a small experiment to settle the question.
    Figure 8.2 · Reconciling qualitative and quantitative findings

    🛡️ Troubleshooting: A Crowded Market

    SituationResponse
    Many identical competitorsPick a narrow segment and own it
    Big platform adds your featureDifferentiate on depth, integration or service
    Price warCompete on outcome or workflow, not price
    Competitor claims you cannot verifyTest with trials and customer calls

    ⚖️ Trade-off Scenario: Two Stakeholders, One Slot

    Sales wants an enterprise SSO feature; support wants fewer onboarding tickets. Score both with RICE using shared data, show reach and revenue at risk, present the ranking and the reasons, offer a plan for the second item and agree a review date.

    Task 4IntermediateScore with RICE
    Compute RICE for A (Reach 6000, Impact 1, Confidence 80%, Effort 2) and B (Reach 2000, Impact 3, Confidence 50%, Effort 1). Which first and what would change your mind?
    Multiply then divide by effort.
    A = 6000×1×0.8÷2 = 2400. B = 2000×3×0.5÷1 = 3000. B ranks first but has low confidence; run a quick test to raise confidence. If B's impact test fails, A goes first.

    Module 11

    Interview Preparation

    Questions asked in PM interviews about strategy and discovery, grouped by level. Draft your answer, then compare with the model answer.

    🌱 Beginner Interview Questions

    IQ1.What is the difference between an output and an outcome?
    An output is something you ship, such as a feature. An outcome is the change in user or business behaviour it causes. PMs are accountable for outcomes, so goals should be written as outcomes.
    IQ2.Why do user interviews ask about past behaviour?
    People are poor at predicting what they will do but good at telling stories about what they did. Past stories reveal real triggers, workarounds and pain, so they give more reliable evidence than opinions about the future.
    IQ3.What is RICE?
    A scoring method: Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. It gives a comparable score so teams can discuss priorities openly, but it informs the decision rather than making it.

    🛠️ Intermediate Interview Questions

    IQ4.What is an opportunity solution tree?
    A visual map linking a desired outcome to user opportunities, candidate solutions and experiments. It keeps the team focused on the outcome, forces comparison of options and links each idea to a test.
    IQ5.How do you decide which assumption to test first?
    List assumptions across desirability, usability, feasibility and viability, then test the riskiest one — the one that would kill the idea if false and is least supported by evidence — with the cheapest possible experiment.
    IQ6.How do you choose between RICE and MoSCoW?
    RICE ranks initiatives against each other using estimated value per effort. MoSCoW scopes a release into must, should, could and won't. I use RICE to choose what to work on and MoSCoW to decide what is in the release.

    🚀 Advanced Interview Questions

    IQ7.Tell me about a time you changed direction based on research.
    Describe the original plan, the evidence (interviews, data, test), how you changed the decision, how you communicated it and the result. Show that you updated based on evidence and kept stakeholders aligned.
    IQ8.How do you research a market you do not know?
    Define the problem and segment, map competitors and substitutes, read reviews, interview customers and experts, size the market bottom-up and look for a gap where we can be clearly better for a specific group.
    IQ9.How do you handle a stakeholder who insists on a pet feature?
    Understand the outcome behind it, share the evidence and the scoring openly, propose a small test, and agree what evidence would change my mind. Record the decision and revisit on a date.

    🏆 Expert Interview Questions

    IQ10.What makes a good product strategy?
    A clear choice of target customer, problem and differentiation, a small set of bets linked to outcomes, explicit trade-offs about what we will not do, and a way to measure and revisit.
    IQ11.How do you balance discovery and delivery?
    Run them in parallel: dual-track. Discovery each week feeds a ready backlog with validated problems, while delivery builds validated solutions. Keep a small, continuous discovery time budget.
    IQ12.How would you enter a market dominated by a big incumbent?
    Choose an underserved segment, differentiate on a specific job the incumbent does poorly, validate with concierge tests, price simply, build tight integrations and expand once we win the niche.
