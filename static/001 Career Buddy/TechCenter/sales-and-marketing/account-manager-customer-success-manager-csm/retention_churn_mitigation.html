    Module 1

    Foundations

    What churn is, how it is measured, and why it happens.

    What is churn?

    Churn is the loss of customers or revenue over a period. Retention is its mirror image: the share you keep. In subscription businesses a small monthly churn compounds into a large annual loss.

    Key metrics

    MetricFormulaMeaning
    Logo churnCustomers lost ÷ customers at startShare of accounts lost
    Gross Revenue Retention (GRR)(Starting MRR − churn − contraction) ÷ starting MRRRevenue kept, ignoring expansion; max 100%
    Net Revenue Retention (NRR)(Starting MRR − churn − contraction + expansion) ÷ starting MRRCan exceed 100% with expansion
    Customer lifetime1 ÷ churn rateExpected lifespan

    Types of churn

    • Voluntary: the customer chooses to leave (poor value, competitor, budget).
    • Involuntary: payment failure or administrative lapse.
    • Logo churn vs revenue churn: losing many small accounts is different from losing one big one.
    • Contraction (downgrade): partial churn through fewer seats or lower tiers.

    Why customers churn

    No value realised
    Poor onboarding or adoption.
    Champion or sponsor change
    Loss of internal advocacy.
    Better alternative
    Competitor, in-house build or consolidation.
    Budget and priorities
    Cost cutting, M&A, reorganisation.
    Poor service
    Unresolved issues and slow support.

    Module 2

    Early-Warning Signals

    Detecting risk early from usage, support, relationship and commercial data.

    Signal families

    FamilyExamples of risk signals
    Product usageFalling logins, fewer active users, key feature abandonment, stalled integrations
    SupportRising tickets, escalations, unresolved bugs, negative CSAT
    RelationshipChampion departure, executive no-shows, declined QBRs, slow replies
    CommercialLate payment, seat reduction, procurement-led renewal, RFP for alternatives
    SentimentLow NPS, negative survey comments, negative tone in emails or calls
    💡
    Usage is a lagging-to-leading signal. Relationship and sentiment signals often move first.

    Usage monitoring

    • Track trends, not snapshots: a 30% drop over 4 weeks matters more than a single low day.
    • Compare to the customer's own baseline and to peer cohorts.
    • Segment by user role; admin-only activity is a warning sign.
    • Monitor feature stickiness for the features that drive outcomes.

    Sentiment analysis

    Sentiment comes from structured sources (NPS, CSAT, CES) and unstructured text (emails, ticket text, call transcripts, review sites). Use it as a trigger for human review, not a verdict. Always combine with context.

    🌍
    Real-world example — Workflow software

    A model flagged negative language in three consecutive tickets from one customer. The CSM called, discovered an unresolved integration bug blocking a key workflow, escalated engineering, and saved a $90k renewal.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Spot the risk

    Account A: logins −35% in 6 weeks; champion unresponsive; two P1 tickets open for 3 weeks. Account B: logins stable; one late payment. Which is higher priority and what would you do first?

    Show model answer

    Account A. Multiple signal families agree (usage, relationship, support). First: escalate the P1s internally, reach the executive sponsor, and propose a recovery plan with a date. B: confirm payment and note it.

    Module 3

    Intervention Playbooks

    Responding to risk with structured, owned plays.

    Risk tiers

    Group accounts into tiers (for example Green, Yellow, Red). Each tier has a defined response: Green—maintain; Yellow—targeted play within 7 days; Red—save plan with executive involvement within 48 hours.

    Save plan elements

    1. Root cause: why is the customer at risk?
    2. Goal: what must change by when?
    3. Actions and owners: product, support, services, executive sponsor.
    4. Cadence: weekly check-in until stable.
    5. Exit criteria: when the account returns to Green.

    Handling cancellation conversations

    Listen first and diagnose; separate price complaints from value complaints. Offer solutions that match the real cause (training, re-implementation, contract restructure, pause), not reflexive discounts. Capture the reason code for learning.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Write a save plan

    A customer will not renew because "the tool is too complicated for our team". Draft a 3-action save plan.

    Show model answer

    (1) Role-based re-training for the 15 active users in 2 weeks. (2) Simplify configuration by removing unused modules. (3) Executive call to agree a 60-day target (for example 70% weekly active). Review at weekly cadence; renewal decision after day 60.

    Module 4

    Expert Retention

    Predictive modelling, cohort analysis and systematic churn reduction.

    Cohort and survival analysis

    Plot retention curves by signup cohort and segment to see when customers churn. A steep early drop points to onboarding; a drop at month 12 points to renewal value. Use survival analysis to estimate lifetime and identify variables that raise hazard.

    Predictive churn models

    Combine usage, support, relationship and commercial features. Validate with precision and recall; a model nobody acts on is worthless. Pair each score tier with a play and review lift: did the play reduce churn versus a control group?

    ⚠️
    Beware false positives that burn CSM time and false negatives that miss real risk. Calibrate thresholds to capacity.

    Systematic churn reduction

    • Run a churn post-mortem on every lost account; classify root causes.
    • Feed product gaps to the roadmap with revenue impact attached.
    • Fix the top three root causes before adding new plays.
    • Compare GRR by segment and by CSM coverage model.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Interpret a retention curve

    Your cohort retention is 100% → 78% at month 3 → 74% at month 12 → 61% at month 13. What are the two problems and likely causes?

    Show model answer

    Early drop (month 3): onboarding/time-to-value failure. Renewal drop (month 13): value not proven by renewal; weak sponsorship; no QBR value story. Fix with onboarding redesign and a renewal-readiness playbook starting 120 days out.

    Module 5

    Voice of Customer & Sentiment

    NPS, CSAT, CES, qualitative feedback and closing the loop.

    The three core survey metrics

    MetricQuestionScaleBest used for
    NPSHow likely are you to recommend us?0-10; %Promoters (9-10) − %Detractors (0-6)Relationship loyalty (quarterly / semi-annual)
    CSATHow satisfied were you with X?1-5Specific interactions: onboarding, support
    CESHow easy was it to do X?1-7Effort in processes: setup, support
    💡
    Calc: 50 promoters, 30 passives, 20 detractors = NPS of 50 − 20 = +30.

    Closing the loop

    A survey without follow-up lowers trust.

    Unstructured sentiment

    • Sources: support tickets, emails, call transcripts, community posts, review sites.
    • Tools: tagging taxonomies, text analytics, conversation intelligence (Gong, Chorus).
    • Use topics and trends (for example "integration" mentions up 40%) rather than single comments.
    • Always read the actual messages behind a negative flag.
    ⚠️
    Beware survey bias: only vocal customers respond. Combine surveys with behaviour data.

    Real-world example

    🌍
    Real-world example — Subscription software

    An NPS dip from +42 to +28 among mid-market accounts looked random. Text tagging showed 60% of detractor comments mentioned a new billing UI. Product shipped a fix, CSMs contacted 40 detractors, and NPS recovered to +40 in two quarters. Detractor-account churn that quarter was 8% versus 19% historically.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Interpret a survey

    200 responses: 90 gave 9-10, 70 gave 7-8, 40 gave 0-6. Compute NPS and decide who to call first.

    Show model answer

    Promoters 45%, detractors 20% → NPS +25. Call detractors first, prioritised by ARR and renewal date, within 48 hours; then thank promoters and ask for a reference.

    Designing a save offer

    Good save offers

    • Match the root cause (training, services, re-scoping)
    • Trade for commitment (term, case study, referral)
    • Time-limited and approved by finance
    • Include a success plan

    Poor save offers

    • Discount without diagnosis
    • Unlimited free extensions
    • No change to usage plan
    • Repeated discounts every renewal

    Renewal risk forecasting

    CategoryDefinitionTypical action
    CommittedSigned or verbal + procurement startedProcess paperwork
    LikelyHealthy, sponsor aligned, no blockersSend proposal early
    At riskOpen blockers or low adoptionSave plan with exec
    UnlikelyNotice of non-renewal, RFPNegotiation, exit planning

    Review the forecast weekly. Track forecast accuracy (forecast GRR vs actual) and fix systematic bias.

    Exit conversations and win-back

    • Run an exit interview: capture the reason code and the competitor chosen.
    • Leave the door open: keep the executive relationship alive.
    • Create a win-back list and re-engage after 6-12 months, or when the trigger changes.
    • Feed insights to product and pricing.

    Real-world example

    🌍
    Real-world example — Marketing analytics vendor

    A churned $150k customer was contacted after 9 months, when their new tool failed an integration. A win-back plan with a 90-day onboarding commitment returned the account at 85% of previous ARR, and the churn post-mortem fed a connector built into the roadmap.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Choose the right play

    Three accounts: (A) champion left, usage steady; (B) logins −40% over 6 weeks, two open P1s; (C) late payment, high usage. Choose the play for each.

    Show model answer

    A: stakeholder re-mapping and executive intro. B: engineering review + exec call + recovery plan. C: dunning and a personal billing check-in. B is highest priority.

    Cohorts, Case Studies & Metrics

    Cohort retention tables, churn post-mortems and cross-functional improvement.

    Reading a cohort table

    Cohort (start)M0M3M6M12M13
    Jan100%84%79%74%63%
    Apr100%88%84%80%—
    Jul100%92%90%——

    Newer cohorts retain better at M3 (onboarding improved) but the January cohort drops again at M13 (renewal). Investigate the renewal motion, not onboarding.

    Churn post-mortem template

    1. Account facts: ARR, term, segment, tenure.
    2. Timeline of signals and actions.
    3. Primary and secondary reason codes.
    4. What we could have done earlier.
    5. Product, process or pricing change recommended.
    6. Owner and date for each change.

    Real-world example — Education platform

    Usage dropped 45% in July. The model flagged Red. CSM contact showed schools were on summer break. Seasonal baselines replaced the flat threshold; false positives fell 38%.

    Case study B: The quiet detractor

    🌍
    Real-world example — Workflow software

    A Green-scored account churned. Post-mortem: usage was high, but the buyer wanted a feature on the roadmap that never shipped, and sentiment in tickets had turned negative for 3 months. Sentiment and "feature request age" were added to the score; two similar accounts were saved the next year.

    Cross-functional improvement

    • Weekly churn review with product, support, sales and finance.
    • Revenue-weighted list of top 5 churn causes.
    • Feedback loops: roadmap items tagged with churned ARR.
    • Executive dashboard: GRR, NRR, early-life churn, at-risk ARR.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Write a post-mortem summary

    A $90k account churned after champion departure; usage fell for 8 weeks; a P2 bug remained open for 5 weeks; there was no executive contact. Summarise reason codes and 3 changes.

    Show model answer

    Primary: relationship loss (champion). Secondary: unresolved bug. Changes: (1) multi-thread rule for accounts > $50k, (2) automatic escalation after 14 days open on P2s, (3) alert on 4 consecutive weeks of declining usage.
