    Module 1

    Foundations

    What account governance is, who the stakeholders are, and what a QBR is.

    What is account governance?

    Account governance is the set of rhythms, roles and documents that keep a customer relationship healthy and aligned over time: who meets whom, how often, about what, and who decides. Good governance makes surprises rare and escalations early.

    Key terms

    • Stakeholder: anyone affected by or influencing the account.
    • Executive Sponsor (customer side): senior leader who owns the business case and budget.
    • Executive Sponsor (vendor side): your senior leader paired with their sponsor.
    • QBR: Quarterly Business Review, a structured meeting on outcomes and plans.
    • EBR: Executive Business Review, a QBR variant for senior audiences.
    • Success plan: shared goals and milestones.
    • Escalation path: the agreed route for urgent issues.

    Stakeholder roles

    • Economic buyer: approves spend.
    • Champion: advocates and drives usage.
    • Day-to-day user: uses the product; feeds adoption data.
    • Technical owner: owns integrations and security.
    • Detractor/blocker: prefers an alternative or fears change.

    Module 2

    Running QBRs

    Designing and delivering a QBR that creates value instead of recapping slides.

    QBR principles

    • It is the customer's meeting, not a vendor presentation.
    • Lead with business outcomes, not feature usage.
    • Use data, but tell the story: so what, now what.
    • End with agreed actions, owners and dates.

    A QBR agenda that works

    SegmentTimeContent
    Goals recap5 minOriginal objectives and any changes
    Results15 minOutcome metrics vs baseline and target
    Adoption & health10 minBreadth, depth, trends, risks
    Support & product5 minOpen issues, roadmap items relevant to them
    Next quarter plan15 minPriorities, expansion opportunities, asks
    Actions5 minOwners and dates

    Preparing a QBR

    1. Pre-align with the champion 1-2 weeks before; no surprises.
    2. Collect data: usage, outcomes, tickets, NPS.
    3. Draft the narrative and the "ask".
    4. Confirm the attendee list, including the sponsor.
    5. Send a short pre-read and agenda 48 hours before.
    6. Follow up within 24 hours with notes and actions.
    ⚠️
    A QBR that only shows login charts invites "so what?". Convert every chart into a business statement.

    Real-world example

    🌍
    Real-world example — Logistics software

    The CSM replaced a 40-slide deck with a 10-slide story: "On-time delivery up from 87% to 93%, saving an estimated $210k in penalties." The CFO joined for the first time, asked about a second region, and a $120k expansion followed within two months.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Draft a QBR outcome slide

    Your customer's goal: reduce invoice processing time. Baseline 9 days, now 5 days, target 4. Write the slide title and two bullets.

    Show model answer

    Title: "Invoice cycle time down 44%, one day from target". Bullets: (1) 9 → 5 days after automation of approval routing. (2) To reach 4 days: enable vendor portal for top 20 suppliers in Q3. Always compute: (9−5)/9 = 44%.

    Module 3

    Executive Sponsors & Mapping

    Mapping influence, building multi-threaded relationships and pairing sponsors.

    Why multi-threading matters

    Single-threaded accounts are fragile: if your champion leaves, the account loses its voice. Build relationships across at least three levels: executive, manager and user, and across functions: business, IT and finance.

    Stakeholder mapping

    Map each person by influence (power over the decision) and support (attitude toward your product).

    QuadrantProfileAction
    High influence / High supportSponsors, championsNurture; ask for advocacy, references
    High influence / Low supportBlockersUnderstand concerns; get sponsor involvement
    Low influence / High supportEnthusiastic usersEquip to evangelise internally
    Low influence / Low supportBystandersKeep informed

    Executive sponsor programs

    Pair a senior leader at your company with the customer's executive sponsor. Their job: quarterly strategic conversation, remove blockers, and show commitment. Use it for strategic accounts, not for every account.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Map an account

    Your champion (Priya, Ops Manager) is leaving in 30 days. You know the VP Ops (supportive), CFO (neutral), IT Director (sceptical). Propose a plan.

    Show model answer

    Immediately ask Priya to introduce a successor and to broker a meeting with the VP Ops. Build a CFO value narrative (cost savings). Address IT's concerns with a security walkthrough. Add a vendor exec sponsor call. Target three threads in 30 days.

    Module 4

    Expert Governance

    Governing complex, multi-business-unit and at-risk accounts.

    Account plans & governance charters

    For strategic accounts, create a joint account plan with business objectives, stakeholder map, risks, whitespace and a governance charter (meetings, owners, escalation, decision rights). Review it quarterly.

    Escalation management

    Define severity levels, response times and named owners. Escalate early with a clear problem statement, impact, proposed fix and timeline. Close the loop with the sponsor. After resolution run a blameless review to prevent recurrence.

    Governance across business units

    Large customers have many units with different needs. Use a steering committee for strategy, a working group for delivery, and a shared scorecard. Recognise that local wins do not automatically produce global renewal; map decision makers at the enterprise level.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Design a governance model

    A global customer with 6 business units uses your product unevenly. Propose a governance model.

    Show model answer

    Steering committee quarterly with enterprise sponsor; monthly working group with BU champions; shared scorecard (adoption, outcomes, risk); BU-level playbooks; exec sponsor pairing; central escalation path. Report outcomes per BU and in aggregate.

    Module 5

    Executive Sponsor Programs

    Designing sponsor programs, mapping influence and building executive trust.

    What a sponsor program does

    A sponsor program is a small, high-touch loop for top accounts.

    The goal is executive-to-executive trust: access, alignment on outcomes, and a path to resolve blockers fast. Limit it to the accounts that justify the time (typically top 5-10% by ARR or strategic value).

    Stakeholder influence map

    RoleCares aboutHow to engageCadence
    Executive sponsorBusiness outcomes, risk, reputationOutcome summary, strategic roadmapQuarterly
    Economic buyer / CFOROI, cost control, riskFinancial value summarySemi-annual + renewal
    ChampionTheir success and credibilityEarly access, enablement, recognitionMonthly
    Technical ownerSecurity, stability, integrationRelease notes, architecture reviewsMonthly / as needed
    UsersEase of daily workTraining, quick supportContinuous

    Preparing an executive conversation

    1. Research their goals (earnings call, strategy memo, LinkedIn).
    2. Pre-brief your executive with 3 outcomes, 2 risks and 1 ask.
    3. Open with their objectives, not your product.
    4. Bring one insight they did not have (benchmark, trend).
    5. Close with a specific, small commitment from each side.
    6. Send a written follow-up within 24 hours.
    ⚠️
    Never let an executive meeting be the first time the champion hears bad news.

    Building champions and detecting risk

    Champion health signals

    • Responds quickly and attends all meetings
    • Shares internal information
    • Uses and promotes the product
    • Introduces you to other stakeholders

    Warning signals

    • Slow replies and cancelled meetings
    • Role change or new manager
    • Stops sharing internal context
    • New executive asks "why do we pay for this?"

    Real-world example

    🌍
    Real-world example — Cybersecurity vendor

    A top-10 account had a supportive champion but no executive access. The CSM arranged a sponsor pairing: the vendor CTO met the customer CISO twice a year. When the customer reorganised and procurement questioned the renewal, the CISO personally defended it. Renewal closed 3 years, +18% ARR.

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Prepare for an executive meeting

    Tomorrow your VP meets the customer COO. Adoption is 72%, a P1 bug was fixed last week, and a competitor has been courting them. Write the 3-2-1 brief.

    Show model answer

    3 outcomes: on-time delivery +6 pts, adoption 72%, cost per shipment −4%. 2 risks: competitor interest, P1 aftermath — acknowledge and show the fix. 1 ask: agree a joint 2-quarter roadmap and name a second executive stakeholder.

    Module 6

    Escalations & Communication

    Managing escalations, difficult conversations and written communication with customers.

    Escalation framework

    SeverityDefinitionResponseOwnerUpdate cadence
    Sev 1Business-critical outage or data risk15 minIncident lead + CSM + exec sponsorEvery 1-2 hours
    Sev 2Major feature broken, workaround exists2 hoursSupport lead + CSMDaily
    Sev 3Minor issue1 business daySupportWeekly
    Relationship escalationCustomer exec dissatisfiedSame dayCSM + vendor execUntil resolved

    Communicating during an incident

    Silence is worse than bad news.
    • Lead with facts: what happened, who is affected, what you are doing.
    • Never speculate on root cause before you know it.
    • Own the problem, avoid blaming other teams.
    • Close the loop with a written review and prevention steps.

    Difficult conversations

    Do

    • Listen fully before responding
    • Name the impact on them
    • Offer a clear next step with a date
    • Follow up in writing

    Avoid

    • Defensive explanations
    • Over-promising on timelines
    • Discounting before diagnosing
    • Blaming product or sales

    A useful structure: Acknowledge → Clarify → Commit → Confirm.

    Writing a customer-ready update

    Subject: [Sev 2] Report export delays — update 14:00 UTC

What happened: Exports over 100k rows are timing out since 09:10 UTC.
Impact: 12 of your users in the Finance team; other features unaffected.
Workaround: Export in batches of 50k rows (steps attached).
What we are doing: Engineering has identified the cause and is deploying a fix.
Next update: 16:00 UTC, or sooner if resolved.

— Name, Customer Success Manager

    Real-world example

    🌍
    Real-world example — Analytics vendor

    A 6-hour dashboard outage hit a top account before quarter-end. The CSM sent a first message within 12 minutes, updated every hour, and followed with a post-incident report and a service credit proposal. The CFO's feedback: "Frankly, how you communicated is why we are staying."

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Escalation triage

    Three issues arrive: (A) a customer exec emails "we are evaluating alternatives"; (B) SSO login fails for 5% of users; (C) a requested report column. Rank and assign actions.

    Show model answer

    A first (relationship escalation, same-day call with CSM + vendor exec). B second (Sev 2, support + engineering, daily updates). C third (Sev 3, add to the roadmap request log and reply with status).

    Tools

    • CRM (Salesforce, HubSpot): contacts, roles and relationship maps.
    • CS platforms (Gainsight, Totango): sponsor tracking, CTAs, success plans, timeline.
    • Collaboration (shared Slack/Teams channels, shared documents): fast communication.
    • Presentation tools: QBR templates with auto-populated data.

    Case study A: Rescuing a single-threaded account

    🌍
    Real-world example — Marketing platform

    Situation: $400k account, one champion who left. Actions: executive outreach, a sponsor pairing, interviews with five users, a recovery QBR with outcomes. Result: a new champion emerged in the product team, renewal closed with +10%.

    Case study B: QBR redesign

    🌍
    Real-world example — Supply-chain software

    Situation: QBRs were 45-slide vendor monologues with 30% attendance. Actions: 10-slide outcome story, co-created agenda, sponsor pre-brief, one decision per meeting. Result: attendance 85%, two expansions sourced from QBRs in two quarters.

    Common mistakes to avoid

    • Treating QBR as a product demo
    • No pre-alignment with the champion
    • Only meeting users, never executives
    • Skipping governance after the first year
    • Taking notes without owners or dates
    • Ignoring non-supporters in the account

    Practice

    🛠️
    Hands-on exercise — Redesign a governance plan

    You manage 40 accounts: 4 strategic, 16 growth, 20 scaled. Propose a governance model per segment.

    Show model answer

    Strategic: monthly check-in, quarterly QBR, exec sponsor pairing, joint account plan. Growth: bi-monthly check-in, semi-annual QBR, shared success plan. Scaled: digital QBR (automated report), pooled office hours, escalation on risk signals.
