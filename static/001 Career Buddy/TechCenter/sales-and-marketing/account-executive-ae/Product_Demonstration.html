    Demo Foundations

    What a product demonstration is, the types of demos, and why feature tours lose deals while outcome-led demos win them.

    🖥️ What Is a Product Demonstration?

    Fresher

    A product demonstration is a structured, buyer-specific showing of how your solution solves the problems found in discovery. The goal is not to show the product; it is to help the buyer see themselves succeeding with it.

    PointDetail
    What it isA tailored walkthrough that links product capability to the buyer’s pains, outcomes and workflow.
    Why it mattersA demo is where interest becomes conviction. Buyers remember outcomes they saw, not menus they were shown.
    How it worksRecap discovery → show the end result first → walk the key workflows → connect each to value → confirm fit → agree next steps.
    Who does itThe AE owns the story; a solution consultant (SC) or sales engineer supports with technical depth.
    TermMeaningExample
    FeatureWhat the product has or doesAutomated approval routing
    AdvantageWhat the feature does better or differentlyRoutes by amount, region and risk
    BenefitWhat it means for the buyerApprovals take hours not days
    Outcome / valueMeasurable business resultQuarter-end close 3 days faster; £120k saved a year
    💡
    The “so what? test”: after every feature you show, say or imagine the buyer asking “so what?” If you cannot answer in business terms, do not show it.

    🗂️ Types of Demonstrations

    FresherIntermediate
    TypePurposeBest when
    Discovery-led (custom) demoTailored to pains found in discoveryMost mid-market and enterprise deals
    Standard / overview demoShort product orientationEarly interest, small deals, webinars
    Executive briefing15–20 min on outcomes, risk and ROIEconomic buyer, steering committee
    Technical deep diveArchitecture, security, integrationIT and security evaluators
    Sandbox / hands-onBuyer clicks through a test environmentUsers need to feel the workflow

    📌
    A generic demo given before discovery is the most common cause of “looks nice, we will think about it.”

    ⚖️ Feature Tour vs Outcome-Led Demo

    FresherIntermediate

    Feature tour (avoid)

    • Follows the menu order
    • Shows every module
    • Uses vendor sample data
    • Ends with “any questions?”

    Outcome-led demo (aim for)

    • Follows the buyer’s workflow
    • Shows only what solves their pains
    • Uses their language and, ideally, their data
    • Ends with a recap of value and a committed next step
    ⚠️
    Showing 40 features to impress usually dilutes the three that matter. A good rule: 3 pains, 3 workflows, 3 outcomes.

    🎬 Demo Structure: Tell–Show–Tell and Beyond

    IntermediateAdvanced
    1. Opening (5 min): confirm agenda, goals and who decides what at the end.
    2. Recap (5 min): restate pains and success criteria in the buyer’s words.
    3. End-state first (3 min): show the final dashboard or result — the “reverse demo.”
    4. Workflows (25 min): for each pain: tell the problem, show the solution, tell the value (Tell–Show–Tell).
    5. Value recap (5 min): link each workflow to an outcome and number.
    6. Next steps (5–10 min): agree a dated, mutual action.
    TechniqueWhat it does
    Reverse demoStarts with the outcome so buyers know why they are watching
    Day-in-the-lifeWalks a role through a real task from start to finish
    Before / AfterContrasts today’s pain with tomorrow’s workflow
    Demo-by-personaSwitches views for finance, ops and IT in one session
    Checkpoint questions“Is this how you would do it today?” keeps the buyer involved
    ✅
    Great demos are conversations with screens. Aim to ask a question every 3–5 minutes.

    👥 Tailoring to Audience and Persona

    IntermediateAdvanced
    PersonaWants to seeLanguage to use
    Economic buyerOutcomes, risk reduction, payback“ROI, time to value, risk”
    Functional managerTeam productivity, control, reporting“Cycle time, visibility, SLA”

    Translating Features into Business Outcomes

    FAB and FABO, the “so what” chain, mapping features to discovery pains and speaking the buyer’s language.

    🔁 From Feature to Benefit to Outcome

    FresherIntermediateAdvanced
    LevelStatementWeakness / strength
    Feature“We have workflow automation.”Vendor-centric; no value
    Advantage“It routes tasks automatically by rule.”Better, still generic
    Benefit“Managers no longer chase approvals.”Buyer-centric
    Outcome (FABO)“Approval time drops from 5 days to 1, freeing about 120 manager hours a month.”Quantified and tied to a metric
    translationFeature  : Real-time inventory sync
So what? : Stock levels are always current
So what? : Store managers reorder before items run out
Outcome  : Fewer stock-outs -> est. 2% sales recovered in affected stores
    💡
    Keep asking “so what?” until you reach a number the buyer already tracks: revenue, cost, risk, time, quality or satisfaction.

    🗺️ Mapping Features to Discovery Pains

    IntermediateAdvanced
    Buyer pain (from discovery)Capability to showOutcome statement
    Reports take 2 analysts 3 daysAutomated scheduled reporting“Reports in minutes; 48 analyst-days a year redeployed.”
    Forecast misses by 25%Pipeline risk scoring and roll-ups“Spot risky deals 3 weeks earlier; forecast within 5–8%.”
    Slow onboarding (6 weeks)Guided onboarding workflow“Go-live in 2 weeks; revenue starts 4 weeks sooner.”
    Compliance exposureAudit trail and permissions“Evidence in one click; audit prep from 10 days to 2.”
    ⚠️
    Only demo features with a pain behind them. Features with no mapped pain belong in a later conversation, if at all.

    🗣️ Before–After–Bridge and Story Language

    IntermediateAdvanced

    Before–After–Bridge (BAB): describe the painful “before,” the improved “after,” and the product as the bridge.

    BABBefore : Your analysts merge five spreadsheets every month (3 days).
After  : A single live report that refreshes automatically.
Bridge : This connector pulls each regional file nightly - let me show you.
    • Use their words and numbers from discovery.
    • Prefer verbs and outcomes (“cut”, “avoid”, “see”) over modules and menus.
    • Use a one-sentence customer story as proof, then return the focus to the buyer.

    ROI and Business Case

    Value drivers, ROI, payback, TCO and building a credible, buyer-validated business case.

    📈 Value Drivers and Core Formulas

    IntermediateAdvanced
    Value driverExamples of measures
    Revenue growthWin rate, deal size, conversion, retention, new-market speed
    Cost reductionLabour hours, tool consolidation, error and rework cost
    Risk reductionCompliance fines avoided, downtime, security incidents
    Strategic / experienceTime to market, customer satisfaction, employee retention
    MeasureFormulaMeaning
    ROI(Total benefit − Total cost) ÷ Total costReturn per unit of money invested
    Payback periodTotal cost ÷ (benefit per month)Months until the investment is recovered
    TCOLicence + implementation + training + support + internal effortFull cost of ownership over the term
    NPVSum of discounted future net cash flowsValue in today’s money (finance teams love it)
    exampleAnnual benefit : 193,200
Annual cost    : 60,000
ROI (year 1)   : (193,200 - 60,000) / 60,000 = 222%
Payback        : 60,000 / (193,200/12) = 3.7 months

    🧮 Building a Credible Business Case

    AdvancedExpert
    1. Baseline: today’s metric from the buyer (hours, error rate, cost).
    2. Improvement assumption: conservative, evidence-based (customer data, pilot, benchmark).
    3. Benefit: baseline × improvement × volume × unit value.
    4. Cost: full TCO, including internal effort — never hide it.
    5. Sensitivity: show a conservative, expected and optimistic case.
    6. Validation: have the buyer (and finance) confirm every input.
    ScenarioImprovementAnnual benefitROI (cost 60,000)
    Conservative60% of expected115,92093%
    Expected100%193,200222%
    Optimistic130%251,160319%
    ⚠️
    An inflated ROI destroys trust. A defensible 150% beats an unbelievable 900%. Show assumptions openly so finance can challenge and adopt them.

    🧾 Value Selling Artifacts

    IntermediateAdvancedExpert
    ArtifactUse
    ROI calculatorInteractive inputs from the buyer; output they own
    Value hypothesisEarly estimate based on peers, refined in discovery

    Delivery, Engagement and Technical Proof

    Presenting with confidence, handling questions and failures, running POCs and remote demos.

    🎤 Delivery Skills and Handling Questions

    FresherIntermediateAdvanced
    • Narrate intent: “I am going to show you how approvals are routed, because you said delays cost you.”
    • Slow down on clicks; say what you are doing before you do it.
    • Park off-topic questions on a visible list and return to them.
    • Answer with a question when a feature request is vague: “What would you do with that?”
    • Be honest about gaps: “We do not do that natively; here is how customers solve it.”
    SituationGood response
    Buyer asks about a module you were not planning to showLink to a pain; if none, park it and offer a follow-up
    A detailed technical question appearsHand to the SC, then summarise the business effect
    The demo environment failsStay calm, switch to the recorded backup or screenshots, return to value
    A stakeholder becomes negativeAcknowledge, ask for the concern, address it with evidence

    🧪 POCs, Pilots and Trials

    AdvancedExpert
    ElementGood practice
    Success criteriaWritten and agreed before start (metrics, data, owners)
    ScopeSmall enough to complete in 2–6 weeks, large enough to prove value
    ResourcesNamed customer champion and technical owner
    Decision rule“If we hit criteria X and Y, we proceed to contract by date Z.”
    ReviewWeekly checkpoint and final readout with the economic buyer
    ⚠️
    A POC without success criteria and a decision rule becomes free consulting that never ends.

    🌐 Remote and Multi-Stakeholder Demos

    IntermediateAdvanced
    • Share only the required window; close notifications and unrelated tabs.
    • Use polls or chat questions to keep remote audiences engaged.
    • Send the agenda and pre-reads 24 hours ahead; record with permission.
    • Name roles on screen (“Priya — finance view”) so people see themselves in the demo.
    • End with a one-slide recap: pains, outcomes, agreed next step.

    📘 Worked Case: From Feature Tour to Outcome Demo

    AdvancedExpert

    Case: Orbit Finance Cloud (illustrative)

    StageWhat happened
    SituationAE gave a 60-minute feature tour to a CFO and two controllers; feedback: “impressive, but not sure why now.”
    DiagnosisDemo started with a login screen, covered 12 modules, used vendor data, ended with “any questions?”
    Re-planAE re-engaged the CFO, replayed discovery: month-end close takes 9 days; auditors flagged manual journals.
    New demo35 minutes, three workflows (reconciliation, journal approval, audit trail), shown on their sample ledger.
    Business caseClose from 9 to 5 days; 300 analyst hours saved a year; audit prep cut from 10 to 2 days; ROI 180% (conservative).
    ResultCFO sponsored a 4-week pilot with agreed success criteria; signed after a successful readout.

    ⚠️ Failure Modes and Expert Checklist

    AdvancedExpert

    Failure modes

    • Demo before discovery
    • Too many features
    • No business case
    • Vendor data only
    • Single persona served
    • No next step

    Expert checklist

    • Does every screen map to a pain?
    • Did the buyer say “yes, that is how we do it”?
    • Are outcomes quantified and validated?
    • Is a mutual next step booked?
    • Are success criteria written for any pilot?

    🧪 Practice: Exercises and Demo Scorecard

    AdvancedExpert
    💡
    🧪 Exercise — Translate five features
    Take any five product features you know.
    1. Write the feature, advantage, benefit and quantified outcome for each.
    2. Name the persona who cares most about each.
    3. Discard any with no matching pain.
    💡
    🧪 Exercise — Build a 3-line ROI
    A customer’s 25 support agents each lose 2 hours a week to manual ticket tagging (46 working weeks). Loaded cost is 30 per hour; your solution costs 25,000 a year.
    1. Calculate hours lost and annual cost.
    2. Compute ROI and payback assuming 70% of the time is recovered.
    3. Write one sentence to present to the CFO.
