    Module 01 · Foundations

    Foundations & Setup

    What actually makes a machine a "robot," the loop every robot runs no matter its shape, and the simulation tools you'll use before touching real hardware.

    🤖 What is a Robot?

    A robot is a physical machine that senses its environment, makes decisions, and acts on the physical world to accomplish a task — the combination of all three is what separates a robot from a plain sensor or a plain motor.

    👁️
    Sense
    Perceive the environment through cameras, lidar, encoders, and other sensors.
    🧠
    Think
    Process sensor data and decide what to do next.
    ⚙️
    Act
    Move motors, grippers, or wheels to change the physical world.

    Levels of autonomy

    Not every robot decides for itself — "robot" spans a spectrum from fully teleoperated (a human drives every motion, like a surgical robot) to fully autonomous (the robot senses, plans, and acts with no human in the loop, like a warehouse AMR). Most real deployed systems sit somewhere in between: supervisory control, where a human sets high-level goals and the robot works out the low-level execution, with a human able to intervene if something looks wrong.

    LevelWho decidesExample
    Manual / teleoperatedHuman, every motionBomb-disposal robot, surgical robot
    SupervisoryHuman sets goals, robot executesWarehouse pick-and-place, robotic vacuum
    Fully autonomousRobot, end to endSelf-driving research platforms, some agricultural robots

    📐 Degrees of Freedom

    Degrees of Freedom (DOF) count the independent ways a robot's joints can move — more DOF means more flexibility in reachable positions, but also more complexity to control.

    concept# a typical 6-DOF industrial arm can reach any position AND orientation
# in its workspace — 3 DOF for position (x,y,z) + 3 for orientation (roll,pitch,yaw)

    Under-actuated vs over-actuated systems

    A system is fully-actuated when it has exactly as many independently controllable actuators as DOF — a standard 6-axis industrial arm is a common example. It's under-actuated when it has fewer actuators than DOF (a walking robot's body swinging freely between footsteps, a drone which has 6 DOF but only 4 independent rotor inputs) — these are dramatically harder to control because you can't independently command every degree of freedom, and must rely on dynamics and clever sequencing instead. A system is redundant (or kinematically redundant) when it has more actuated DOF than strictly needed to reach a target pose — a 7-DOF arm reaching a 6-DOF target pose has one "spare" DOF it can use to avoid obstacles or singularities while keeping the end effector fixed.

    💡
    A drone is the classic under-actuated example: it has 6 DOF (x,y,z,roll,pitch,yaw) but typically only 4 direct control inputs (total thrust + 3 torques). It reaches lateral position by first tilting (changing roll/pitch) and then letting thrust push it sideways — position is controlled indirectly through orientation.
    Module 02 · Fundamentals

    Core Robotics Fundamentals

    The vocabulary every roboticist shares: how a robot's body is structured, how positions are described, and the basic distinction that underlies all robot control.

    🦾 Robot Anatomy

    PartRole
    LinkA rigid body segment between two joints
    JointConnects two links, allowing relative motion (rotary or linear)
    End effectorThe "hand" — gripper, tool, or sensor at the tip of the arm
    BaseThe fixed (or mobile) foundation the robot is built on

    🧭 Coordinate Frames

    Every point on a robot is described relative to a coordinate frame. Robots typically juggle several: the world frame, the base frame, and a frame attached to each link.

    Figure 2.1 · Each frame nests inside the one before it via a transform

    Homogeneous transforms

    Converting a point from one frame to another combines a rotation and a translation. Rather than tracking these separately, robotics packs both into a single 4×4 homogeneous transformation matrix, which makes chaining multiple frame conversions a simple matrix multiplication instead of a growing pile of separate rotation/translation bookkeeping.

    T = [ R  t ]     R = 3×3 rotation matrix
    [ 0  1 ]     t = 3×1 translation vector

p_world = T_base→world · T_link→base · p_link   # chain transforms left to right
    📝
    In ROS this is exactly what the tf2 library manages for you — every sensor and link publishes its transform relative to a parent frame, and tf2 resolves the full chain to convert a point between any two frames on request.

    🔁 Open-Loop vs Closed-Loop Control

    🔵 Open-Loop

    Sends a command and never checks the actual result — simple, but drifts with any disturbance or error over time.

    🟢 Closed-Loop (Feedback)

    Continuously measures the actual output and corrects the command based on the error — what nearly all real robots use.

    🖐️ End Effectors

    TypeUsed for
    Parallel gripperPicking up rigid objects
    Vacuum/suction cupFlat or smooth surfaces (boxes, glass)
    Welding torch / spray nozzleManufacturing tasks, not grasping

    🗺️ Robot Workspace

    The workspace is the full set of positions an end effector can reach. Its shape depends entirely on the arm's joint types and link lengths — planning a task always starts by checking whether the target lies inside it.

    Workspace typeDefinition
    Reachable workspaceEvery point the end effector can reach in any orientation
    Dexterous workspaceEvery point the end effector can reach in every possible orientation — always a subset of the reachable workspace, often much smaller
    ⚠️
    A target can be inside the reachable workspace but unreachable at the orientation you actually need — a common source of "impossible" IK requests that aren't a bug in the solver, just outside the dexterous workspace.

    Module 03

    Kinematics

    The geometry of motion — how joint angles translate into end-effector position, and how to solve the problem in reverse.

    ➡️ Forward Kinematics

    Given all the joint angles, forward kinematics (FK) computes exactly where the end effector ends up. This direction is always straightforward — just chain the transforms link by link.

    T_end = T_base→link1 · T_link1→link2 · ... · T_linkN→endEffector

    ⬅️ Inverse Kinematics

    Inverse kinematics (IK) goes the other way: given a desired end-effector position, find the joint angles that achieve it. This is much harder — solutions may not exist, or multiple solutions ("elbow up" vs "elbow down") may exist for the same target.

    Figure 3.1 · Both configurations reach the same end point

    Analytical vs numerical IK

    For simple arms (typically 6 DOF with specific joint geometries), a closed-form analytical solution exists — direct trigonometric equations that give exact joint angles instantly. Most real arms, especially redundant ones, instead rely on numerical IK: start from a guess, use the Jacobian to iteratively nudge the joint angles toward the target, and converge over several steps. Numerical solvers are more general but slower and can get stuck in local minima or fail to converge if the target is unreachable or too close to a singularity.

    ApproachSpeedHandles redundancy
    AnalyticalInstant, closed-formNo — needs a specific joint structure
    Numerical (e.g. Jacobian-based, CCD)Iterative, slowerYes — works for any arm geometry

    📏 DH Parameters

    Denavit-Hartenberg (DH) parameters are a standardized way to describe the geometry of a robot arm using just four numbers per joint, making forward kinematics systematic and easy to automate.

    ParameterDescribes
    aLink length
    α (alpha)Link twist
    dLink offset
    θ (theta)Joint angle

    🧮 Jacobians & Velocity Kinematics

    The Jacobian matrix relates joint velocities to end-effector velocity — essential for smooth, continuous motion control rather than just reaching static target poses.

    end_effector_velocity = J(θ) · joint_velocities

    Singularities

    A singularity is a joint configuration where the Jacobian loses rank — the arm momentarily loses the ability to move in some direction no matter how the joints are commanded (think of a fully-extended arm: it physically cannot move any further outward). Near a singularity, small desired end-effector velocities require enormous, sometimes unachievable joint velocities, which is why motion planners actively steer trajectories away from these configurations rather than through them.

    Module 04

    Dynamics & Control Systems

    Forces, torques, and the feedback loop that turns a desired position into an actual, physically-achieved one.

    ⚖️ Statics vs Dynamics

    🔵 Statics

    Analyzes forces and torques on a robot at rest or moving at constant velocity — no acceleration involved.

    🟢 Dynamics

    Accounts for acceleration, mass, and inertia — needed for fast or heavy-load motion where forces actually change speed.

    🎛️ PID Control

    The PID controller is the workhorse of robot control — it computes a correction signal from three terms based on the error between desired and actual state.

    output = Kp·error + Ki·∫error dt + Kd·(d(error)/dt)
    TermResponds to
    P (Proportional)The current error — bigger error, bigger correction
    I (Integral)Accumulated past error — eliminates steady-state offset
    D (Derivative)The rate of change of error — dampens overshoot

    🔧 Tuning a PID Controller

    Figure 4.1 · A response curve settling toward the target after overshoot
    ⚠️
    Tuning is iterative: raise Kp until the system responds quickly but starts to oscillate, then add Kd to damp the oscillation, then add a small Ki to remove any remaining steady-state error.

    Two common failure modes

    Integral windup happens when the error stays large for a long time (e.g. the actuator is physically saturated and can't move any faster) — the integral term keeps accumulating unboundedly, and once the system finally catches up it massively overshoots. The standard fix is anti-windup clamping: cap the integral term, or freeze its accumulation while the actuator is saturated. Derivative kick happens when the setpoint itself jumps suddenly (not the error from disturbance) — the instantaneous rate-of-change spikes and the D-term fires a sharp, often physically damaging correction. The fix is computing the derivative of the *measurement* rather than the error, so a setpoint change doesn't create a false spike.

    🌀 Stability & Feedback

    A control system is stable if, after a disturbance, it settles back toward the target rather than oscillating with growing amplitude or diverging entirely. Feedback is what makes stability possible — without measuring the actual state, a controller can't correct for real-world error.

    Engineers describe how "well" a stable system responds using a few standard metrics that a PID tune is ultimately optimizing for:

    MetricWhat it measures
    Rise timeHow quickly the system first reaches near the target
    OvershootHow far past the target it swings before settling
    Settling timeHow long until it stays within an acceptable band of the target
    Steady-state errorThe remaining gap once the system has settled

    Module 05

    Sensors & Perception

    How a robot perceives itself and its surroundings — and why raw sensor data is never clean enough to trust directly.

    📡 Proprioceptive vs Exteroceptive Sensors

    🔵 Proprioceptive

    Measures the robot's own internal state — joint angles, wheel speed, battery level.

    🟢 Exteroceptive

    Measures the external environment — cameras, lidar, ultrasonic distance sensors.

    📶 Common Sensor Types

    SensorMeasures
    EncoderJoint angle or wheel rotation
    IMU (Inertial Measurement Unit)Acceleration and angular velocity — used for orientation
    LidarDistance to surrounding objects via laser time-of-flight
    Ultrasonic sensorShort-range distance via sound wave echo
    CameraVisual images — used for object detection and recognition

    📷 Cameras & Computer Vision Basics

    Vision pipelines typically detect edges/features, classify objects, and estimate their pose (position + orientation) relative to the robot.

    Python · OpenCVimport cv2
frame = cv2.imread("scene.jpg")
gray = cv2.cvtColor(frame, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)
edges = cv2.Canny(gray, 100, 200)  # classic edge detection

    🔗 Sensor Fusion

    No single sensor is perfect — cameras struggle in the dark, lidar can't read color, IMUs drift over time. Sensor fusion (often via a Kalman filter) combines multiple noisy, imperfect sensors into one more reliable estimate.

    A concrete example: an IMU alone drifts badly over time (double-integrating noisy acceleration compounds error quickly), while GPS is accurate over the long term but noisy and low-frequency moment to moment. Fusing them — trusting the IMU for fast, short-term changes and GPS to continually correct the long-term drift — gives a far better position estimate than either sensor alone. This exact pairing (IMU + GPS via an Extended Kalman Filter) is the backbone of most outdoor mobile robot and drone localization stacks.

    🌫️ Sensor Noise & Filtering

    Raw sensor readings are always noisy. A Kalman filter is the classic algorithm for estimating the true state of a system from a sequence of noisy measurements, weighing new data against its prior prediction.

    📌
    The Kalman filter alternates between two steps: predict (use the motion model to guess the next state) and update (correct that guess using a new noisy measurement).

    How much to trust each source

    The filter's key insight is weighting the prediction and the new measurement by their respective uncertainty (variance) — if the sensor is known to be noisy, it's trusted less; if the motion model's prediction has grown uncertain (e.g. a lot of time has passed since the last update), the new measurement is trusted more. This weighting, called the Kalman gain, is recomputed at every step from the two variances, which is why the filter adapts automatically rather than using a fixed blending ratio.

    gain = prediction_uncertainty / (prediction_uncertainty + measurement_uncertainty)
estimate = prediction + gain × (measurement − prediction)
    ⚠️
    The standard Kalman filter assumes linear motion and Gaussian noise. Real robots are usually nonlinear (a turning car, a rotating arm), which is why the Extended Kalman Filter (EKF) — linearizing around the current estimate at each step — or the particle filter (Module 9) is used instead in practice.

    Module 06

    Actuators & Mechanisms

    The muscles of a robot — how electrical signals become physical motion, and the mechanical trade-offs behind speed, torque, and precision.

    ⚙️ Motor Types

    Motor typeStrength
    DC motorSimple, cheap, continuous rotation
    Servo motorPrecise angular position control, built-in feedback
    Stepper motorPrecise open-loop positioning in fixed increments
    Brushless DC (BLDC)High efficiency and durability — used in drones

    🔩 Gearing & Torque

    Gears trade speed for torque — a motor that spins fast but weak can be geared down to spin slower with much more force.

    output_torque = input_torque × gear_ratio
output_speed  = input_speed  / gear_ratio

    Backlash — the hidden cost of gearing

    Backlash is the small amount of "slop" or free play between meshing gear teeth. It's invisible when the motor is driving steadily in one direction, but becomes very visible the moment direction reverses — the output doesn't move at all for a brief instant while the gear teeth re-engage on the other side. For precision robotics this is a real problem: it introduces position error that a simple encoder on the motor (rather than on the output shaft, after the gearbox) can't even see. High-precision arms often use harmonic drives — a gearing mechanism with near-zero backlash — specifically to avoid this.

    🖐️ Grippers & Manipulation

    Manipulation isn't just closing a gripper — it requires knowing the object's pose, choosing a grasp point that won't slip, and controlling grip force to avoid crushing fragile objects or dropping heavy ones.

    Control modeUsed for
    Position controlClosing to a fixed width — fine for rigid, known-size objects
    Force/torque controlGrasping unknown or fragile objects — close until a target contact force is sensed, not a target position
    💡
    Force-controlled grasping typically closes the gripper slowly while monitoring a force/torque sensor at the wrist or fingertips, stopping the instant contact force reaches a safe threshold — this is what lets a robot pick up both a steel bolt and a paper cup without a different program for each.

    🏗️ Materials & Mechanical Design

    MaterialTrade-off
    AluminumLight and strong, but more expensive to machine
    SteelVery strong and stiff, but heavy
    3D-printed plasticCheap and fast to prototype, weaker under load

    Module 07

    Robot Programming & ROS

    The Robot Operating System — the standard framework that lets many independent pieces of robot software talk to each other reliably.

    🧩 Why Middleware Matters

    A robot's software is rarely one program — it's dozens of independent processes (camera driver, motor controller, path planner) that must communicate. ROS (Robot Operating System) standardizes exactly how they do that, so you don't reinvent messaging infrastructure for every project.

    ROS 1 vs ROS 2

    ROS 2 is a ground-up rewrite, not just a version bump. ROS 1 relied on a single central process (the "master") for node discovery — if it crashed, the whole system's ability to connect new nodes went with it. ROS 2 uses DDS (Data Distribution Service), a decentralized, industrial-grade communication standard with no single point of failure, along with configurable Quality of Service (QoS) — you can choose whether a topic should guarantee delivery (like a service call) or just drop old messages under load (like a high-rate camera feed). ROS 2 is now the standard for new projects; ROS 1 reached its final supported release in 2025.

    📡 ROS Nodes & Topics

    Figure 7.1 · Nodes communicate over named topics

    A node is a single running process. A topic is a named channel nodes publish messages to and subscribe to — decoupling producers and consumers completely.

    📤 Publishers & Subscribers

    Python · rclpypub = node.create_publisher(String, '/status', 10)
msg = String()
msg.data = "battery ok"
pub.publish(msg)   # any node subscribed to /status receives this

    🔧 Services & Actions

    PatternWhen to use
    TopicContinuous streaming data, no reply expected (sensor readings)
    ServiceA quick request-response call, like a function call across processes
    ActionA long-running task with progress feedback and the ability to cancel (e.g. "navigate to point")

    💻 Writing a Simple ROS Node

    Python · rclpyimport rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node

class MyNode(Node):
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('my_node')
        self.get_logger().info('Node started')

rclpy.init()
rclpy.spin(MyNode())

    rclpy.spin() hands control to ROS's event loop, which calls your subscriber/service callbacks as messages arrive — conceptually similar to Node.js's event loop, but here it's dispatching robot messages instead of I/O completions.

    Launch files

    A real robot rarely runs from a single node — a launch file starts many nodes together with their configuration, so you don't manually run a dozen terminal commands every time.

    Module 08

    Motion Planning & Navigation

    How a robot decides where to go and how to get there without colliding with anything along the way.

    🗺️ Path Planning vs Trajectory Planning

    🔵 Path Planning

    Finds a collision-free geometric route from start to goal — no timing involved.

    🟢 Trajectory Planning

    Adds timing and velocity/acceleration profiles to that path so it can actually be executed smoothly.

    🔍 Grid-Based Search — A*

    A* searches a grid map for the shortest collision-free path, using a heuristic (usually straight-line distance to the goal) to explore promising directions first.

    f(n) = g(n) + h(n)
# g(n): actual cost from start to node n
# h(n): heuristic estimated cost from n to goal

    A* is a generalization of Dijkstra's algorithm: with h(n) = 0 everywhere, A* degrades to plain Dijkstra (explores uniformly in all directions, guaranteed shortest path but slower). A well-chosen heuristic focuses the search toward the goal, exploring far fewer nodes while still guaranteeing the optimal path — provided the heuristic never overestimates the true remaining cost (this property is called "admissible").

    🌳 Sampling-Based Planning — RRT

    Rapidly-exploring Random Trees (RRT) randomly sample points in the space and grow a tree toward them, making them effective in high-dimensional spaces (like a 6-DOF arm) where grid search becomes impractical.

    Plain RRT finds a valid path quickly, but it's rarely a good one — often jagged and far from optimal. RRT* extends the algorithm to keep improving the tree as more samples are added, asymptotically converging toward the optimal path the longer it's given to run. In practice, RRT finds a first working plan almost instantly; RRT* is used when there's a time budget to refine it.

    🚧 Obstacle Avoidance

    Global planners (A*, RRT) compute a path once against a known map; local obstacle avoidance reacts in real time to things the map didn't know about — a person walking into the robot's path, for example.

    A common local approach is the Dynamic Window Approach (DWA): at each control cycle, sample a set of achievable velocity commands (given the robot's current speed and acceleration limits), simulate a short trajectory forward for each, discard any that would collide, and pick the surviving one that best balances progress toward the goal, obstacle clearance, and speed. Because it re-plans every cycle at high frequency, it reacts to moving obstacles the global planner never knew about.

    Module 09

    SLAM & Localization

    How a robot figures out both where it is and what its environment looks like — usually at the same time, from scratch.

    📍 The Localization Problem

    Localization answers "where am I?" given a known map. Wheel encoders alone drift over time due to slippage and imperfect measurements — this drift is called odometry error, and it's why localization needs external references too.

    🗺️ What is SLAM?

    Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM) builds a map of an unknown environment while simultaneously tracking the robot's position within that same map — used any time a robot enters a space with no prior map, like a vacuum robot in a new house.

    🥚 The Chicken-and-Egg Problem

    SLAM is hard precisely because the two problems depend on each other: you need a good map to localize accurately, but you need to know your position accurately to build a good map. Modern SLAM algorithms solve both jointly rather than sequentially.

    ApproachIdea
    EKF-SLAMTrack robot pose + all landmark positions in one large Extended Kalman Filter state vector
    Graph-based SLAMRepresent poses and landmark observations as nodes/edges in a graph, then solve for the most consistent overall layout ("pose graph optimization")
    Particle filter SLAM (FastSLAM)Each particle carries its own full map hypothesis, resampled as evidence arrives

    Graph-based SLAM (used by most modern systems, e.g. Cartographer, ORB-SLAM) scales far better to large environments than EKF-SLAM, since it doesn't require maintaining one giant covariance matrix over every landmark ever seen.

    🎯 Particle Filters

    A particle filter represents the robot's possible position as a cloud of weighted hypotheses ("particles"). Each new sensor reading re-weights the particles toward the more likely locations, and the cloud gradually converges on the true position.

    Figure 9.1 · Particles tighten as more sensor evidence arrives

    🔁 Loop Closure

    Loop closure happens when a robot recognizes it has returned to a previously visited location, letting it correct accumulated drift across the whole map — a critical step for building consistent large-scale maps.

    Detecting a loop closure means comparing the current sensor reading against past ones to find a match — visually (does this camera frame look like one we've seen before?) or geometrically (does this lidar scan align with a past scan?). Once a match is found and confirmed, the SLAM backend distributes the resulting correction backward across the whole trajectory, not just the current pose — this is why a single loop closure can visibly "snap" a previously drifted, curved map back into a straight, consistent hallway.

    ⚠️
    A false loop closure — matching two different locations that happen to look similar (e.g. two identical office cubicles) — can corrupt the entire map instantly. Robust SLAM systems verify a candidate match geometrically before accepting it, not just on visual similarity alone.

    Module 10

    Machine Learning in Robotics

    Where learned models are replacing hand-coded rules — perception, control policies, and the gap between simulation and reality.

    🧠 Where ML Fits in Robotics

    Robotics stageML application
    PerceptionObject detection, semantic segmentation from camera images
    PlanningLearned policies replacing hand-tuned planners in complex environments
    ControlLearned locomotion policies for legged robots on uneven terrain

    📦 Object Detection for Robots

    Deep learning models (like YOLO) let robots identify and localize objects in a camera frame in real time, feeding that information directly into grasp planning or navigation decisions.

    🎮 Reinforcement Learning

    In reinforcement learning (RL), a robot learns a control policy by trial and error, receiving a reward signal and adjusting its behavior to maximize cumulative reward over time — no hand-written control rules needed.

    conceptstate → policy(state) → action → reward → update policy to prefer higher-reward actions

    Designing the reward function is often the hardest part — a robot given a reward for "moving forward fast" might learn to fling itself forward and fall rather than walk, technically maximizing the reward exactly as specified while completely missing the intent. This mismatch, called reward hacking, is why real RL reward functions are usually carefully shaped with several weighted terms (progress, energy use, stability, smoothness) rather than one single simple signal.

    📝
    Policy learning algorithms you'll see referenced in robotics papers: PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) is the current default for its stability; SAC (Soft Actor-Critic) is popular for continuous control tasks like locomotion due to its sample efficiency.

    👀 Imitation Learning

    Instead of exploring by trial and error, imitation learning trains a policy directly from demonstrations — a human teleoperating the robot, for example — which is often faster and safer than pure RL for many tasks.

    🌉 Sim-to-Real Transfer

    Training in simulation is fast, cheap, and safe — but policies trained purely in simulation often fail on real hardware due to the reality gap (imperfect physics, sensor noise the simulator didn't model). Domain randomization — varying simulated textures, lighting, and physics during training — helps policies generalize to the messier real world.

    The idea behind domain randomization is counterintuitive at first: instead of trying to make the simulator perfectly match reality (extremely hard), you deliberately make the simulator less accurate by randomizing dozens of parameters every training episode — friction coefficients, motor strength, lighting, textures, even camera noise. A policy that has already learned to succeed across thousands of wildly different simulated variations treats the one real-world physics as just another point inside a range it already handles, rather than an unfamiliar edge case.

    Randomized parameterWhy it matters
    Friction / massReal surfaces and payloads are never known exactly
    Sensor noise & latencyReal cameras/IMUs are never as clean or instant as a simulator's
    Visual textures & lightingVision policies must not overfit to the simulator's specific rendering

    Module 11

    Platforms & Applications

    A tour of where robots actually work today, and the safety and ethical questions that come with putting them near people.

    🏭 Industrial Robotics manufacturing

    Fixed-base robot arms dominate manufacturing — welding, painting, assembly, and pick-and-place — operating with high precision and repeatability in structured, well-controlled environments.

    A growing category, collaborative robots ("cobots"), are specifically designed to work directly alongside humans without safety cages — using force limiting, rounded exteriors, and speed monitoring to make accidental contact safe rather than physically preventing all contact. This is a distinct engineering approach from traditional industrial arms, which are typically fenced off entirely because they aren't designed to detect or limit contact force.

    🚗 Mobile Robots & AGVs logistics

    Automated Guided Vehicles (AGVs) and Autonomous Mobile Robots (AMRs) move goods around warehouses, using the navigation and SLAM techniques covered earlier to operate without fixed tracks.

    🚁 Aerial Robotics (Drones) aerial

    Drones rely heavily on IMUs and GPS for stabilization, and increasingly on onboard cameras and SLAM for GPS-denied environments like indoor inspection.

    🚶 Humanoid & Legged Robots research

    Legged robots (Boston Dynamics' Spot and Atlas) must solve dynamic balance in real time — unlike wheeled robots, falling over is always one bad step away, making control far harder.

    🛡️ Safety & Ethics in Robotics

    ConcernWhy it matters
    Human-robot collision safetyForce/torque limits and emergency stops prevent injury near people
    Job displacementAutomation reshapes labor markets, requiring workforce transition planning
    Autonomous decision-makingWho is accountable when an autonomous system causes harm?

    Industrial robot safety isn't ad hoc — it's governed by formal standards like ISO 10218 (industrial robot safety) and ISO/TS 15066 (specifically for collaborative robots, defining exact force and pressure thresholds a cobot must stay under on human contact). Compliance with these standards, not just good intentions, is what actually lets a robot legally operate near people in most jurisdictions.
