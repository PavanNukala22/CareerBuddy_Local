    Module 01 · Foundations

    Foundations & Setup

    What VR actually simulates, where it sits among related technologies, and the toolchain you'll use to build immersive experiences throughout this course.

    🥽 What is Virtual Reality?

    Virtual Reality is a computer-generated simulation that replaces a user's sensory input — primarily sight and hearing, sometimes touch — with a synthetic 3D environment convincing enough that the brain responds to it as if it were real. Unlike a video game viewed on a monitor, VR surrounds the user: turning your head reveals more of the world, not the edge of a screen.

    👁️
    Immersion
    The objective degree to which a system replaces real sensory input with synthetic input.
    🧠
    Presence
    The subjective feeling of "being there" — a psychological, not technical, measure.
    🕹️
    Interactivity
    The environment responds in real time to the user's actions and viewpoint.
    🔁
    Real-time Rendering
    Frames must be generated fast enough (typically 90 Hz+) to avoid breaking the illusion.
    💡
    A common interview trap: immersion is technical, presence is psychological. A headset with a wide field of view and high resolution has high immersion — but a user can still feel "not there" if latency or tracking is off.

    📏 The Reality-Virtuality Continuum

    VR is one point on a spectrum first described by Milgram & Kishino in 1994, running from the fully physical world to a fully synthetic one.

    Figure 1.1 · Milgram's Reality-Virtuality Continuum
    TermWhat the user seesExample device
    AR (Augmented Reality)Real world with digital overlaysPhone camera + filters
    MR (Mixed Reality)Digital objects anchored to and occluded by real onesMeta Quest 3 passthrough, HoloLens
    VR (Virtual Reality)Fully synthetic environment, real world blocked outQuest, Valve Index, PSVR2

    🧰 Setting Up a Dev Environment

    Most professional VR content is built in a game engine rather than from scratch. This course uses Unity and Unreal Engine for native apps, plus WebXR for browser-based experiences that need no install.

    🎮
    Unity + XR Toolkit
    C#, huge asset store, gentle learning curve, strong Quest support.
    🎬
    Unreal Engine
    C++/Blueprints, best-in-class visuals, common in enterprise/training sims.
    🌐
    WebXR
    JavaScript API, runs directly in a browser tab — zero install for the user.
    ⚠️
    Standalone headsets like Quest run on Android internally. Unity builds target the Android platform with the XR Plug-in Management module enabled — a step first-timers frequently miss.

    🚀 Your First VR Scene

    Every Unity XR project starts the same way: install the XR Interaction Toolkit, add an XR Origin (camera rig that tracks the headset), and configure input actions.

    unity package manager// Window > Package Manager > install:
com.unity.xr.interaction.toolkit
com.unity.xr.management
com.unity.xr.oculus   // or openxr for cross-platform

    Once installed, right-click in the Hierarchy → XR → XR Origin (VR). This single prefab gives you a tracked head camera and two tracked hand controllers, ready to move as the player moves in the real world.

    💡
    Prefer OpenXR over vendor-specific SDKs (Oculus Integration, SteamVR) when possible — it's the industry-standard cross-platform API, so one build target reaches Quest, Index, and PSVR2 alike.
    Module 02 · Fundamentals

    Core VR Concepts

    The vocabulary and physiology behind every design decision in VR — why 90Hz matters, why some users get sick, and how headsets trick your eyes.

    🎯 Degrees of Freedom (DOF)

    Degrees of freedom describe how many independent ways an object (or a user's head) can move in space: three rotational axes plus three positional axes.

    Figure 2.1 · 3DOF (look around) vs 6DOF (walk around)
    TypeCan trackExample
    3DOFHead rotation only — where you're lookingGoogle Cardboard, early Gear VR
    6DOFRotation + physical translation — where you're standing/leaningQuest 3, Valve Index, PSVR2
    📝
    Nearly all modern consumer headsets are 6DOF for both the head and the hand controllers — that's what lets you physically duck behind cover or reach out and grab a virtual object at the correct distance.

    🔭 Field of View & Resolution

    Human peripheral vision spans roughly 200-220°; most headsets cover 90-120°, which is why users can perceive a soft-edged "goggle" boundary. Resolution is usually reported per eye, not combined.

    📐
    FOV
    Wider FOV increases immersion but demands more GPU fill-rate at the lens edges.
    🔲
    Screen-Door Effect
    Visible gaps between pixels at low PPD (pixels-per-degree); reduced by higher-res panels.
    🎯
    Foveated Rendering
    Render sharp only where the eye-tracked gaze lands, blur the periphery to save GPU cost.

    ⚡ Latency, Frame Rate & Motion-to-Photon

    Motion-to-photon latency is the time between a physical head movement and the corresponding pixel updating on-screen. Above roughly 20ms, users start to consciously or subconsciously notice the lag — and that mismatch is the single biggest cause of VR discomfort.

    Figure 2.2 · The motion-to-photon pipeline

    Two techniques keep perceived latency low even when the GPU can't hit full frame rate:

    • Asynchronous Timewarp (ATW): re-projects the last rendered frame using the newest head-pose data right before display.
    • Asynchronous Spacewarp (ASW): synthesizes an entirely new interpolated frame when the app can't sustain the target frame rate.
    ⚠️
    VR apps typically must hit 72-120 FPS depending on headset — far above the 30-60 FPS acceptable for flat-screen games. Dropped frames are felt physically, not just seen.

    🤢 Motion Sickness & Vergence-Accommodation Conflict

    VR sickness stems from a mismatch between what your eyes report and what your inner ear (vestibular system) feels — your eyes say you're moving, your body says you're standing still.

    👓
    The vergence-accommodation conflict is a separate, optical issue: your eyes converge (rotate inward) to focus on a nearby virtual object, but must accommodate (focus their lenses) to the fixed distance of the physical lens — a mismatch that causes eye strain over long sessions.

    Common mitigation techniques

    • Vignetting — narrowing peripheral vision during fast artificial locomotion
    • Teleportation locomotion — instant position swap instead of a smooth slide
    • Fixed horizon reference — a visible stable cockpit or floor grid
    • Snap turning — rotating in discrete steps rather than continuously

    🔊 Spatial Audio

    Sound in VR must be 3D-positional — quieter and phase-shifted appropriately as a virtual sound source moves relative to your tracked head — or the illusion breaks even with a perfect visual. Head-Related Transfer Functions (HRTFs) model how the shape of the ear filters sound from different directions.

    💡
    Spatial audio is one of the cheapest immersion upgrades available: it costs comparatively little GPU/CPU budget but strongly reinforces presence, especially for locating off-screen threats or NPCs.
    Module 03 · Hardware

    Hardware & Display Systems

    What's actually inside a headset, how it tracks your body in 3D space, and the trade-offs between the current generation of consumer devices.

    🔩 Anatomy of a Headset

    🖥️
    Displays
    One panel (or two) split into left/right eye views, each rendered from a slightly different virtual camera.
    🔍
    Lenses
    Fresnel or pancake lenses magnify a close panel to fill your field of view.
    📷
    Cameras/Sensors
    Outward-facing cameras for inside-out tracking and passthrough video.
    📟
    IMU
    Accelerometer + gyroscope sampled at 1000Hz+ for fast, low-latency rotational tracking.

    📺 Display Technologies

    Panel typeStrengthWeakness
    Fast-switch LCDCheap, bright, no burn-in riskLower contrast, weaker blacks
    OLEDTrue blacks, high contrastMore expensive, risk of burn-in
    Pancake lens + Mini-LEDMuch thinner headset form factorNarrower optical sweet spot
    📝
    "Pancake lenses" fold light back and forth between two lens elements instead of one long Fresnel lens — this is what let Quest 3 and PSVR2 become noticeably slimmer than the Quest 2 generation.

    📡 Tracking Systems

    🔦 Outside-in Tracking

    External base stations or cameras placed around the room observe the headset.

    • Very high precision
    • Requires room setup
    • e.g. SteamVR Lighthouse

    👁️ Inside-out Tracking

    Cameras on the headset itself watch the room and compute position via SLAM.

    • No external hardware, portable
    • Can lose tracking in low light/featureless rooms
    • e.g. Meta Quest, Apple Vision Pro

    🕹️ Controllers & Haptics

    Most 6DOF controllers combine an IMU, tracked LED or camera markers, capacitive touch sensors (to detect finger position without a button press), and a haptic actuator for vibration feedback.

    💡
    Newer headsets increasingly favor hand tracking (camera-based, controller-free finger tracking) for casual interactions, while retaining physical controllers for precision tasks like shooting or fast-paced gaming, where haptic feedback and button latency still matter.

    ⚖️ Headset Comparison

    Module 04

    Spatial Math & Tracking

    The 3D math that every VR engine runs under the hood — you don't need to derive it by hand, but you must recognize what it's doing.

    📐 Coordinate Systems & Handedness

    VR engines place the origin at a real-world reference point (often floor level at the center of your play space) and track everything else as an offset from it. Unity uses a left-handed Y-up coordinate system; Unreal uses left-handed Z-up; most math/graphics textbooks use right-handed — a frequent source of bugs when porting code between engines.

    Figure 4.1 · Unity's left-handed, Y-up world axes

    ➡️ Vectors & Transforms

    A tracked object's pose is represented every frame as a Transform: a position vector plus a rotation. Parenting objects to the XR camera rig lets child objects (like a held weapon) inherit the head or hand's position automatically.

    c# — unitypublic class HandFollow : MonoBehaviour {
    public Transform trackedHand;

    void Update() {
        // Directly copy the controller's tracked pose each frame
        transform.position = trackedHand.position;
        transform.rotation = trackedHand.rotation;
    }
}

    🌀 Quaternions vs Euler Angles

    Euler angles (pitch/yaw/roll) are intuitive but suffer from gimbal lock — a state where two rotational axes align and a degree of freedom is lost. VR engines track and interpolate head/hand rotation using quaternions, a 4-component representation that avoids gimbal lock and interpolates smoothly (via SLERP — spherical linear interpolation).

    📝
    You rarely construct quaternions by hand in Unity/Unreal — Quaternion.Slerp() and similar built-ins handle it — but recognizing why engines default to quaternions for head tracking is a common conceptual question.

    👀 Stereoscopic Rendering

    Depth perception in VR comes largely from rendering the scene twice per frame — once from a virtual camera at the left eye position, once at the right — offset by the user's interpupillary distance (IPD, ~63mm average). The brain fuses the two slightly different images into a sense of depth.

    ⚠️
    An incorrectly configured IPD (too wide or narrow) is a leading cause of eye strain and can make the whole scene feel like miniature "toy town" or oversized objects.

    Module 05

    Unity VR Development

    Building interactive, grabbable, walkable VR scenes with Unity's official XR Interaction Toolkit (XRI).

    🧩 XR Interaction Toolkit

    XRI provides ready-made components — XR Origin, XR Controller, XR Grab Interactable, XR Ray Interactor — that implement the standard interaction patterns most VR apps need, so you rarely write raw input-polling code.

    🎯
    Interactor
    Lives on the hand — detects and initiates interactions (a ray, or a direct grab volume).
    📦
    Interactable
    Lives on the object — defines how it responds to being hovered, selected, or activated.

    ✋ Grabbing & Interactable Objects

    Making any object grabbable is largely configuration, not code:

    unity setup1. Add a Rigidbody + Collider to the object
2. Add component: XR Grab Interactable
3. Set Movement Type: Kinematic (for solid objects)
4. Done — hand rays/direct touch now pick it up

    For custom behavior — say, a lever that only rotates on one axis — you hook into XRI's events instead of rewriting the grab logic:

    c# — unitypublic class LeverSound : MonoBehaviour {
    public AudioSource clickSound;

    public void OnSelectEntered() {
        // Wired to the Interactable's "Select Entered" UnityEvent
        clickSound.Play();
    }
}

    🚶 Teleportation Locomotion

    Because sliding movement can trigger motion sickness (Section 2.4), most Unity VR apps default to teleportation: the user points a curved ray at the floor, previews a landing reticle, and releases to instantly reposition.

    💡
    XRI ships a Teleportation Provider + Teleportation Area component pair. Tag your floor mesh as a Teleportation Area and the ray/reticle/fade transition is handled automatically.

    🖼️ UI in 3D Space

    Flat Canvas UI (as used in mobile/desktop games) must be switched to World Space render mode and interacted with via an XR UI Input Module so hand rays can click buttons floating in 3D, rather than relying on a mouse cursor that doesn't exist in VR.

    Module 06

    Unreal Engine VR

    Unreal's visual-scripting-first workflow and rendering pipeline, and where it diverges from Unity's approach.

    🎬 VR Template & Motion Controllers

    Unreal ships an official VR Template project with a VRPawn already configured for teleportation, grabbing, and haptics — the closest analog to Unity's XR Origin prefab.

    Unity conceptUnreal equivalent
    XR OriginVRPawn / VR Template
    XR Grab InteractableMotion Controller Component + Grab logic
    PrefabBlueprint Class
    MonoBehaviour scriptActor Component / Blueprint graph

    🧠 Blueprints vs C++

    Unreal's signature feature is Blueprints — a visual, node-based scripting layer that compiles down to the same engine calls as C++, letting designers iterate on gameplay logic without recompiling code.

    🧩 Blueprints

    • Visual node graph, fast iteration
    • Great for gameplay logic, UI, level scripting
    • Can get unwieldy for complex systems

    ⚙️ C++

    • Full engine access, best performance
    • Preferred for core systems, plugins, heavy math
    • Slower iteration (recompile required)
    📝
    A common production pattern: write performance-critical systems in C++, then expose them as Blueprint-callable functions so designers can wire up gameplay without touching code.

    💡 Nanite, Lumen & VR Performance

    Unreal 5's headline renderer features — Nanite (virtualized geometry) and Lumen (dynamic global illumination) — are, as of today, largely disabled or heavily restricted in VR because their deferred-rendering techniques conflict with the strict, low-latency forward-rendering path VR requires for stereo output at 90Hz+.

    ⚠️
    VR projects in Unreal almost always use the Forward Shading renderer with baked lighting instead of Lumen — a key architectural decision to make at project creation, since switching later is costly.

    Module 07

    WebXR & Browser VR

    Delivering immersive experiences through a URL — no app store, no install, running directly inside the browser that shipped with the headset.

    🌐 The WebXR Device API

    WebXR is a JavaScript API, supported by Chrome, Meta Quest Browser, and others, that grants a web page access to headset pose data and stereo rendering through the existing WebGL/WebGPU pipeline.

    🔗
    Zero install
    Share a link; the experience runs in the browser instantly.
    📉
    Lower ceiling
    Typically less performant than native apps for heavy 3D scenes.
    🔄
    Cross-platform
    One codebase can target VR, AR passthrough, and flat desktop fallback.

    💻 A Minimal WebXR Scene

    javascriptasync function startVR() {
  const session = await navigator.xr.requestSession('immersive-vr', {
    requiredFeatures: ['local-floor']
  });

  const glLayer = new XRWebGLLayer(session, gl);
  session.updateRenderState({ baseLayer: glLayer });

  const refSpace = await session.requestReferenceSpace('local-floor');
  session.requestAnimationFrame(onXRFrame);
}

    Every frame, WebXR provides a pose object containing left/right eye transform matrices — your render loop draws the scene twice, once per view, exactly like a native engine does internally.

    Module 08

    Interaction & Comfort Design

    Designing for a medium where a bad decision doesn't just look wrong — it can make the user physically ill.

    🚶‍♂️ Locomotion Techniques

    TechniqueComfortTrade-off
    TeleportationHighBreaks spatial continuity/immersion
    Smooth locomotion (joystick)Low-MediumMost natural feel, highest sickness risk
    Room-scale walkingHighestLimited by physical play space size
    Redirected walkingHighSubtly curves the user's real path; complex to implement

    🛋️ Comfort Design Patterns

    🌀
    Vignetting
    Darken peripheral vision during acceleration/turning to reduce vection.
    🧭
    Fixed reference frame
    A stable cockpit, nose, or grid anchors the user's sense of "self."
    ⏱️
    Snap turning
    Discrete rotation steps (e.g. 30°) instead of smooth continuous turning.

    🖥️ Diegetic vs Non-Diegetic UI

    Diegetic UI exists as an object inside the virtual world itself (a watch on your wrist, a screen on an in-world control panel). Non-diegetic UI floats independent of the world (a heads-up health bar). VR strongly favors diegetic UI because floating 2D overlays anchored to the camera can feel disorienting and break presence.

    ♿ Accessibility in VR

    • Seated mode — full functionality without requiring standing/room-scale space
    • One-handed control schemes — for users who can't operate two controllers
    • Subtitles & visual sound cues — for deaf/hard-of-hearing users
    • Adjustable comfort settings — locomotion type, vignette strength, turn style, exposed as user options rather than hard-coded
    ✅
    Ship comfort options as defaults on, adjustable rather than defaults off — the majority of new users are more sensitive to VR sickness than experienced developers testing the build.

    Module 09

    AR, MR & Spatial Computing

    Where VR headsets are heading next: blending digital content directly into the room you're standing in.

    📷 Passthrough & Mixed Reality

    Passthrough streams live video from a headset's outward cameras to its internal displays, letting the user see (a processed version of) the real room. Mixed Reality goes further, compositing virtual objects into that feed so they appear to occupy real space — behind your real couch, in front of your real wall.

    📝
    Passthrough is fundamentally different from a camera app: it must be re-projected and warped in real time to match your exact current eye position, or the world will appear to swim as you move your head.

    🗺️ SLAM & Scene Understanding

    SLAM (Simultaneous Localization and Mapping) is the algorithm family that lets a headset build a 3D map of an unknown room while simultaneously tracking its own position inside that map — the foundation of inside-out tracking and of any MR app that needs to know where your walls and furniture are.
