    HSE Fundamentals in Oil & Gas

    Start from zero: what Health, Safety and Environment means in oil and gas, the difference between a hazard and a risk, how controls are chosen, how HSE management systems work, the main hazards in the industry and the basic rules every worker follows.

    🦺 What is HSE in Oil & Gas?

    HSE Function

    risk assessmentsafety proceduresincident/emergency response
    Definition
    HSE (Health, Safety and Environment) is the discipline that protects people's health, prevents injuries and major accidents, and protects the environment wherever oil and gas is explored, produced, processed and transported.
    Why it matters
    Oil and gas work involves flammable hydrocarbons, toxic gases, high pressure, heavy lifting, heights, remote locations and long driving distances. Failures can kill people, destroy facilities, pollute land and sea, and end a company's licence to operate.
    Explanation
    HSE is everyone's job; the HSE department provides expertise, systems, monitoring and assurance. The core HSE cycle: identify hazards → assess risk → put controls and procedures in place → train people → monitor and audit → respond to incidents and emergencies → learn and improve.
    Types
    Occupational safety, process safety, occupational health and hygiene, environmental management, emergency response, security (sometimes HSSE).
    Components
    HSE policy, risk register, procedures (PTW, isolation), training and competence, inspections and audits, incident reporting and investigation, emergency response plans, KPIs.
    Process / workflow
    Plan (policy, risk assessment) → Do (procedures, training) → Check (inspections, KPIs, audits) → Act (corrective actions, improvement).
    O&G example
    Before a drilling rig moves to a new location, the HSE team runs a HAZID, checks the H2S contingency plan, the medevac route, the waste and spill plan, and contractor competence — and gives the work a go/no-go recommendation.
    Common mistakes
    Seeing HSE as “the HSE department's job”; paperwork without field presence; counting only injuries.
    Best practices
    Visible field presence; simple, usable procedures; involve workers in risk assessment; learn from near misses.
    🧪 Practice activity: Whose job?
    A contractor sees a missing guardrail. Is it their job to report it, or only the HSE officer's?
    Answer: Everyone's. Anyone can and should stop work and report a hazard — Stop Work Authority applies to all.
    EXAMPLE · One permit, many roles

    A hot work job: the HSE Engineer sets the gas-testing standard; the Planner schedules the job away from a lift; the Supervisor runs the toolbox talk; the Technician tests gas; the Inspector checks the fire watch and extinguisher; the Analyst later tracks permit compliance. All ten HSE roles support safe work.

    🧩 Health, Safety & Environment Explained

    🩺 Health

    Prevent illness from work: H2S, benzene, noise, heat, fatigue, ergonomics, NORM, fitness to work, medical emergency.

    ⚠️ Safety

    Prevent injuries and major accidents: occupational safety (falls, lifting, driving) and process safety (fires, explosions, toxic releases).

    🌿 Environment

    Prevent pollution and protect nature: emissions, flaring, spills, produced water, waste, biodiversity, permits.

    🔗 How they connect

    One event can hit all three: a gas release can injure workers (safety), expose them to H2S (health) and emit methane (environment).

    ⚖️ Hazard, Risk & the Hierarchy of Controls

    Figure 1.1 · Hierarchy of controls — most effective at the top

    Hazard, Risk & Controls

    risk assessmentsafety
    Definition
    A hazard is anything with the potential to cause harm (e.g. H2S, a suspended load). Risk is the combination of how likely harm is and how severe it would be.
    Why it matters
    You can't remove every hazard, but you can reduce risk. Confusing the two leads to weak controls.
    Explanation
    Risk = Likelihood × Severity. Controls are chosen from the top of the hierarchy first; PPE is used when other controls can't reduce risk enough, never as the only control for serious hazards.
    Types
    Physical, chemical, biological, ergonomic, psychosocial, process, environmental hazards.
    Components
    Hazard identification, risk rating, controls, residual risk, review.
    Process / workflow
    Identify hazard → who could be harmed and how → rate risk → apply controls (hierarchy) → rate residual risk → monitor.
    O&G example
    Manual handling of 50 kg drilling chemical sacks: elimination — bulk delivery; substitution — 25 kg sacks; engineering — vacuum lifter; administrative — two-person lift rule; PPE — gloves.
    Common mistakes
    Starting with PPE; listing hazards without rating risk; never reviewing.
    Best practices
    Work top-down through the hierarchy; involve the people doing the job.
    🧪 Practice activity: Hazard or risk?
    Classify: (a) a 3 m deep open excavation; (b) “there is a medium chance someone falls into it and is seriously injured”.
    Answer: (a) hazard; (b) risk statement (likelihood + severity).

    🔄 HSE Management Systems

    HSE Management System (HSE-MS)

    documentationsafety proceduresreporting
    Definition
    A structured framework of policies, processes and responsibilities to manage HSE risks and improve performance continuously.
    Why it matters
    It turns good intentions into consistent practice across sites, contractors and shifts, and is expected by regulators and clients.
    Explanation
    Common standards: ISO 45001 (occupational health and safety), ISO 14001 (environment), IOGP Report 510 (operating management system framework for oil and gas). All follow Plan–Do–Check–Act: leadership and policy → planning (risks, legal requirements, objectives) → support (competence, communication, documents) → operation (procedures, contractors, emergency preparedness) → performance evaluation (monitoring, audit, management review) → improvement.
    Types
    Company HSE-MS, contractor HSE plans, project HSE plans, safety cases (offshore regimes).
    Components
    Policy, risk register, legal register, procedures, training matrix, inspection/audit programme, incident system, KPIs, management review.
    Process / workflow
    PDCA cycle, reviewed at least annually by management.
    O&G example
    A contractor bidding for well-services work must show a bridging document linking its HSE-MS to the operator's, covering permits, emergency response and incident reporting.
    Common mistakes
    Systems on paper only; procedures nobody uses; no management review.
    Best practices
    Short, practical procedures; audit what actually happens; leaders review results.
    🧪 Practice activity: PDCA
    Which PDCA stage is an internal audit?
    Answer: Check.

    🛑 Basic Safety Rules

    Life-Saving Rules & Stop Work Authority

    safety proceduressafety
    Definition
    Simple, non-negotiable rules for the activities most likely to cause fatalities. The IOGP Life-Saving Rules cover: bypassing safety controls, confined space, driving, energy isolation, hot work, line of fire, safe mechanical lifting, work authorisation, working at height.
    Why it matters
    Analysis of industry fatalities shows the same activities recur; clear rules prevent the most serious outcomes.
    Explanation
    Each rule describes what every worker must do (e.g. “I verify isolation and zero energy before work begins”). Stop Work Authority means anyone can stop a job they believe is unsafe, without blame.
    Types
    Company “golden rules”, IOGP Life-Saving Rules, site-specific rules.
    Components
    Rule cards, training, toolbox talks, supervisor verification, consequence management.
    Process / workflow
    Brief rules → verify at worksite → stop and correct deviations → report and learn.
    O&G example
    A rigger notices a person walking under a suspended load. They stop the lift immediately (line of fire rule) — the supervisor thanks them and reinforces the exclusion zone.
    Common mistakes
    Punishing people who stop work; rules not explained; inconsistent enforcement.
    Best practices
    Recognise good stops; leaders model the rules; consistent consequences for deliberate violations.
    🧪 Practice activity: Which rule?
    A worker removes a guard “just for a minute” to clear a jam on a running conveyor. Which rules apply?
    Answer: Bypassing safety controls and energy isolation — stop the machine, isolate and verify before clearing.

    Risk Assessment in Practice

    The core HSE skill: identify hazards, rate risk, choose controls, demonstrate ALARP, use bow-ties, assess risk dynamically at the worksite and review risk when things change.

    🔎 Hazard Identification Methods

    Hazard Identification (HAZID)

    risk assessmentinspectionchecklists
    Definition
    Systematic methods to find what could cause harm in a facility, project or task.
    Why it matters
    A hazard that isn't identified can't be controlled. Many incident investigations find the hazard was never recognised.
    Explanation
    • HAZID workshop: team review using guidewords (fire, toxic, dropped objects, weather, security…) early in projects.
    • HAZOP: detailed process deviation study on P&IDs (led with process engineers).
    • JSA/JHA: step-by-step task analysis.
    • Workplace inspections and observation cards.
    • Incident and near-miss data.
    • Environmental aspects register (ISO 14001).
    Types
    Facility-level (HAZID, HAZOP), task-level (JSA), routine (inspections), reactive (incident learning).
    Components
    Team with field experience, guidewords/checklists, drawings and procedures, risk register.
    Process / workflow
    Prepare scope → team session → record hazards, causes, consequences, existing controls → rate → actions → register.
    O&G example
    A HAZID for a new well pad identifies H2S from a nearby sour well, flood risk in the wet season, and a school 1.5 km away — leading to wind-sock placement, raised pad design and a community emergency plan.
    Common mistakes
    Office-only HAZIDs; no field workers involved; registers never updated.
    Best practices
    Mix of experience; site visit; living risk register.
    🧪 Practice activity: Match the method
    Which method fits: (a) new pipeline route at concept stage, (b) changing a pump seal tomorrow, (c) detailed review of a separator P&ID?
    Answer: (a) HAZID; (b) JSA; (c) HAZOP.

    Risk Rating

    risk assessmentcalculations
    Definition
    Assigning a level of risk using likelihood and severity on an agreed matrix (example 5×5 above — each company defines its own).
    Why it matters
    Ratings decide who can approve work, which controls are required and what is prioritised.
    Explanation
    Severity is usually rated for people, environment, asset and reputation — use the worst credible outcome. Likelihood considers existing controls. Rate inherent (before controls) and residual (after) risk. Typical actions: Low — proceed; Medium — controls and supervisor approval; High — senior approval and extra controls; Very High — do not proceed.
    Types
    Qualitative matrices, semi-quantitative (LOPA), quantitative risk assessment (QRA).
    Components
    Severity definitions, likelihood definitions, risk bands, approval levels.
    Process / workflow
    Worst credible outcome → likelihood with controls → score → band → actions/approval → residual risk.
    O&G example
    Lifting a 12 t module over a live process area: severity 5 (fatality/major release), likelihood 2 with lift plan → 10 High → requires senior approval, shut-in of lines underneath or SIMOPS controls.
    Common mistakes
    Scoring to get a “green” result; using best-case severity; ignoring environmental severity.
    Best practices
    Calibrate the team with examples; challenge low scores; record assumptions.
    🧪 Practice activity: Rate it
    Working at 15 m on a scaffold: severity 5, likelihood 2 with guardrails and harness. Score and band?
    Answer: 10 — High (per example matrix): requires senior approval and verified controls (scaffold inspection tag, rescue plan).

    📝 Job Safety Analysis (JSA)

    Job Safety Analysis

    risk assessmentsafety proceduresteam supervision
    Definition
    A JSA breaks a task into steps, identifies hazards for each step and defines controls and responsibilities.
    Why it matters
    Most injuries happen during specific task steps; a good JSA prepares the crew for exactly those moments.
    Explanation
    Columns: step → hazards → controls → responsible person. Written with the crew, reviewed at the worksite, attached to the permit. Updated if conditions change.
    Types
    Generic JSA (template), task-specific JSA, critical task analysis.
    Components
    Task steps, hazards, controls, PPE, sign-off by the crew.
    Process / workflow
    Select job → list steps → hazards → controls → discuss at toolbox talk → sign → review after the job.
    O&G example
    JSA for changing a valve on a gas line: steps include isolation (stored pressure), depressuring (venting), breaking flange (residual gas, H2S, line of fire), lifting valve (manual handling, dropped object), reinstalling, leak test (pressure).
    Common mistakes
    Copy-paste JSAs; written by someone not doing the job; signed without reading.
    Best practices
    Crew writes it; specific to today's conditions; supervisor challenges it.
    🧪 Practice activity: Write two lines
    Write JSA lines for “break flange on depressured gas line”.
    Model: Step: break flange — Hazards: trapped pressure, gas/H2S release, line of fire — Controls: confirm zero pressure on gauge and vent, gas test, BA available, break far side bolts first and stand clear — Responsible: technician, verified by supervisor.

    🎀 ALARP & Bow-Tie

    Figure 2.1 · Bow-tie: threats → prevention barriers → top event → mitigation barriers → consequences

    ALARP & Barrier Thinking

    risk assessmentadvanced technical analysis
    Definition
    ALARP (As Low As Reasonably Practicable) means reducing risk until the cost or effort of further reduction is grossly disproportionate to the benefit. A bow-tie shows threats, prevention barriers, the top event, mitigation barriers and consequences.
    Why it matters
    ALARP is a legal concept in many regimes (e.g. UK). Bow-ties make barriers visible so they can be owned, maintained and audited.
    Explanation
    Risk is “tolerable” only if ALARP is demonstrated — options considered, good practice applied, reasons for rejected options recorded. Each barrier in a bow-tie has an owner, performance standard and assurance activity; escalation factors (e.g. barrier impaired by maintenance backlog) are shown too.
    Types
    Hardware barriers (relief valves, gas detectors) and human/operational barriers (procedures, operator response).
    Components
    Hazard, top event, threats, consequences, barriers, escalation factors, barrier owners.
    Process / workflow
    Select major hazard → build bow-tie in workshop → assign barrier owners → monitor barrier health.
    O&G example
    A bow-tie for an offshore gas release shows gas detection as a key mitigation barrier; finding 15% of detectors overdue for calibration is reported as a barrier impairment and prioritised.
    Common mistakes
    Bow-ties drawn once and filed; counting procedures as strong barriers; no owners.
    Best practices
    Use bow-ties for briefings and barrier dashboards; review after incidents.
    🧪 Practice activity: Prevention or mitigation?
    Classify for a hydrocarbon release: (a) corrosion inspection, (b) deluge system, (c) relief valve, (d) emergency response team.
    Answer: (a) prevention; (c) prevention (stops overpressure rupture); (b) and (d) mitigation.

    👀 Dynamic Risk Assessment

    Point-of-Work Risk Assessment

    risk assessmentsafetycommunication
    Definition
    A quick, continuous check at the worksite of whether conditions have changed from those assumed in the JSA/permit (also called last-minute risk assessment, “Take 5”).
    Why it matters
    Weather, people, equipment and nearby activities change during the day; the plan written in the morning may no longer be valid.
    Explanation
    Typical steps: Stop → Look (what's changed?) → Assess → Control → Proceed (or Stop Work and re-plan). Triggers: new crew members, wind change, new activity nearby, unexpected finding, alarm.
    Types
    Take 5 / STAR cards, pre-task checklists, re-assessment after breaks.
    Components
    Pocket cards, simple checklists, Stop Work culture.
    Process / workflow
    Before start and whenever something changes.
    O&G example
    During a lift the wind picks up to near the crane's limit. The banksman stops the lift, checks the anemometer and lift plan limits, and waits — conditions had changed from the plan.
    Common mistakes
    Ticking cards without looking; continuing because “it's nearly done”.
    Best practices
    Supervisors ask “what changed?” during field visits; praise stops.
    🧪 Practice activity: Change triggers
    List four changes that should trigger re-assessment.
    Answer: Weather (wind, lightning), new people on the crew, a new activity nearby (SIMOPS), unexpected condition (corrosion, extra pressure), gas alarm, fatigue/long shift.

    🔁 Risk in Management of Change

    • Any change to equipment, chemicals, procedures, organisation or people (e.g. reduced crew) can introduce new hazards.
    • MOC requires an HSE risk review before implementation — including temporary changes, with an expiry date.
    • Organisational change (e.g. removing a safety role, extending shifts) also needs risk assessment.
    EXAMPLE · Flixborough

    Flixborough (UK, 1974, 28 killed): a temporary bypass pipe was installed between two reactors without a proper engineering or risk review. It failed, releasing cyclohexane that exploded. It is the classic case for formal MOC.

    The procedures that keep hazardous work under control: permits, isolation, gas testing and confined space, hot work, heights and lifting, simultaneous operations, contractors, PPE and field inspections.

    📋 Permit to Work

    Permit to Work (PTW) System

    safety proceduresdocumentationcommunication
    Definition
    A formal written system that controls non-routine and hazardous work by defining the job, hazards, precautions, authorisations and hand-back.
    Why it matters
    Piper Alpha (North Sea, 1988, 167 killed): a pump whose relief valve had been removed under a permit was restarted by the next shift, which did not know. PTW and handover failures were key causes.
    Explanation
    Roles: permit issuer/area authority (controls the area), performing authority (does the work), isolating authority, gas tester. Permits are linked to JSAs and isolation certificates, cross-referenced when they affect the same equipment, displayed at the worksite, suspended at shift change and handed back when complete.
    Types
    Hot work, cold work, confined space entry, electrical, excavation, radiography, diving, working at height, line break.
    Components
    Permit form, JSA, isolation certificate, gas test record, permit log/board, signatures, hand-back.
    Process / workflow
    Request → plan and JSA → isolate and prepare → gas test → issue at worksite → toolbox talk → work → monitor → suspend/close → hand back.
    O&G example
    A cold work permit to replace a valve is cross-referenced with the isolation certificate and a nearby hot work permit; the area authority suspends the hot work while the line is opened.
    Common mistakes
    Permits issued from the office; permits for “whole day, whole area”; no handover of open permits.
    Best practices
    Site visit before issue; specific scope; permit status in every shift handover; permit audits.
    🧪 Practice activity: Piper Alpha
    Name two PTW controls that would have prevented the Piper Alpha restart.
    Answer: Cross-referencing the PSV-removal permit with the pump (locking it out), and a robust shift handover of open/suspended permits.

    🔐 Energy Isolation & LOTO

    Energy Isolation

    safety proceduressafetyequipment/process operation
    Definition
    Separating equipment from all energy sources — process fluids, pressure, electricity, mechanical, thermal, chemical, gravity — and securing that separation with locks and tags.
    Why it matters
    “Energy isolation” is an IOGP Life-Saving Rule because unexpected release of energy is a leading cause of serious injuries.
    Explanation
    Isolation standards: positive isolation (blinds, removed spools) for confined space and high hazard; double block and bleed for many hydrocarbon jobs; single valve only for low risk. Electrical: isolate at switchgear, lock, tag, prove dead, “try” start. Each worker applies a personal lock (or uses a group lock box). Verify zero energy before work.
    Types
    Mechanical/process, electrical, instrument, multi-energy isolations.
    Components
    Isolation certificate, LOTO devices, blind list, verification records.
    Process / workflow
    Identify energy sources from drawings → isolate → lock/tag → depressure/drain → verify zero energy → work → remove locks in order.
    O&G example
    A pump motor is locked off at the MCC but the technician's “try start” at the local station still turns the motor — a wrong breaker was locked. Verification prevented a serious injury.
    Common mistakes
    Not verifying zero energy; sharing one lock for the whole crew; missing small-bore connections.
    Best practices
    Personal locks; verification step mandatory; isolation audits.
    🧪 Practice activity: Energy sources
    List the energy sources for a hydraulic-actuated valve on a gas line.
    Answer: Process gas pressure, hydraulic pressure in the actuator, electrical power to the solenoid/HPU, stored spring energy, gravity (heavy parts).

    🧪 Gas Testing & Confined Space Entry

    Gas Testing & Confined Space

    safety proceduresmeasurementsincident/emergency response
    Definition
    Gas testing measures oxygen, flammable gas and toxic gases before and during work. A confined space is an enclosed space with limited entry/exit where hazardous atmospheres can occur (vessels, tanks, pits).
    Why it matters
    Confined spaces kill entrants and, very often, the people who try to rescue them without breathing apparatus.
    Explanation
    Test sequence: oxygen first (typical acceptable 19.5–23.5%), then flammables (%LEL — hot work usually requires 0% LEL; many companies allow cold work below a low % LEL), then toxics (H2S, CO, benzene). Test at several levels (gases stratify) and continuously during entry. Entry needs positive isolation, ventilation, a trained standby person, communication and a rescue plan with equipment.
    Types
    Pre-entry tests, periodic re-tests, continuous personal monitoring.
    Components
    Calibrated, bump-tested gas detectors, entry permit, attendant log, rescue equipment (tripod, BA).
    Process / workflow
    Isolate → clean/ventilate → test → issue entry permit → standby in place → monitor → exit and close.
    O&G example
    Gas test at the manway shows 20.9% O2, 0% LEL; at the vessel bottom 17% O2 — nitrogen had pooled. Entry is stopped and ventilation extended.
    Common mistakes
    Testing only at the opening; detector not bump-tested; standby person entering to rescue.
    Best practices
    Test at all levels; continuous monitoring; drills of the rescue plan.
    🧪 Practice activity: Test order
    Why is oxygen tested before flammable gas?
    Answer: Many catalytic LEL sensors need oxygen to read correctly; low oxygen can give false low LEL readings — and oxygen deficiency itself is lethal.

    🤝 SIMOPS & Contractor Management

    Simultaneous Operations & Contractors

    cross-team coordinationsafety procedurescoordination
    Definition
    SIMOPS are activities that happen at the same time and place and may interact (e.g. drilling, production, construction, lifting). Contractor management covers selecting, inducting, supervising and reviewing contractors' HSE performance.
    Why it matters
    Interactions between activities create hazards that no single permit sees; contractors perform much of the high-risk work in oil and gas.
    Explanation
    SIMOPS matrix defines which activities can run together, with which controls. Contractor lifecycle: pre-qualification → HSE plan and bridging document → induction and competence check → supervision and inspections → performance review.
    Types
    Planned SIMOPS (matrix), ad-hoc conflicts (resolved by area authority); contractor modes (integrated, independent).
    Components
    SIMOPS matrix, daily coordination meeting, contractor HSE plans, KPIs.
    Process / workflow
    Plan activities → check matrix → agree controls → communicate → monitor.
    O&G example
    Wireline operations on one well and a crane lift above an adjacent wellhead are planned for the same morning. The SIMOPS review moves the lift to the afternoon.
    Common mistakes
    No daily SIMOPS review; contractors working to different rules.
    Best practices
    Daily permit/SIMOPS meeting; one set of site rules for all.
    🧪 Practice activity: SIMOPS conflict
    Why can't hot work run next to a line being opened for maintenance?
    Answer: The line break may release hydrocarbons while hot work provides an ignition source.

    ✅ HSE Inspections & Observations

    Workplace Inspections

    inspectionchecklistsmeasurementsreporting
    Definition
    Planned checks of worksites, equipment and practices against standards, plus behavioural observations of how people work.
    Why it matters
    They find unsafe conditions and acts before they cause incidents, and show leaders what really happens in the field.
    Explanation
    The HSE Inspector uses checklists (permits, housekeeping, scaffolds, lifting gear, fire equipment, gas detectors), takes measurements (noise, gas, lighting), and reports findings with photos and risk ranking. Observations focus on behaviour and coaching, not blame.
    Types
    Planned inspections, permit audits, equipment inspections (fire extinguishers, eyewash), behavioural observations, leadership site visits.
    Components

    Common mistakes
    Inspections as tick-box; findings not closed; blame-focused observations.
    Best practices
    Close findings with evidence; trend repeat findings; coach on the spot.

    Occupational Health & Environmental Protection

    The “H” and “E” of HSE: toxic gas, chemical exposure and industrial hygiene, noise, heat and fatigue, environmental management, emissions and waste, and spill response.

    ☠️ Hydrogen Sulfide (H₂S)

    H₂S Hazard Management

    incident/emergency responsesafety proceduresmeasurements
    Definition
    H2S is a colourless, flammable, highly toxic gas found in sour oil and gas, produced water, sewers and tanks. It is heavier than air.
    Why it matters
    At low levels it smells of rotten eggs; at higher levels it paralyses the sense of smell (olfactory fatigue), and at high concentrations it causes rapid collapse and death.
    Explanation
    Typical reference values (check local regulations): ACGIH TLV 1 ppm (8-h TWA), 5 ppm STEL; NIOSH IDLH 50 ppm; olfactory paralysis around 100–150 ppm; rapid unconsciousness at several hundred ppm. Many sites set personal monitor alarms at about 10 ppm (low) and 15–20 ppm (high). Controls: fixed and personal detection, wind socks, briefings on muster/escape routes (upwind/crosswind), SCBA for entry into sour areas, buddy system, H2S training (e.g. OPITO/approved courses).
    Types
    Sour wells, sour gas plants, sour water systems, confined spaces with decaying organic matter.
    Components
    Fixed detectors, personal monitors, wind indicators, BA sets, H2S contingency plan, rescue team.
    Process / workflow
    Alarm → stop work → hold breath/don escape set if issued → move upwind/crosswind to muster → account for people → trained team with BA responds.
    O&G example
    A worker collapses near a sour water tank. His colleague, without BA, runs in to help and also collapses. The correct action: raise alarm, stay upwind, rescue only with BA — a common pattern in H2S fatalities.
    Common mistakes
    Relying on smell; unprotected rescue; monitors not bump-tested.
    Best practices
    Daily bump tests; wind awareness; regular drills.
    🧪 Practice activity: Escape direction
    The windsock shows wind blowing from north to south and the leak is north of you. Which way do you move?
    Answer: Crosswind (east or west) away from the plume, then to the upwind muster point — never downwind (south).

    🧴 Chemical Exposure & Industrial Hygiene

    Industrial Hygiene

    risk assessmentmeasurementsdata analysis
    Definition
    Anticipating, recognising, evaluating and controlling workplace health hazards — chemicals, dusts, noise, radiation, biological agents.
    Why it matters
    Health effects (cancer from benzene, hearing loss, lung disease) can appear years later, so prevention depends on measurement and control now.
    Explanation
    Use Safety Data Sheets (SDS) and a chemical register; assess exposure against OELs (TWA, STEL, ceiling). Key oil and gas agents: benzene (carcinogen, very low limits), H2S, mercury, NORM (naturally occurring radioactive scale in production equipment), drilling-fluid chemicals, welding fumes, asbestos in older facilities. Monitoring: personal sampling pumps, badges, direct-reading instruments.
    Types
    Inhalation, skin contact, ingestion; acute vs chronic exposure.
    Components
    Chemical register, SDS, exposure assessments, monitoring results, health surveillance.
    Process / workflow
    Inventory chemicals → assess exposure groups → monitor → compare with OELs → control (hierarchy) → health surveillance.
    O&G example
    Personal sampling during tank gauging shows benzene exposures above the company limit. Controls: closed gauging systems, remote level measurement, and respirators until engineering changes are in place.
    Common mistakes
    No exposure data; using SDS only for spills; ignoring NORM.
    Best practices
    Exposure monitoring plan; closed systems; NORM surveys before opening equipment.
    🧪 Practice activity: TWA
    A worker is exposed to 2 ppm for 4 hours and 0 ppm for the other 4 hours. 8-hour TWA?
    Answer: (2 × 4 + 0 × 4) ÷ 8 = 1 ppm.

    🛢️ Spill Prevention & Response

    Oil Spill Preparedness & Response

    incident/emergency responseplanningcommunication
    Definition
    Preventing oil and chemical spills, and responding effectively when they occur, using a tiered response.
    Why it matters
    Deepwater Horizon (Gulf of Mexico, 2010): a well blowout killed 11 people and caused one of the largest marine oil spills in history — showing how safety and environmental outcomes are linked.
    Explanation
    Tiered response (IPIECA/IOGP): Tier 1 — small, local, handled with on-site resources; Tier 2 — larger, regional resources; Tier 3 — major, national/international resources. Prevention: integrity, secondary containment (bunds), drip trays, transfer procedures, hose inspections. Response: stop source, contain (booms, absorbents), recover, protect sensitive areas, report to authorities.
    Types
    Land spills, marine spills, chemical spills.
    Components
    Oil spill contingency plan, spill kits, trained responders, sensitivity maps, reporting procedures.
    Process / workflow
    Detect → stop source safely → contain → notify → recover/clean up → report → investigate.
    O&G example
    A diesel transfer hose bursts during refuelling; 200 L reach the deck. The crew stops the pump, uses the spill kit, plugs scuppers to protect the sea and reports a Tier 1 spill.
    Common mistakes
    Spill kits empty or missing; transfers unattended; late reporting.
    Best practices
    Attended transfers; inspected hoses; regular spill drills.
    🧪 Practice activity: Which tier?
    A crude spill offshore needs national response vessels and international equipment. Which tier?
    Answer: Tier 3.

    Incident Management, Investigation & HSE Performance

    How incidents are reported, classified and investigated; how root causes and CAPA prevent recurrence; and how leading and lagging KPIs and trend analysis measure HSE performance.

    🚩 Incident Reporting & Classification

    Incident Reporting

    incident/emergency responsereportingdocumentation
    Definition
    Recording every unplanned event that caused, or could have caused, harm to people, environment, assets or reputation — and classifying it by actual and potential severity.
    Why it matters
    Near misses and small incidents share causes with serious ones. A strong reporting culture gives early warning.
    Explanation
    Typical injury classes: first aid case (FAC), medical treatment case (MTC), restricted work case (RWC), lost time injury (LTI), fatality. Process safety events: Tier 1 and Tier 2 (API RP 754 / IOGP 456). Environmental: spills by volume, permit breaches. Every event is also rated for potential severity — a dropped 5 kg tool that missed a person is a high-potential incident (HiPo).
    Types
    Unsafe act/condition reports, near misses, injuries, illnesses, process safety events, environmental incidents, property damage, security events.
    Components
    Reporting form/app, classification rules, notification matrix (who to tell, how fast), incident database.
    Process / workflow
    Make safe and give first aid → report immediately → classify → notify (management, regulator if required) → investigate (level by potential).
    O&G example
    A 2 m scaffold coupler falls 20 m and lands in an empty walkway. Actual harm: none. Potential: fatality → classified HiPo, full investigation.
    Common mistakes
    Classifying by actual outcome only; discouraging reports to protect statistics; late regulatory notification.
    Best practices
    Easy reporting; thank reporters; investigate by potential severity.
    🧪 Practice activity: Classify
    A worker cuts a hand, gets stitches and returns to normal work next shift. Classification?
    Answer: Medical treatment case (MTC) — stitches are beyond first aid, but no lost or restricted time.

    🔍 Incident Investigation

    Incident Investigation

    incident/emergency responseadvanced technical analysisleadership
    Definition
    A structured process to establish what happened, why it happened, and what must change to prevent recurrence — not to blame individuals.
    Why it matters
    Investigations that stop at “human error” leave the system weaknesses in place.
    Explanation
    Steps: secure scene and preserve evidence → form team (independent lead for serious events) → collect evidence (people, positions, parts, paper — the “4 Ps”) → build timeline → analyse causes (immediate, underlying/system, root) → recommendations → report and share. Methods: 5 Whys, fishbone, ICAM, TapRooT, barrier/bow-tie analysis, fault tree.
    Types
    Level 1 (local, low potential), Level 2 (supervisor/HSE), Level 3 (formal team, HiPo and serious events).
    Components
    Investigation procedure, team charter, interview guides, evidence log, timeline, report template.
    Process / workflow
    As above; report within agreed timeframes (e.g. 30 days for serious events).
    O&G example
    A gas release during valve maintenance: immediate cause — flange broken under pressure; underlying — isolation verification not done, gauge was faulty; root/system — isolation procedure didn't require two independent verifications, supervisor workload too high.
    Common mistakes
    Blame culture; jumping to conclusions; recommendations like “be careful”.
    Best practices
    Just culture; evidence-based; SMART recommendations tied to causes.
    🧪 Practice activity: 4 Ps
    What are the “4 Ps” of evidence?
    Answer: People (witnesses), Positions (scene, locations), Parts (equipment, failed components), Paper (permits, procedures, records, data).

    🧠 Root Cause Analysis & CAPA

    RCA & Corrective/Preventive Actions

    RCACAPAquality plans
    Definition
    RCA identifies the fundamental system causes of an incident; CAPA defines corrective actions (fix this) and preventive actions (stop it happening anywhere).
    Why it matters
    Without effective CAPA, the same incident happens again — often on another site.
    Explanation
    Good actions target root causes and sit high on the hierarchy of controls (engineering changes beat retraining). Actions have owners, due dates and effectiveness checks. The HSE Quality Engineer manages the CAPA register and verifies closure evidence.
    Types
    Immediate (containment), corrective, preventive, systemic (procedure, design, organisation).
    Components
    RCA method, action register, owners, due dates, verification, KPIs (overdue actions).
    Process / workflow
    Causes → actions → approve → implement → verify effectiveness → close → share.
    O&G example
    5 Whys on a hand injury: caught in pipe-handling equipment → hand in pinch point → no guard → guard removed months ago and not replaced → no inspection of guards in PM routine. CAPA: refit guard (corrective); add guard checks to PM and inspection checklists across the fleet (preventive).
    Common mistakes
    “Retrain the worker” as the only action; closing without evidence.
    Best practices
    Hierarchy of controls for actions; effectiveness check after 3–6 months.
    🧪 Practice activity: Corrective or preventive?
    After a scaffold collapse, (a) rebuilding the scaffold correctly, (b) introducing scaffold inspection tags site-wide.
    Answer: (a) corrective; (b) preventive.

    📊 Trend Analysis & Dashboards

    HSE Data Analysis

    data analysisKPI trackingExcel/Power BItrend analysis
    Definition
    Turning incident, inspection, observation and monitoring data into trends and insights that drive action.
    Why it matters
    Patterns (e.g. hand injuries on night shift, dropped objects on one rig) are invisible in single reports.
    Explanation
    The HSE Analyst cleans data, normalises by exposure hours, uses Pareto charts (which categories cause most events), trend lines and control charts, and builds Excel/Power BI dashboards by site, contractor, activity and severity — each with action owners.
    Types
    Monthly KPI reports, Pareto analysis, heat maps, contractor scorecards.
    Components
    Incident database, hours data, classification codes, dashboard.
    Process / workflow
    Collect → clean → normalise → analyse → visualise → recommend → track.
    O&G example
    Pareto of 180 incidents shows 42% are hand and finger injuries, mostly during pipe handling. The focus campaign introduces hands-free tools; hand injuries fall by half in six months.
    Common mistakes
    Counting without normalising; charts without actions; ignoring data quality.
    Best practices
    One question per dashboard; owners for every action.
    🧪 Practice activity: Normalise
    Site A: 4 recordables in 400,000 h; Site B: 6 in 1,200,000 h. Which performs better (×200,000)?
    Answer: A = 2.0; B = 1.0 → Site B performs better despite more cases.

    After studying Deepwater Horizon (2010), a drilling contractor strengthens well-control drills, independent review of negative pressure tests and stop-work culture on its own rigs.

    🚨 Emergency Response Planning

    Emergency Response Plan (ERP)

    incident/emergency responseplanningcommunication
    Definition
    A documented plan defining how a site responds to credible emergencies: organisation, roles, alarms, muster, communication, resources and recovery.
    Why it matters
    In an emergency there is no time to improvise; the first minutes decide outcomes.
    Explanation
    Based on credible scenarios from risk assessment. Uses a tiered organisation: on-scene command (site incident commander, emergency teams) → emergency management team (support, logistics, regulators, families) → crisis management team (corporate, media, reputation). Many companies use the Incident Command System (ICS). Includes medevac, mutual aid, community notification, business recovery.
    Types
    Site ERP, H2S contingency plan, oil spill contingency plan, medical emergency plan, security plan.
    Components
    Scenarios, roles and call trees, alarm types, muster points and headcount, equipment, communication plan, contact lists.
    Process / workflow
    Identify scenarios → plan responses → resource → train → drill → review after drills and real events.
    O&G example
    A remote onshore site's ERP includes the nearest trauma hospital (3 h by road), a helicopter medevac contract with landing site, and satellite phones because mobile coverage is poor.
    Common mistakes
    Generic plans; outdated contact lists; plans never tested.
    Best practices
    Scenario-specific actions; monthly contact checks; realistic drills.
    🧪 Practice activity: Three tiers
    Who handles media statements in a major incident — on-scene, emergency management or crisis management team?
    Answer: Crisis management team (corporate level), with input from the others.

    🎯 Drills, Exercises & Competence

    Emergency Drills & Exercises

    incident/emergency responseschedulingprogress tracking
    Definition
    Planned practice of emergency responses — from short drills (muster, H2S) to full-scale exercises involving external services.
    Why it matters
    Drills turn plans into reflexes and reveal gaps before a real emergency does.
    Explanation
    Types: tabletop (discussion), functional (one team/system), full-scale (multiple agencies). The HSE Planner maintains the annual drill matrix (scenario × location × shift) so every shift practises every key scenario. Debriefs record lessons; actions are tracked.
    Types
    Muster, fire, H2S, man-down, confined space rescue, spill, medevac, crisis tabletop.
    Components

    Plan → brief observers → run → time key steps → debrief → actions → retest.
    O&G example
    An unannounced H2S drill shows muster took 9 minutes (target 5) because one route passed downwind. The route is re-signed and the drill repeated.
    Common mistakes
    Same scenario every time; only day shift; no debrief.
    Best practices
    Varied, realistic and some unannounced drills; measure times; track actions.

    🦁 HSE Leadership & Safety Culture

    Safety Culture & Leadership

    leadershipteam supervisioncommunication
    Definition
    Safety culture is the shared values, beliefs and behaviours about safety — “how we do things here when no one is watching”.
    Why it matters
    Procedures and equipment only work if people use and maintain them; culture decides that.
    Explanation
    The Hudson culture ladder (developed with Shell): pathological → reactive → calculative → proactive → generative. Leadership behaviours: visible field presence, asking open questions, listening, recognising good catches, consistent consequences, just culture (honest mistakes are learned from; reckless violations are not tolerated), challenging normalisation of deviance.
    Types
    Felt leadership visits, safety conversations, culture surveys, behaviour-based programmes.
    Components
    Leadership commitments, field visit programme, recognition scheme, culture survey, workforce engagement.
    Process / workflow
    Assess culture → set leadership behaviours → engage workforce → measure → adjust.
    O&G example
    A rig manager praises a new roustabout publicly for stopping a lift. Near-miss reports on the rig double over the next quarter — people feel safe to speak up.
    Common mistakes
    Blaming individuals; rewarding low injury numbers that discourage reporting; leaders absent from site.
    Best practices
    Leaders in the field weekly; act on what workers report; celebrate reporting.
    🧪 Practice activity: Culture level
    “We have lots of systems and we count everything, but safety is mainly the HSE department's job.” Which Hudson level?
    Answer: Calculative.

    🕸️ Integrated Risk Management

    Cumulative & Enterprise Risk

    risk assessmentadvanced technical analysisreporting
    Definition
    Managing HSE risks across activities, sites and the organisation — including the combined effect of degraded barriers, overrides, deferred maintenance and organisational change.
    Why it matters
    Major accidents usually involve several barriers failing together; each looked “acceptable” in isolation.
    Explanation
    Tools: barrier health dashboards, cumulative risk reviews per area, deferred safety-critical maintenance register, override register, organisational MOC, top-risk registers reported to management with owners.
    Types
    Operational risk, project risk, enterprise/corporate risk.
    Components
    Risk registers, barrier dashboards, review meetings, escalation thresholds.
    Process / workflow
    Collect → review cumulatively → compensate → escalate → restore.
    O&G example
    In one process area: two gas detectors impaired, a deluge test deferred, and a permit request for hot work. The cumulative review refuses the hot work until barriers are restored.
    Common mistakes
    Reviewing each item alone; registers without owners or expiry.
    Best practices
    Weekly cumulative review; clear escalation rules.
    🧪 Practice activity: Escalate?
    Why must an organisational change like reducing night-shift crew be risk-assessed?
    Answer: Fewer people can mean slower emergency response, fatigue and loss of critical skills — it can weaken barriers just like an equipment change.

    🧭 HSE Decision Making

    Making Hard HSE Calls

    problem-solvingleadershiprisk assessment
    Definition
    Deciding whether work proceeds, stops or changes when safety, schedule and cost pressures conflict.
    Why it matters
    Investigations often find a moment when a “stop” decision was possible but production pressure won.
    Explanation
    Fixed priorities: people → environment → asset → production/reputation. Use facts, risk assessment, and pre-agreed criteria (weather limits, gas levels, barrier status). Escalate to the right authority with options rather than just objections.
    Types
    Go/no-go decisions, stop-work decisions, deferral approvals, emergency decisions.
    Components
    Criteria, risk assessment, authority matrix, documentation.
    Process / workflow
    Facts → options (incl. stop) → risks → decide at right level → communicate → review.
    O&G example

    Interview Preparation

    Click a question to reveal a model answer. Role-based questions show what a strong and a weak answer look like.

    🧭 Fundamentals

    What is the difference between a hazard and a risk?
    A hazard is something with potential to cause harm (e.g. H2S). Risk is the likelihood and severity of harm from that hazard in a given situation.
    Explain the hierarchy of controls.
    Elimination → substitution → engineering controls → administrative controls → PPE. Higher controls are more reliable; PPE is the last line of defence.
    What is Stop Work Authority?
    Every person's right and duty to stop work they believe is unsafe, without fear of blame, until the concern is resolved.
    What is an HSE management system?
    A structured PDCA framework (e.g. ISO 45001, ISO 14001, IOGP 510) of policy, risk management, procedures, competence, monitoring, audit and improvement.

    ⚖️ Risk & Procedures

    How do you carry out a JSA?
    With the crew: break the job into steps, identify hazards per step, define controls and responsibilities, review at the worksite, sign, and update if conditions change.
    What does ALARP mean?
    As Low As Reasonably Practicable — risk reduced until the cost/effort of further reduction would be grossly disproportionate to the benefit, with options and reasoning documented.
    Explain how a permit-to-work system works.
    Request and plan → JSA → isolation and preparation → gas test → issue at worksite by area authority → toolbox talk → work and monitoring → suspend/close → hand back; permits cross-referenced and handed over between shifts.
    What is the gas test sequence for confined space entry?
    Oxygen first, then flammables (%LEL), then toxics (H2S, CO etc.), at several levels, and continuously during entry — with a standby and rescue plan.
    What is a bow-tie?
    A diagram showing threats leading to a top event (e.g. loss of containment) through prevention barriers, and consequences limited by mitigation barriers — used to manage and assure barriers.

    🩺 Health, Environment & Incidents

    How do you protect workers from H2S?
    Fixed and personal detection, training, wind awareness and escape routes, BA for entry, buddy system, contingency plan and drills; never rely on smell; no unprotected rescue.
    What's the difference between TWA and STEL?
    TWA is the average exposure limit over a working day (usually 8 h); STEL is a limit for a short period (usually 15 min).
    How do you calculate LTIF and TRIR?
    LTIF = LTIs × 1,000,000 ÷ hours worked. TRIR (OSHA basis) = recordable cases × 200,000 ÷ hours worked.
    How do you investigate an incident?
    Make safe, preserve evidence, form a team, gather people/positions/parts/paper evidence, build a timeline, analyse immediate and root causes (5 Whys, ICAM, bow-tie), set SMART actions, verify effectiveness and share lessons.
    What are oil spill response tiers?
    Tier 1 — local, on-site resources; Tier 2 — regional resources; Tier 3 — national/international resources.

    🚨 Emergency & Expert

    What makes an emergency response plan effective?
    Credible scenarios, clear roles and call trees, alarms and muster with headcount, resources, external links (medevac, mutual aid), regular realistic drills and updates after lessons.
    Why don't low injury rates prove good process safety?
    Personal injury rates measure frequent, low-severity events; process safety failures are rare and catastrophic and need their own indicators (Tier 1/2 events, barrier health) — as Texas City showed.
    How would you improve safety culture on a site?
    Visible leadership, listening and acting on concerns, recognising good catches, just culture, involving workers in risk assessment, consistent consequences, sharing lessons.
    What are leading indicators? Give examples.
    Measures of activities that prevent incidents: near-miss reporting rate, inspections and audits completed, action close-out on time, training compliance, safety-critical tests on time.

    👥 Role-Based & Behavioural

    (Supervisor) A crew is behind schedule and wants to skip gas re-testing. What do you do?
    Strong answer: Stop, re-test as required, explain why, and escalate the schedule issue rather than accept the shortcut.
    Weak answer: “Allow it this once.”
    (Planner) How do you build the annual drill programme?
    Strong answer: Drill matrix of key scenarios × locations × shifts, including unannounced and night drills, resource bookings, tracking and debrief actions.
    Weak answer: “A fire drill every few months.”
    (Quality Engineer) How do you know a CAPA worked?
    Strong answer: Define a measurable effectiveness check and verify it with data after implementation (e.g. permit audit error rate before/after).
    Weak answer: “When the action is marked closed.”
    (Analyst) What would your HSE dashboard show?
    Strong answer: Lagging (LTIF, TRIR, Tier 1/2, spills) and leading KPIs (near misses, inspections, actions, training), Pareto of incident types, trends by site/contractor, with action owners.
    Weak answer: “Total injuries this year.”
    (Behavioural, STAR) Tell me about a time you stopped work.
    Situation (lift with person under load), Task (prevent injury), Action (stopped lift, reset exclusion zone, briefed crew, reported near miss), Result (lift completed safely; site added exclusion-zone barriers to lift plans).
