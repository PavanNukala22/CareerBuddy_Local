    Refinery Fundamentals: Crude, Flow, Sections & Safety

    Start from zero: what a refinery does, where it sits in the oil and gas chain, what crude oil is made of, how it moves through the plant, and the safety basics every refinery operations person must know on day one.

    🏭 What is a Refinery?

    Petroleum Refinery

    process operationsengineering
    Definition
    A petroleum refinery is an industrial plant that converts crude oil into saleable products — LPG, gasoline (petrol), jet fuel/kerosene, diesel, fuel oil, bitumen, petroleum coke, sulfur and petrochemical feedstocks — by separating, converting, treating and blending hydrocarbons.
    Why it matters
    Crude oil has almost no direct use. Refineries turn it into the fuels that move transport, power industry and feed petrochemical plants. A refinery runs 24/7 on thin margins, so safe, stable and efficient operation decides whether it makes or loses money.
    Explanation
    Crude is a mixture of thousands of hydrocarbons with different boiling points. The refinery first separates it by boiling range (distillation), then converts heavy, low-value molecules into lighter, high-value ones (cracking, reforming), treats streams to remove sulfur and other contaminants, and finally blends components into products that meet specifications. Operations teams run these units continuously, around the clock, in shifts.
    Types
    • Topping / hydroskimming — distillation, reforming and hydrotreating only; simple, makes a lot of fuel oil.
    • Cracking (medium conversion) — adds FCC or hydrocracking to convert vacuum gas oil.
    • Coking (deep conversion) — adds a coker to convert vacuum residue; high complexity, high light-product yield.
    • Integrated refinery–petrochemical complex — sends naphtha, LPG and aromatics to petrochemical units.
    Components
    Crude and product tank farms, process units, utilities (steam, power, cooling water, air, nitrogen, fuel gas), flare system, control room (DCS), laboratory, dispatch facilities, effluent treatment.
    Process / workflow
    Receive crude → store & settle → desalt → distil → convert → treat → blend → test & certify → dispatch.
    Refinery example
    A 200,000 barrels-per-day (bpd) coking refinery may produce roughly 40–45% diesel/jet, 25–30% gasoline, small amounts of LPG, and sulfur and coke as by-products — almost no heavy fuel oil, because the coker converts the residue.
    Common mistakes
    Thinking a refinery is “one big column”; ignoring that units depend on each other (one unit's product is another's feed); treating a refinery as a batch plant — it is continuous.
    Best practices
    Learn the refinery's overall block flow diagram first, then each unit's P&ID. Always ask “where does my feed come from and where does my product go?”
    🧪 Practice activity: Classify the refinery
    A refinery has a crude unit, vacuum unit, naphtha hydrotreater, catalytic reformer, diesel hydrotreater and an FCC — but no coker. Which type is it, and where does its vacuum residue go?
    Answer: A cracking (medium-conversion) refinery. Vacuum residue goes to fuel oil / bitumen blending (or is sold), because there is no coker to convert it.

    🛢️ Crude Oil Basics

    Crude Oil Quality

    process operationscalculations
    Definition
    Crude oil is a naturally occurring liquid mixture of hydrocarbons plus impurities (sulfur, nitrogen, metals, salt, water, sediment). Its quality is described by a crude assay.
    Why it matters
    Crude quality decides product yields, corrosion risk, catalyst life, hydrogen demand and how hard each unit must work. A change of crude can change almost every operating target in the refinery.
    Explanation
    • API gravity measures lightness: °API = 141.5 ÷ SG(60°F) − 131.5. Higher API = lighter crude = more naphtha and distillates.
    • Sulfur: sweet crude < ~0.5 wt% S; sour crude above that. Sour crude needs more hydrotreating and sulfur recovery, and brings H2S hazards.
    • Hydrocarbon families: paraffins (straight/branched chains), naphthenes (rings), aromatics (benzene rings). Olefins are not normally present in crude — they are formed by cracking.
    • Contaminants: salts (chlorides → HCl corrosion), water and sediment (BS&W), metals Ni and V (poison catalysts), nitrogen, naphthenic acids (TAN → corrosion).
    Types
    Light sweet (e.g. ~38–42 °API, <0.5% S), medium sour, heavy sour (<22 °API, high S and metals), high-TAN (acidic) crudes, and opportunity/discounted crudes.
    Components
    Assay data: API/SG, sulfur, TAN, pour point, viscosity, salt (PTB = pounds per thousand barrels), BS&W, metals, and the TBP distillation curve showing the % of crude in each boiling range.
    Process / workflow
    Planning selects crude using the assay and LP model → operations receives the crude assay and operating guidelines → targets for desalter, CDU cut points and downstream units are set → lab confirms actual quality on receipt.
    Refinery example
    Switching from a light sweet crude to a heavy sour crude: atmospheric residue increases, the vacuum unit and coker get more load, diesel hydrotreater hydrogen demand rises, sour water and amine loads rise, and the overhead corrosion risk increases.
    Common mistakes
    Ignoring the assay when crude changes; not adjusting desalter wash water or chemical injection; assuming the same cut points work for every crude.
    Best practices
    Plan crude switches in advance with a written crude-change procedure, ramp rates and extra lab sampling; watch overhead chloride and pH trends closely during and after the switch.
    🧪 Practice activity: Calculate API gravity
    A crude has a specific gravity of 0.876 at 60°F. Calculate its API gravity and classify it.
    Answer: 141.5 ÷ 0.876 − 131.5 = 161.53 − 131.5 ≈ 30.0 °API — a medium crude.

    Basic Refinery Process Flow

    P&IDprocess operations
    Definition
    The process flow is the route each stream takes between units, shown on the Block Flow Diagram (BFD), Process Flow Diagram (PFD) and, in full detail, the Piping & Instrumentation Diagram (P&ID).
    Why it matters
    Operators must know what happens upstream and downstream of their unit. A change in CDU naphtha end point, for example, changes the reformer feed and gasoline blending.
    Explanation
    Crude → CDU splits into gas, naphtha, kerosene, diesel and atmospheric residue. Residue → VDU → vacuum gas oil (to FCC/hydrocracker) and vacuum residue (to coker, bitumen or fuel oil). Naphtha → NHT → reformer (gasoline + hydrogen). Kerosene/diesel → hydrotreaters. Sour gases → amine unit → sulfur recovery. Finished components → blending → tanks → dispatch.
    Types
    BFD (overview, one box per unit), PFD (main equipment, streams, heat & material balance), P&ID (every line, valve, instrument, interlock).
    Components
    Feeds, products, intermediate tanks, recycle streams, utility streams (steam, fuel gas, hydrogen), relief and flare connections.
    Process / workflow
    Trace a stream: start at the source, follow line numbers on the P&ID, note control valves, block valves, check valves and instruments, and end at the receiving unit or tank.
    Refinery example
    When the FCC trips, its VGO feed must be routed to an intermediate tank; the CDU/VDU may need to reduce rate if tank space runs out. Operators who understand the flow act in minutes instead of hours.
    Common mistakes
    Using an out-of-date P&ID; ignoring small lines (bypasses, drains, vents) that create unexpected paths; not knowing which tank a rundown line is aligned to.
    Best practices
    Walk the line in the field with the P&ID; keep line-up checklists; verify tank alignment at every shift handover.
    🧪 Practice activity: Trace a stream
    Name the path of straight-run heavy naphtha from crude to finished gasoline.
    Answer: CDU overhead/side draw → naphtha splitter → naphtha hydrotreater (removes sulfur, nitrogen that poison reformer catalyst) → catalytic reformer (raises octane, produces hydrogen) → reformate tank → gasoline blending → certified product tank → dispatch.

    🦺 Basic Safety Concepts

    Refinery Hazards & Basic Controls

    process safetysafety
    Definition
    Refinery safety is the control of hazards from flammable hydrocarbons, toxic gases, high pressure, high temperature and stored energy so that nobody is hurt and nothing is released.
    Why it matters
    A refinery holds large inventories of flammable material above its flash or auto-ignition temperature. A small leak can become a fire or explosion within seconds.
    Explanation
    • Fire triangle: fuel + oxygen + ignition. Remove one and there is no fire.
    • LEL/UEL: a gas burns only between its lower and upper explosive limits; gas detectors alarm at a % of LEL.
    • H2S: highly toxic, deadens the sense of smell at higher concentrations — never rely on smell; always carry a personal gas monitor.
    • Benzene: carcinogen in gasoline streams; control exposure during sampling.
    • Pyrophoric iron sulfide: can self-ignite when exposed to air after opening equipment.
    • Hot surfaces, steam, pressure: burns and line-opening incidents.
    Types
    Occupational (personal) safety — slips, falls, burns; Process safety — loss of containment, fire, explosion, toxic release.
    Components
    PPE (FR clothing, helmet, goggles, gloves, H2S monitor), Permit to Work, energy isolation (LOTO), gas testing, confined-space controls, fire and gas detection, emergency shutdown (ESD), Stop Work Authority.
    Process / workflow
    Before any task: Job Safety Analysis (JSA) → permit → isolation & gas test → toolbox talk → work → hand back → remove isolations.
    Refinery example
    A field operator taking a sour water sample wears a personal H2S monitor, stands upwind, uses a closed-loop sampling point and has a buddy watching — because sour water can release H2S.
    Common mistakes
    Opening a drain without checking for pressure; entering a unit without a gas monitor; assuming a line is empty because the gauge reads zero.
    Best practices
    Use Stop Work Authority whenever unsure; double-check isolations; report near-misses; follow the “two-key” rule — check and verify before every line break.
    🧪 Practice activity: Spot the hazard
    An operator plans to open a pump casing drain to check for liquid after the pump was stopped and the suction/discharge valves closed. What is missing?
    Answer: Confirmation the casing is depressured and drained to a closed system, a valid permit/isolation, gas test, PPE and awareness that the fluid may be hot, flammable or contain H2S. Closed valves can pass — verify, do not assume.
    Q. A crude has 1.8 wt% sulfur. How is it classified, and what does it mean for operations?
    • A Sweet crude — no extra treating needed
    • B Sour crude — more hydrotreating, amine/sulfur recovery load and H₂S hazards
    • C Light crude — higher naphtha yield only
    • D Sulfur does not affect refinery operations

    🚢 Crude Receipt, Handling & Desalting

    Crude Receipt & Desalting

    process operationsequipment/process operationmeasurements
    Definition
    Crude receipt is the controlled transfer of crude from pipeline or tanker into refinery tanks. Desalting removes salts, water and solids from crude before distillation using wash water, heat, mixing and an electrostatic field.
    Why it matters
    Salts (mainly chlorides) hydrolyse in the crude heater to form HCl, which corrodes the CDU overhead system. Solids foul exchangers; water flashes and causes pressure surges. Good desalting protects the whole refinery.
    Explanation
    Crude is received, allowed to settle, then free water is drained. Tanks are gauged and sampled for API, sulfur, BS&W and salt. In the desalter (typically ~120–150 °C), wash water (about 4–8% of crude) is mixed in through a mix valve, forming an emulsion; an electrostatic grid coalesces water droplets, which settle with dissolved salt and leave as brine. Demulsifier chemicals help break the emulsion.
    Types
    Single-stage and two-stage desalters; tank receipt by pipeline vs marine discharge; tank mixers (side-entry) for crude blending.
    Components
    Crude tanks with water draw-offs, mixers, sampling points, gauging; desalter vessel, transformers and grids, mix valve, wash-water and demulsifier injection, interface level instrument, brine outlet.
    Process / workflow
    Line-up check → receipt → settle (often 12–24 h) → water drain → sample & certify → feed to CDU → preheat → desalter → monitor desalted crude salt (e.g. target < 1–3 PTB) and brine oil content.
    Refinery example
    Desalted salt rises from 1 PTB to 6 PTB after a crude switch. Overhead chlorides climb. The operator checks interface level, mix-valve ΔP, wash-water rate and demulsifier dosing — the new crude formed a tight emulsion, so demulsifier is increased and mix-valve ΔP reduced.
    Common mistakes
    Running mix-valve ΔP too high (stable emulsion, oil in brine); poor interface level control (water carry-over to the heater or oil to the effluent plant); skipping tank water draining.
    Best practices
    Trend desalted salt, brine oil content and transformer amps every shift; drain tanks fully before feeding; coordinate crude switches with the lab.
    🧪 Practice activity: Diagnose the desalter
    Desalter transformer amps are high and brine contains oil. What is the likely cause and first action?
    Answer: High interface level or an emulsion (“rag”) layer near the grid shorting the field. Lower the interface level, check mix-valve ΔP and demulsifier rate, and check brine for oil.

    ⚗️ Separation & Distillation Principles

    Distillation

    P&IDcalculationstechnical design
    Definition
    Distillation separates a liquid mixture into fractions by repeatedly vaporising and condensing it, using the difference in boiling points (relative volatility) of the components.
    Why it matters
    Distillation is the first and largest separation step in every refinery and is used again in almost every unit (stabilisers, splitters, fractionators). Poor separation sends wrong material to downstream units and makes off-spec products.
    Explanation
    Vapour rises up the column and liquid flows down. On each tray or packed section they contact, light components move into the vapour and heavy components into the liquid. Reflux (liquid returned at the top) improves purity of top products; reboiling or stripping steam at the bottom drives light components out of the bottoms. Product quality is controlled mainly by temperature profile, reflux, draw rates and pressure.
    Types
    Atmospheric, vacuum, pressure distillation (e.g. debutaniser); tray columns vs packed columns; flash drums (single-stage separation).
    Components
    Column shell, trays/packing, feed (flash) zone, overhead condenser, reflux drum, reflux pump, side draws and side strippers, pumparounds, reboiler or stripping steam, bottoms pump.
    Process / workflow
    Feed enters → flash → vapour/liquid contact on trays → overhead condensed and partly refluxed → side products drawn and stripped → bottoms removed. Operator adjusts draw rates and temperatures to hit cut points.
    Refinery example
    Kerosene flash point is low. Light material is staying in the kerosene. The operator increases kerosene side-stripper steam to strip the light ends out — flash point rises back into specification.
    Common mistakes
    Changing several variables at once; ignoring column ΔP (early sign of flooding); pulling too much product from one draw and “drying” the trays below.
    Best practices
    Make one change at a time, wait for the column to settle, confirm with lab results; keep a material balance closed across the column.
    🧪 Practice activity: Which lever?
    Diesel 95% distillation point is too high (product too heavy). Which draw do you change?
    Answer: Reduce the diesel draw rate (or lower the diesel draw temperature). Less draw means more heavy material goes down to the next cut/residue, so diesel becomes lighter.

    🗼 Atmospheric Distillation (CDU)

    Figure 2.1 · Simplified crude distillation unit (temperatures are typical, not design values)

    Crude Distillation Unit Operation

    P&IDprocess operationstroubleshooting
    Definition
    The CDU (atmospheric distillation unit) heats desalted crude and separates it in a column operating slightly above atmospheric pressure into gas, naphtha, kerosene, diesel, gas oil and atmospheric residue.
    Why it matters
    It sets the throughput of the whole refinery. Every barrel passes through it; its cut points decide feed quality for every downstream unit.
    Explanation
    Crude is heated in a preheat train (exchangers recovering heat from products and pumparounds), desalted, then heated in a fired heater to roughly 350–370 °C — hotter would start thermal cracking and coking. In the flash zone the vaporised part rises; side products are drawn off and steam-stripped in side strippers. Pumparounds remove heat at intermediate points, controlling internal reflux and recovering energy. The overhead is condensed; ammonia/amine neutraliser, filming inhibitor and wash water protect against HCl corrosion.
    Types
    Single-column CDU; pre-flash drum or pre-flash column ahead of the heater (removes light ends, debottlenecks the heater); two-stage overhead systems.
    Components
    Preheat train, desalter, fired heater, main column, side strippers, pumparounds, overhead condensers, reflux drum, naphtha stabiliser, corrosion-control injection points.
    Process / workflow
    Set crude rate → control heater outlet temperature (COT) → control top temperature with reflux → control side draws on flow/temperature → strip with steam → monitor overhead pH, chlorides and iron → confirm product quality with lab.
    Refinery example
    Overhead water pH drops from 6.0 to 4.8 and chlorides rise. The board operator increases neutraliser injection, checks wash-water flow, informs the process engineer, and asks the field operator to verify desalter performance.
    Common mistakes
    Raising heater outlet temperature to increase distillate yield beyond the cracking limit; letting overhead temperature fall below the water dew point (salt deposition and corrosion); ignoring preheat-train fouling.
    Best practices
    Respect heater and corrosion IOWs; monitor preheat-train outlet temperature (fouling indicator); keep pumparound duties balanced; keep overhead temperature above the salt/water dew point margin.
    🧪 Practice activity: Read the trend
    Over three months the furnace inlet temperature has fallen 12 °C at the same crude rate, and firing has increased. What is happening and what is the cost?
    Answer: The preheat train is fouling, recovering less heat. The furnace burns more fuel (higher energy cost and CO2), and may hit its firing limit and cap throughput. Plan exchanger cleaning based on the economics.

    🌡️ Vacuum Distillation (VDU)

    Vacuum Distillation Unit

    process operationscalculations
    Definition
    The VDU distils atmospheric residue under deep vacuum (typically a few tens of mmHg absolute in the flash zone) to recover vacuum gas oils without cracking.
    Why it matters
    Reducing pressure lowers boiling points, so heavy gas oil can be vaporised at ~390–420 °C instead of far higher temperatures that would crack and coke the oil. Vacuum gas oil (VGO) is the main feed for FCC and hydrocracker units — high-value products.
    Explanation
    Residue is heated in the vacuum heater (with velocity steam in some designs) and flashed in a large-diameter column. Steam ejectors or vacuum pumps create the vacuum. Packed beds condense LVGO and HVGO; a wash bed removes entrained residue and metals from HVGO. Vacuum residue leaves the bottom, often quenched to stop coking.
    Types
    Wet vacuum (stripping steam) vs dry vacuum; fuels-type VDU (max VGO) vs lube-type VDU (narrow cuts for lube oils).
    Components
    Vacuum heater, transfer line, column with packed beds, wash bed, ejector system with condensers, hot well, bottoms quench, LVGO/HVGO pumparounds.
    Process / workflow
    Establish vacuum → heat residue → control flash zone temperature and pressure → control wash oil rate → draw LVGO/HVGO → quench and cool residue.
    Refinery example
    Column top pressure rises from 15 to 30 mmHg. VGO yield drops and residue increases. Cause: low motive steam pressure to the ejectors. Restoring steam pressure recovers vacuum and VGO yield.
    Common mistakes
    Cutting wash-oil flow too low — the wash bed dries out and cokes; overheating the residue; ignoring ejector condenser cooling-water temperature.
    Best practices
    Keep minimum wetting rate on the wash bed; trend HVGO metals/colour (entrainment); check ejector steam pressure and condenser performance each shift.
    🧪 Practice activity: Why vacuum?
    Explain to a new technician in one sentence why the residue is not simply heated hotter in the CDU.
    Answer: Above roughly 370–400 °C the oil starts to crack and form coke, so instead we lower the pressure, which lowers its boiling point and lets us vaporise gas oil safely.

    Conversion (Upgrading Heavy Oil)

    process operationsprocess safetyproblem-solving
    Definition
    Conversion processes change the molecular structure of hydrocarbons — breaking large molecules (cracking), rearranging them (reforming, isomerization) or combining small ones (alkylation).
    Why it matters
    Straight-run distillation gives too much heavy oil and not enough gasoline/diesel. Conversion units create most of the refinery's margin.
    Explanation
    Thermal conversion uses heat alone (coking, visbreaking). Catalytic conversion uses catalyst to work at lower severity and steer product quality (FCC, reforming). Hydro-conversion adds hydrogen under pressure to crack and saturate at the same time (hydrocracking). Severity (temperature, residence time, catalyst activity, pressure) controls conversion level.
    Types
    Thermal, catalytic, hydroprocessing; molecular rearrangement (reforming, isomerization); combination (alkylation, polymerization).
    Components
    Feed heaters, reactors, catalyst systems (fixed bed, moving bed, fluidised), regenerators, fractionators, gas plants, compressors.
    Process / workflow
    Prepare feed (remove contaminants) → heat → react → separate products in a fractionator → recover gases → send products to treating/blending; monitor catalyst activity and yields.
    Refinery example
    A hydrocracker bed temperature rises rapidly (exotherm). Procedures call for quench gas increase and, if it continues past the limit, the emergency depressuring system — because a temperature runaway can damage the reactor within minutes.
    Common mistakes
    Increasing severity without checking downstream capacity (gas compressor, fractionator); feeding contaminated feed to catalyst units; slow response to reactor temperature excursions.
    Best practices
    Operate inside IOWs; track catalyst activity trends; coordinate severity changes with the planning (LP) team and downstream units.
    🧪 Practice activity: Match the unit
    Which unit would you use to (a) raise naphtha octane and produce hydrogen, (b) convert vacuum residue with no catalyst, (c) produce top-quality jet and diesel from VGO?
    Answer: (a) Catalytic reformer, (b) Delayed coker, (c) Hydrocracker.

    🧼 Treatment Processes

    Treating: Hydrotreating, Amine & Sulfur Recovery

    process safetyqualityprocess operations
    Definition
    Treatment processes remove impurities — sulfur, nitrogen, mercaptans, olefins, H2S, CO2 — so products meet specifications and catalysts and the environment are protected.
    Why it matters
    Fuel specifications (e.g. diesel and gasoline ≤ 10 ppm sulfur under Euro VI / BS-VI) cannot be met without treating. Untreated H2S would go to fuel gas and atmosphere as SO2.
    Explanation
    • Hydrotreating (HDS/HDN): feed + hydrogen over Co-Mo or Ni-Mo catalyst at ~300–400 °C converts sulfur to H2S and nitrogen to NH3.
    • Amine treating: MDEA/DEA solution absorbs H2S from gases; a regenerator strips it out as acid gas.
    • Sulfur Recovery Unit (Claus): burns one-third of H2S to SO2, which reacts with remaining H2S to form elemental sulfur; a tail-gas unit raises recovery above ~99%.
    • Sour water stripper: removes H2S and NH3 from process water.
    • Merox / caustic treating: converts or removes mercaptans in LPG and kerosene.
    Types
    Naphtha, kerosene, diesel and VGO hydrotreaters; amine absorbers and regenerators; Claus SRU + TGTU; caustic and Merox treaters.
    Components
    Feed/effluent exchangers, heater, reactors, high/low-pressure separators, recycle gas compressor, amine scrubber, stripper; amine contactors and regenerator; SRU reaction furnace, converters, sulfur pit.
    Process / workflow
    Hydrotreater: feed + H2 → heat → react → cool → separate gas (recycled after amine scrubbing) → strip product → lab test sulfur. Sour gas → amine → acid gas → SRU → liquid sulfur.
    Refinery example
    Diesel product sulfur rises to 14 ppm (spec 10). The operator raises reactor inlet temperature slightly within the IOW, checks recycle-gas H2 purity and amine scrubber performance, and diverts product to an off-spec tank until the lab confirms recovery.
    Common mistakes
    Raising reactor temperature too fast (shortens catalyst life); letting rich amine carry hydrocarbons to the regenerator (foaming); losing SRU air/acid-gas ratio control (sulfur emissions).
    Best practices
    Use weighted average bed temperature (WABT) trends to track catalyst ageing; keep amine clean and lean loading in range; maintain SRU tail-gas ratio analyser.
    🧪 Practice activity: Follow the sulfur
    Trace a sulfur atom in crude diesel to its final form.
    Answer: Diesel hydrotreater converts it to H2S → recycle gas amine scrubber absorbs it → amine regenerator releases it as acid gas → SRU converts it to elemental sulfur → sold as liquid or solid sulfur.

    🧴 Blending

    Product Blending

    calculationsqualityExcel/Power BI
    Definition
    Blending mixes refinery components in set proportions to make finished products that meet every specification at the lowest cost.
    Why it matters
    Blending is where the refinery earns or loses money at the end: “giveaway” (quality better than needed, e.g. octane above spec) wastes value; off-spec blends need costly re-blending and delay ships.
    Explanation
    Gasoline is blended from reformate, FCC gasoline, isomerate, alkylate and butane to meet RON/MON octane, RVP (vapour pressure), sulfur, benzene, aromatics, distillation. Diesel is blended from hydrotreated straight-run and cracked diesels to meet cetane, sulfur, flash point, density, cold-flow (CFPP/pour point). Some properties blend linearly (sulfur by weight), others do not (octane, RVP) — blend models use indices.
    Types
    Batch (tank) blending — components into a tank, mix, sample, certify. In-line blending — components metered continuously with on-line analysers, often certified on the run.
    Components
    Component tanks, blend header, flow meters/ratio controllers, on-line analysers, mixers, additive injection, blend optimisation software, lab.
    Process / workflow
    Planner issues blend recipe → operations lines up tanks → blend → mix/circulate → sample → lab certification → release for dispatch.
    Refinery example
    A gasoline blend has RON 95.6 vs spec 95.0 — 0.6 octane giveaway on 50,000 m3. Re-optimising with less reformate and more butane (within RVP limits) brings RON to 95.2 and saves significant value.
    Common mistakes
    Sampling before the tank is fully mixed; wrong tank line-up contaminating a certified tank; ignoring the non-linear blending of octane.
    Best practices
    Verify line-ups with a second person; circulate for the required time; track giveaway as a KPI; reconcile tank gauges with meter totals.
    🧪 Practice activity: Linear blend
    Blend 60% diesel A (8 ppm S) with 40% diesel B (13 ppm S). Is the blend within a 10 ppm spec? (Assume similar density.)
    Answer: 0.6×8 + 0.4×13 = 4.8 + 5.2 = 10.0 ppm — right at the limit with zero margin. Lab reproducibility makes this risky; reduce B or add a lower-sulfur component.

    💧 Pumps

    Refinery Pumps

    equipment/process operationtroubleshootingpreventive maintenance
    Definition
    A pump adds energy to a liquid to move it. Refineries mostly use centrifugal pumps; positive displacement (PD) pumps are used for dosing chemicals, viscous liquids and high pressure.
    Why it matters
    Pump seal failures are among the most frequent causes of hydrocarbon leaks and fires. A tripped charge pump can stop a whole unit.
    Explanation
    • NPSH: the pump needs enough suction pressure above the liquid's vapour pressure (NPSH available > NPSH required) or it cavitates — noise, vibration, low discharge pressure, damage.
    • Centrifugal pumps are started with the discharge valve closed or nearly closed (low power) and must keep a minimum flow to avoid overheating.
    • PD pumps must never run against a closed discharge — pressure rises until something fails; they need a relief valve.
    • Mechanical seals (often dual seals with barrier fluid for hazardous service) prevent leaks along the shaft.
    Types
    Centrifugal (overhung, between-bearing, multistage), PD (reciprocating, gear, screw, diaphragm metering); main + spare (A/B) configuration.
    Components
    Casing, impeller, shaft, bearings, mechanical seal and seal support system, coupling, driver (motor/steam turbine), suction strainer, discharge check valve, minimum-flow line.
    Process / workflow
    Change-over: check spare (lube, seal pot, cooling, warmed-up if hot service) → open suction → vent/prime → start → open discharge slowly → confirm flow/pressure → reduce and stop the running pump → isolate if needed.
    Refinery example
    A hot residue pump's spare is started without warming. Thermal shock damages the seal and it leaks — residue above its auto-ignition temperature catches fire. Warm-up lines exist exactly to prevent this.
    Common mistakes
    Closing suction instead of discharge; running dry; ignoring rising vibration or seal-pot level; leaving the strainer blocked.
    Best practices
    Follow the pump change-over checklist; record vibration and temperature on rounds; keep spares ready (“hot standby” where required); report seal leaks immediately.
    🧪 Practice activity: Cavitation check
    A pump is noisy, discharge pressure is fluctuating and the suction drum level is low. Diagnosis and action?
    Answer: Cavitation due to low NPSH available. Restore drum level (reduce pump flow / increase inflow), check suction strainer ΔP and ensure the suction valve is fully open.

    🌀 Compressors

    Refinery Compressors

    equipment/process operationprocess safety
    Definition
    A compressor raises the pressure of a gas. Key services: hydrotreater/hydrocracker recycle hydrogen, make-up hydrogen, FCC wet gas, FCC main air blower, fuel gas and instrument air.
    Why it matters
    Compressor trips often shut down the whole unit (e.g. a hydrocracker without recycle gas must depressure). They are expensive, critical, and sensitive to liquid.
    Explanation
    Centrifugal compressors can surge — flow reverses when flow is too low against the discharge pressure, causing violent vibration; an anti-surge controller opens a recycle valve to keep flow above the surge line. Reciprocating compressors are damaged by liquid (incompressible), so knock-out drums with high-level trips protect the suction.
    Types
    Centrifugal, reciprocating, screw, axial (air blowers).
    Components
    Suction KO drum, compressor, driver, lube and seal-oil/dry-gas-seal system, inter/after-coolers, anti-surge valve, vibration and temperature monitoring, trip system.
    Process / workflow
    Pre-start: KO drum drained, lube/seal systems running, permissives satisfied, purge done → start on recycle → load gradually → monitor vibration, temperatures, surge margin.
    Refinery example
    FCC wet gas compressor suction pressure rises because the main fractionator overhead is hotter — more gas, and the compressor reaches its limit. Operators reduce reactor temperature slightly to reduce gas make — a classic compressor-constrained unit.
    Common mistakes
    Putting anti-surge control in manual; ignoring KO drum level; restarting after a trip before the cause is known.
    Best practices
    Keep anti-surge on automatic; monitor surge margin and vibration trends; investigate every trip before restart.
    🧪 Practice activity: Protect the compressor
    A reciprocating compressor KO drum high-level alarm comes in. What do you do and why?
    Answer: Drain the KO drum immediately (to a closed system), find why liquid is carrying over (upstream separator level, cooler temperature). Liquid entering a reciprocating compressor can break valves, rods or the cylinder; the high-high level trip will stop the machine.

    🔥 Heat Exchangers & Fired Heaters

    Heat Exchangers

    calculationsequipment/process operation
    Definition
    A heat exchanger transfers heat from a hot stream to a cold stream without mixing them.
    Why it matters
    Exchangers recover energy (preheat trains), condense overheads and cool products for storage. Fouling and tube leaks are common causes of lost energy, contamination and unplanned shutdowns.
    Explanation
    Duty Q = U × A × LMTD. Fouling reduces U (heat-transfer coefficient), so outlet temperatures drift and ΔP rises. A tube leak lets the higher-pressure fluid contaminate the other side — e.g. hydrocarbon in cooling water.
    Types
    Shell-and-tube, air-cooled (fin-fan), plate, double-pipe; condensers, reboilers, coolers, feed/effluent exchangers.
    Components
    Shell, tube bundle, tube sheets, channel heads, baffles, bypass and isolation valves, vents/drains, temperature and pressure indicators.
    Process / workflow
    Monitor inlet/outlet temperatures and ΔP → calculate U trend → plan cleaning when economics justify → isolate, drain, purge, hand over → clean → leak test → recommission slowly (avoid thermal shock).
    Refinery example
    Hydrocarbon detected at the cooling tower; cooling-water return from a product cooler shows oil sheen. The cooler is isolated and leak-tested — a tube has failed.
    Common mistakes
    Closing the cold side while the hot side is flowing (thermal expansion overpressure); opening a bypass without accounting for downstream temperature.
    Best practices
    Trend U-values; follow warm-up procedures; check cooling-water hydrocarbon analysers.
    🧪 Practice activity: Fouling or flow?
    Exchanger outlet temperature fell 8 °C, ΔP rose 40%, flow is unchanged. Fouling or flow change?
    Answer: Fouling — lower heat transfer with higher pressure drop at constant flow.

    Fired Heaters (Furnaces)

    process safetycalculationssafety
    Definition
    A fired heater heats process fluid flowing in tubes using heat from burning fuel in a refractory-lined firebox.
    Why it matters
    Heaters are the largest energy users in a refinery and a major fire/explosion risk (firebox explosions on light-off, tube ruptures).
    Explanation
    Radiant section (flames heat tubes by radiation) and convection section (flue gas heats tubes). Key variables: coil outlet temperature (COT), excess O2 in flue gas (typically ~2–4% for gas firing), draft (slightly negative pressure at the arch), tube metal temperature (TMT) and pass-flow balance. Low pass flow → tube overheating → coking → rupture.
    Types
    Natural, forced, induced and balanced draft; cabin and vertical cylindrical heaters; with or without air preheaters.
    Components
    Burners and pilots, fuel gas system, Burner Management System (BMS), tubes/coils, stack and damper, O2/CO analysers, TMT thermocouples, snuffing steam, low-flow trips.
    Process / workflow
    Light-off: purge the firebox (typically 5 volume changes), gas test, light pilots, light burners one by one → raise temperature at controlled rate → monitor flames, O2, draft, TMTs.
    Refinery example
    O2 falls to 0.5% and CO rises — sub-stoichiometric combustion. Unburnt fuel can ignite suddenly in the convection section. The operator reduces firing first, then increases air — never adds air quickly into a fuel-rich firebox.
    Common mistakes
    Running excessive O2 (wastes fuel); ignoring flame impingement on tubes; relighting a burner from hot refractory without purging.
    Best practices
    Daily visual inspection through peep holes; keep pass flows balanced; respect TMT IOW limits; follow BMS purge and light-off sequences.
    🧪 Practice activity: Heater rounds
    List four things a field operator checks on a heater during rounds.
    Answer: Flame shape and colour (no impingement), tube colour (no hot spots/glowing), draft at the arch, burner and pilot condition, fuel gas pressure, O2 analyser reading, any leaks or smoke from the stack.

    🛢️ Vessels & Columns

    Vessels, Drums & Column Hydraulics

    troubleshootingP&ID
    Definition
    Pressure vessels hold or separate process fluids (drums, separators, reactors). Columns are vertical vessels for distillation, absorption or stripping.
    Why it matters
    Levels in drums decide whether liquid goes to a compressor or gas goes to a pump. Column hydraulics decide separation quality and capacity.
    Explanation
    • Flooding: too much vapour/liquid traffic; liquid backs up, ΔP rises sharply, product quality collapses.
    • Weeping/dumping: too little vapour; liquid falls through tray holes, efficiency drops.
    • Entrainment / carry-over: liquid droplets carried up with vapour (dark distillate colour).
    • Foaming: common in amine contactors; rapid ΔP rise and liquid carry-over.
    Types
    Reflux drums, flash drums, three-phase separators (oil/water/gas), KO drums, surge drums, reactors; tray and packed columns.
    Components
    Shell, nozzles, internals (trays, packing, demisters, distributors), level gauges and transmitters, relief valves, manholes, water boot.
    Process / workflow
    Monitor ΔP across sections, level (two independent measurements on critical vessels), interface level, temperatures; compare with design capacity.
    Refinery example
    Main column ΔP rises from 0.25 to 0.6 bar after crude rate increase; diesel colour darkens. The column is flooding — crude rate is reduced until ΔP returns to normal.
    Common mistakes
    Trusting a single level gauge; overfilling a drum to the relief valve; ignoring water accumulation in the reflux drum boot.
    Best practices
    Cross-check level readings (field gauge vs transmitter); trend ΔP; drain water boots on schedule.
    🧪 Practice activity: Flood or weep?
    Column ΔP is very low and product separation is poor after a large rate reduction. Which problem?
    Answer: Weeping — vapour rate too low to hold liquid on the trays. Increase vapour (reboil/stripping) within limits or operate at a higher minimum rate.

    🎛️ Instrumentation, Control & P&ID Reading

    Figure 3.1 · A level control loop as drawn on a P&ID: transmitter LT-101 → controller LIC-101 (DCS, line in bubble) → control valve LV-101

    Instrumentation & Control Basics

    P&IDtechnical documentationtroubleshooting
    Definition
    Instrumentation measures process variables (pressure, temperature, flow, level, composition). Control systems compare the measurement with a setpoint and move a final element (usually a control valve) to keep the process on target.
    Why it matters
    Operators run the refinery through the DCS. Understanding loops, alarms and trips lets them recognise bad measurements, sticking valves and controllers fighting each other.
    Explanation
    • Tag letters: first letter = variable (F flow, L level, P pressure, T temperature, A analysis); next letters = function (I indicate, C control, T transmit, A alarm, V valve). FIC-205 = flow indicating controller, loop 205.
    • PID control: proportional (acts on error), integral (removes offset), derivative (acts on rate of change).
    • Cascade: a master (e.g. temperature) sets the setpoint of a slave (e.g. fuel flow). Split range: one controller drives two valves. Ratio: keeps two flows in proportion.
    • Safety Instrumented System (SIS/ESD) is separate from the DCS and trips equipment independently when safe limits are exceeded.
    Types
    Controller modes: manual, auto, cascade; basic regulatory control vs Advanced Process Control (APC/MPC); alarms (H, HH, L, LL) vs trips.
    Components
    Transmitters, DCS controllers, control valves with positioners, SIS logic solver, shutdown valves, analysers, field gauges.
    Process / workflow
    Read P&ID: identify line number and spec → equipment tags → instruments and loops → valve fail position → interlocks (often shown with a diamond or note) → relief devices.
    Refinery example
    A flow controller shows PV steady at setpoint but the valve output is at 100%. The valve is fully open and can no longer control — a hidden capacity limit. The operator reports it before a disturbance turns it into an upset.
    Common mistakes
    Leaving controllers in manual for long periods; trusting a frozen transmitter; bypassing a trip without authorisation and a risk assessment.
    Best practices
    Cross-check critical measurements; watch controller output, not just PV; manage trip bypasses through a formal override register with time limits.
    🧪 Practice activity: Decode the tags
    Decode: TIC-301, PSV-12, LAHH-104, FV-205.
    Answer: Temperature indicating controller 301; pressure safety (relief) valve 12; level alarm high-high 104 (usually linked to a trip); flow control valve 205.

    📟 Process Monitoring

    Process Monitoring & Operating Envelopes

    progress monitoringtrend analysisKPI tracking
    Definition
    Process monitoring is the continuous checking of process variables, equipment condition and product quality against defined limits — from both the control room and field rounds.
    Why it matters
    Most incidents give early warning signs (slow trends, small leaks, unusual noise). Monitoring catches them while there is still time to act calmly.
    Explanation
    Limits are layered: target / normal operating range → IOW standard limits (equipment damage over time if exceeded) → critical IOW limits (prompt action required) → safety trip limits → design limits. Alarms should be meaningful — industry guidance (EEMUA 191 / ISA-18.2) targets about one alarm per 10 minutes per operator in steady operation; alarm floods hide the important alarm.
    Types
    DCS trends and overview screens, field rounds with checklists, lab results, on-line analysers, condition monitoring (vibration, thermography), shift logs.
    Components
    Operating envelope document, IOW list, alarm rationalisation database, round sheets, shift log, handover notes.
    Process / workflow
    Start of shift: review handover, standing alarms, bypasses, open permits → hourly scan of key trends → field rounds (look, listen, feel, smell — safely) → record deviations → act / escalate → handover.
    Refinery example
    On rounds a technician notices a new hissing sound and frost on a small LPG drain line — a passing valve leaking. Isolating and reporting it early prevents a vapour cloud.
    Common mistakes
    Silencing alarms without action; filling round sheets without walking the plant; normalising a “standing alarm”.
    Best practices
    Review trends over 24 h and 7 days, not only current values; escalate IOW exceedances; keep the standing-alarm list at zero.
    🧪 Practice activity: Prioritise alarms
    Three alarms arrive together: (1) tank high level in a product tank with 6 h to overflow, (2) heater pass low-flow, (3) lube-oil filter ΔP high on a spare pump. Order them.
    Answer: (2) heater pass low-flow first — tube overheating/rupture risk in minutes; then (1) tank level — plan the change of rundown; then (3) spare pump filter — schedule maintenance.

    📋 Operating Procedures

    Operating Procedures & Management of Change

    documentationtechnical documentationprocess safety
    Definition
    Operating procedures are approved, written step-by-step instructions for operating a unit safely in every phase. Management of Change (MOC) is the formal process for reviewing any change to equipment, chemicals, procedures or operating limits before it is made.
    Why it matters
    Many major refinery incidents happened during non-routine operations (startup, shutdown) when procedures were not followed or were out of date. Process safety regulations (e.g. OSHA PSM 1910.119) require written procedures and MOC.
    Explanation
    Procedures should state who does each step, what to check, the limits and the consequences of deviation. Critical steps have sign-off. Any change not “replacement in kind” needs MOC: hazard review, update of P&IDs and procedures, training, and pre-startup review.
    Types
    Normal operation, initial/normal startup, normal shutdown, emergency shutdown, temporary operations, emergency operating procedures, safe work practices; checklists and work instructions.
    Components
    Purpose and scope, safety precautions, operating limits and consequences, step list with sign-offs, references (P&IDs), revision control, approval.
    Process / workflow
    Draft (by operators + engineers) → technical and safety review → approve → train → use in the field with sign-offs → review periodically (and after incidents/MOCs).
    Refinery example
    An operator wants to route a new slop stream to the crude tank. It is a change in feed composition → MOC: engineer checks corrosion and desalter impact, HSE checks emissions, procedure and P&ID updated, crew trained before it starts.
    Common mistakes
    Working from memory; “temporary” changes without MOC that become permanent; procedures that do not match the field.
    Best practices
    Use the procedure in hand for non-routine tasks; report mismatches; close MOCs only after documents and training are complete.
    🧪 Practice activity: MOC or not?
    Which need MOC? (a) Replacing a pump seal with an identical part. (b) Raising a heater outlet temperature limit by 10 °C. (c) Installing a temporary hose bypass around a control valve.
    Answer: (a) No — replacement in kind. (b) Yes — change of operating limit. (c) Yes — temporary change to equipment/piping.

    🔐 Permit to Work & Energy Isolation

    Permit to Work (PTW) & Isolation

    safetywork allocationprocess safety
    Definition
    A Permit to Work is a written authorisation system that controls non-routine and hazardous work. Energy isolation separates equipment from all energy sources (process fluid, pressure, electricity, steam) before work.
    Why it matters
    Operations hands equipment to maintenance. If isolation or preparation is wrong, people are exposed to hydrocarbons, H2S, pressure or electricity.
    Explanation
    Isolation hierarchy: positive isolation (spades/blinds or removed spool) → double block and bleed → single valve (low-risk only). Electrical isolation by LOTO at the switchgear. Equipment is then drained, depressured, flushed, steamed or purged, and gas tested before the permit is issued.
    Types
    Hot work, cold work, confined space entry, electrical, excavation, radiography, working at height, line-break permits.
    Components
    Permit form, JSA/risk assessment, isolation certificate and blind list, gas test record, LOTO locks and tags, signatures of issuer, performer and area authority, hand-back section.
    Process / workflow
    Plan job → JSA → prepare equipment (isolate, drain, purge) → gas test → issue permit at the job site → toolbox talk → work → periodic gas re-test → complete → hand back → remove isolations in order → return to service.
    Refinery example
    A pump is handed over for seal replacement: motor locked out, suction and discharge valves closed and locked, casing drained to the closed drain, flushed, vented, gas tested (0% LEL, 0 ppm H2S) — then the permit is issued.
    Common mistakes
    Issuing permits from the control room without visiting the site; missing a small line (seal flush, drain); removing isolations before all permits on that system are closed.
    Best practices
    Verify isolations physically (“try” the start button after LOTO); use blind lists with numbered blinds; cross-reference permits on the same equipment.
    🧪 Practice activity: Isolation choice
    A vessel that contained sour gas will have people entering it. What isolation level is required?
    Answer: Positive isolation — blinds/spades on every connected line (or spools removed), plus electrical isolation of internals/mixers, purge, ventilation, gas test and a confined-space entry permit with a standby person.

    🔬 Quality & Inspection Interfaces

    Product Quality & Equipment Inspection Interfaces

    inspectionquality plansRCACAPAchecklists
    Definition
    Operations works with two quality-related functions: the laboratory (product and process stream quality) and inspection (mechanical integrity of vessels, piping, tanks and heaters).
    Why it matters
    Off-spec products cost money and reputation; undetected corrosion causes loss-of-containment incidents. Operations controls the conditions that decide both.
    Explanation
    • Lab interface: sampling schedule, LIMS results, product certificates, off-spec notification, retests. The Refinery Operations Quality Engineer owns quality plans, investigates off-spec events with RCA and drives CAPA.
    • Inspection interface: inspection plans to codes such as API 510 (vessels), API 570 (piping), API 653 (tanks), API 573/530 (heaters); corrosion monitoring (coupons, probes, thickness measurements); IOW exceedance reporting. The Refinery Operations Inspector uses checklists, drawings/specifications and measurements and reports findings.
    Types
    Routine samples, spot samples, certification samples; on-stream inspection, turnaround inspection, risk-based inspection (RBI).
    Components
    Sampling points and procedures, LIMS, spec sheets, off-spec procedure, inspection test plans, thickness monitoring locations (TMLs), IOW register, NCR/CAPA log.
    Process / workflow
    Off-spec: detect (lab/analyser) → contain (divert to slop/off-spec tank) → correct (adjust unit) → confirm (resample) → investigate (RCA) → CAPA → close.
    Refinery example
    Jet fuel fails the copper-strip corrosion test (indicates active sulfur). Operations stops rundown to the certified tank, checks the Merox/caustic treater, and the quality engineer runs an RCA — spent caustic was not changed on schedule; CAPA adds the change-out to the CMMS PM plan.
    Common mistakes
    Taking a sample from a dead leg; not flushing the sample point; continuing rundown to a certified tank while waiting for a retest; not reporting IOW exceedances to inspection.
    Best practices
    Follow sampling procedures exactly; divert first, investigate second; share IOW exceedance data with inspection monthly.
    🧪 Practice activity: Contain first
    Diesel flash point result is below spec while rundown continues to a 30,000 m3 certified tank. First action?
    Answer: Divert rundown away from the certified tank (to off-spec/slop or an intermediate tank) immediately, then correct the unit (more stripping steam) and resample.

    🔧 Maintenance Interfaces

    Operations–Maintenance Interface

    preventive maintenanceschedulingExcel/ERPcross-team coordination
    Definition
    The structured way operations requests, prepares, hands over, accepts back and tests equipment that maintenance works on.
    Why it matters
    Poor interfaces cause delays, rework and incidents (equipment returned without leak test, wrong pump worked on). Good interfaces raise availability.
    Explanation
    Operations raises a notification/work request in the CMMS/ERP (e.g. SAP PM, Maximo) with clear symptoms and a priority based on risk (safety, environment, production). The Refinery Operations Planner plans and schedules work, resources and spares. Operations prepares equipment and issues the permit. After work, a post-maintenance test (run test, leak test, stroke test of a valve) confirms readiness.
    Types
    Breakdown (corrective), preventive (time-based), predictive (condition-based), operator-driven care (lubrication, cleaning, tightening), turnaround work.
    Components
    Work request, priority matrix, weekly schedule, permit, isolation, job card, hand-back form, test record, CMMS history.
    Process / workflow
    Identify → notify → prioritise → plan → schedule → prepare & isolate → execute → hand back → test → close notification with findings.
    Refinery example
    A control valve hunts. Notification: “FV-205 cycling ±15%, positioner suspected, unit stable on bypass flow, priority 2.” The instrument technician replaces the positioner; operations confirms stroke test before putting the loop back in auto.
    Common mistakes
    Vague notifications (“pump bad”); everything marked as top priority; closing the work order without recording what was found.
    Best practices
    Write symptom + location + risk + operating impact; attend the daily maintenance meeting; record failure codes for reliability analysis.
    🧪 Practice activity: Write a notification
    Write a good notification for a leaking gland on a kerosene pump P-302B (spare).
    Model: “P-302B (spare, kerosene rundown) — mechanical seal leaking ~10 drops/min at outboard side. Pump isolated and drained; P-302A running normally. No spare available if A trips — priority 2, request repair within 72 h.”

    🦺 HSE Requirements

    Health, Safety & Environment in Refinery Operations

    process safetysafetyreporting
    Definition
    HSE requirements are the legal, corporate and site rules that protect people, assets and the environment during refinery operation.
    Why it matters
    Refineries are major-hazard sites regulated under process safety laws (e.g. OSHA PSM in the USA, Seveso/COMAH in Europe, PNGRB/OISD standards in India). Breaches can mean injuries, deaths, shutdown orders and loss of licence.
    Explanation
    • Health: H2S, benzene, noise, heat stress, chemical handling (caustic, acids, amine).
    • Safety: PTW, LOTO, confined space, hot work, working at height, PPE, Stop Work Authority.
    • Environment: flaring minimisation, SO2/NOx emissions, fugitive emissions (LDAR), effluent quality, spills, waste (spent catalyst, oily sludge).
    • Process Safety Management elements: process safety information, PHA/HAZOP, operating procedures, training, contractor management, PSSR, mechanical integrity, hot work, MOC, incident investigation, emergency planning, compliance audits.
    Types
    Occupational safety, process safety, occupational health, environmental compliance.
    Components
    Gas detectors and personal monitors, fire and gas systems, emission monitors (CEMS), effluent treatment, incident reporting system, audits, training records.
    Process / workflow
    Identify hazards → assess risk → control (eliminate, substitute, engineer, administrate, PPE) → monitor → report incidents and near misses → learn.
    Refinery example
    A flare is smoking during an upset. Operations increases steam to the flare tip to improve combustion, notifies HSE (possible permit exceedance) and works to reduce the relief load at its source.
    Common mistakes
    Treating “no injuries” as proof of good process safety; not reporting near misses; draining hydrocarbons to open drains.
    Best practices
    Report every near miss; carry personal H2S monitors; use closed sampling and closed draining; minimise flaring as a daily KPI.
    🧪 Practice activity: Hierarchy of controls
    Benzene exposure during gasoline sampling — give one control at each level from engineering to PPE.
    Answer: Engineering: closed-loop sampling system; Administrative: sampling procedure, limited duration, stand upwind; PPE: respirator where required, gloves, goggles.

    Optimization, Troubleshooting & Performance

    Move from running a unit to improving it: push constraints safely, troubleshoot systematically, handle abnormal situations, save energy, raise reliability and read KPIs and trends like an analyst.

    🎯 Process Optimization

    Process Optimization

    advanced technical analysiscalculationsdata analysis
    Definition
    Process optimization is adjusting operating conditions to maximise profit (or another objective) while staying inside every safety, equipment, environmental and quality constraint.
    Why it matters
    Small changes multiplied by large volumes are worth a lot: 0.5% more diesel yield on 150,000 bpd is 750 bpd of higher-value product, every day.
    Explanation
    The planning LP (linear programming) model sets monthly targets (crude slate, unit rates, product mix). Operations then optimises day-to-day by pushing units toward their active constraints — the limit actually stopping more profit (heater firing, compressor capacity, column flooding, product spec, tank space). Optimising beyond a real constraint only moves the problem elsewhere.
    Types
    Throughput maximisation, yield optimisation (cut points, severity), energy optimisation, quality giveaway reduction, catalyst life optimisation, hydrogen network optimisation.
    Components
    LP model and planning targets, unit constraint list, heat and material balance, APC, lab data, economics (product price differences).
    Process / workflow
    Identify objective → list constraints → find the active constraint → test a change in small steps → measure result (yields, lab, energy) → standardise or roll back → feed back to planning.
    Refinery example
    Diesel is worth more than fuel oil. Raising the CDU diesel/AGO cut point recovers diesel from residue until diesel 95% point approaches the spec limit — at that point the product spec becomes the active constraint, and the engineer stops there.
    Common mistakes
    Optimising one unit while hurting the refinery (e.g. maximising FCC conversion when the gas plant is full); eroding safety margins to gain throughput.
    Best practices
    Think refinery-wide; never trade away IOW or safety margins; document and share results with planning.
    🧪 Practice activity: Find the active constraint
    CDU rate is limited. Heater firing is at 85%, column ΔP is 95% of flood, overhead condenser has spare duty. Which is the active constraint?
    Answer: Column hydraulics (95% of flood). Raising rate further would flood the column before the heater limit is reached.

    🧰 Process Troubleshooting

    Systematic Troubleshooting

    troubleshootingproblem-solvingroot cause analysisP&ID
    Definition
    Troubleshooting is the structured process of finding the cause of a process or equipment problem and correcting it, using evidence rather than guesswork.
    Why it matters
    Guess-and-change troubleshooting makes upsets worse and hides the true cause. A disciplined method fixes problems faster and safely.
    Explanation
    Start with what changed (feed, rate, weather, maintenance, set points). Check whether the measurement is real (cross-check instruments, field gauge). Use the heat and material balance and the P&ID to form hypotheses, then test them one at a time, from the easiest and most likely to the hardest.
    Types
    Process (separation, reaction), equipment (pump, compressor, exchanger), instrument/control, product quality, hydraulics.
    Components
    Trends, alarm and event log, shift log, lab data, P&ID, design data sheets, field observation, maintenance history.
    Process / workflow
    1 Define the problem (what, where, when, how much) → 2 Make it safe/contain → 3 Gather data → 4 Verify instruments → 5 List hypotheses → 6 Test → 7 Correct → 8 Verify and document.
    Refinery example
    Naphtha stabiliser bottoms RVP is high. Data: reboiler outlet temperature dropped 6 °C since the steam header pressure fell. Root cause: LP steam header pressure low due to a turbine change. Fix the steam supply → RVP recovers.
    Common mistakes
    Changing several things at once; trusting a single instrument; stopping at the first plausible cause.
    Best practices
    Ask “what changed?”; test one hypothesis at a time; write a short troubleshooting report for repeat problems.
    🧪 Practice activity: Instrument or process?
    A drum level reads 50% steady for 6 hours on the DCS while inflow and outflow have both changed. What do you suspect?
    Answer: A frozen/failed level transmitter (a perfectly flat trend is suspicious). Check the field gauge glass and the second level instrument before trusting it.

    🚨 Abnormal Operating Conditions

    Abnormal Situation Management

    process safetytroubleshootingcommunication
    Definition
    An abnormal operating condition is any state where the process has moved outside its normal operating envelope but is not yet an emergency — the window where operator action can prevent an incident.
    Why it matters
    Industry studies of abnormal situation management show that unplanned upsets cost refineries several percent of capacity and are the precursor to most process safety incidents.
    Explanation
    Escalation path: normal → deviation → upset (abnormal) → emergency → incident. In the abnormal zone, the operator's goals in order are: protect people → protect the environment → protect equipment → protect production. If the process cannot be brought back within a defined time or approaches critical limits, move the unit to a safe state (reduced rate, hot standby, circulation, shutdown) rather than “hanging on”.
    Types
    Feed loss, utility failure, equipment trip, control failure, product off-spec, weather events (heavy rain, freezing), leaks.
    Components
    Alarm response manuals, emergency operating procedures, safe-state definitions, trip systems, communication plan.
    Process / workflow
    Recognise (alarm/trend) → Stabilise (reduce rate, take manual control where needed) → Diagnose → Correct → Communicate (supervisor, neighbouring units) → Recover step by step → Review.
    Refinery example
    Reflux pump trips on the CDU. Top temperature climbs and naphtha goes off-spec. The board operator starts the spare, meanwhile reduces heater firing and crude rate to limit overhead load, and informs the reformer/NHT board that naphtha quality will deviate.
    Common mistakes
    Trying to keep full rate during an upset; tunnel vision on one variable; silent upsets (not telling neighbouring units).
    Best practices
    Reduce rate early; use the alarm response manual; communicate early; practise upset scenarios on simulators.
    🧪 Practice activity: Safe state
    The hydrotreater feed pump trips and the spare will not start. What is the safe state?
    Answer: Cut heater firing to avoid overheating stagnant tubes, keep recycle gas circulating to cool the reactors, maintain pressure, route make-up per procedure, and hold the unit in hot circulation until feed is restored — or follow the shutdown procedure if it cannot be.

    🧠 Advanced Process Control

    Process Control Performance & APC

    engineeringdata analysisdesign review
    Definition
    Advanced Process Control (APC), usually Model Predictive Control (MPC), coordinates many controllers at once using a process model to push the unit to its economic optimum within constraints.
    Why it matters
    APC reduces variability, and lower variability lets the unit run closer to limits safely — typically worth a few % of unit margin.
    Explanation
    APC sits above regulatory (PID) control and writes their setpoints. It only works if the base layer is healthy: well-tuned loops in auto/cascade, valves not saturated, reliable analysers. Watch controller performance KPIs: % time in normal mode, valve output at 0/100%, oscillation.
    Types
    Regulatory PID, feed-forward, cascade, inferential (soft-sensor) control, MPC, real-time optimisation (RTO).
    Components
    Base-layer loops, MPC application, models, inferentials, operator interface with limits, APC service factor KPI.
    Process / workflow
    Fix base layer → build model (step tests) → commission APC → operators set high/low limits → monitor service factor and benefits.
    Refinery example
    CDU APC service factor dropped from 95% to 60% because the diesel analyser kept failing. Fixing the analyser restored APC and recovered the diesel yield gain.
    Common mistakes
    Operators clamping APC limits tight “to be safe” (APC can no longer optimise); ignoring loops in manual.
    Best practices
    Review controller and APC KPIs weekly; train operators on what APC is doing and why.
    🧪 Practice activity: Base layer check
    40 of 300 loops on a unit are in manual. Why does that matter for APC and safety?
    Answer: APC cannot move manual loops, so it loses its handles and benefits; manual loops also do not respond to disturbances, increasing upset risk and operator workload.

    ♻️ Energy Efficiency

    Refinery Energy Efficiency

    calculationsKPI trackingtrend analysis
    Definition
    Energy efficiency is producing the same products with less fuel, steam and power. A common benchmark is the Energy Intensity Index (EII), comparing actual energy use with a standard for the refinery's configuration.
    Why it matters
    Energy is often the largest operating cost after crude, and the largest source of refinery CO2 emissions.
    Explanation
    Big levers: heater excess O2 (every extra % of O2 wastes fuel), preheat-train fouling, steam traps and leaks, stripping-steam rates, reflux ratios above what quality needs, flaring, idle equipment running, and steam letdown instead of turbine use.
    Types
    Fuel efficiency, steam balance, power efficiency, heat integration (pinch), flare minimisation.
    Components
    O2 analysers, fuel and steam meters, steam trap surveys, energy dashboard, exchanger cleaning plan.
    Process / workflow
    Measure → benchmark against target → find losses → fix (operating change or maintenance) → verify savings → sustain via KPI.
    Refinery example
    Crude heater runs at 6% O2. Tuning burners and dampers to 3% O2 reduces fuel firing by roughly 2–3% — a significant saving on a large heater, with lower CO2.
    Common mistakes
    Running excess reflux/steam “for safety margin” forever; ignoring steam leaks as normal; cutting O2 so low that CO and unburnt fuel appear.
    Best practices
    Daily energy KPI review; target O2 bands with CO monitoring; routine steam trap surveys.
    🧪 Practice activity: Energy hunt
    List three operating (no-cost) energy savings on a CDU.
    Answer: Reduce heater excess O2 to target; optimise stripping steam to the minimum needed for flash point; optimise pumparound duties to maximise preheat recovery; switch off unnecessary air-cooler fans in cold weather.

    🛠️ Reliability & Equipment Performance

    Reliability & Equipment Performance

    root cause analysispreventive maintenanceKPI tracking
    Definition
    Reliability is the probability that equipment performs its function without failure for a stated period. Equipment performance measures how well it does that function (efficiency, capacity, head, duty).
    Why it matters
    Unplanned downtime is the most expensive loss in a refinery, and many leaks start with failing equipment (seals, bearings, corroded piping).
    Explanation
    Key measures: MTBF (mean time between failures), MTTR (mean time to repair), availability = MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR). Operations improves reliability by running equipment inside its design envelope, operator care rounds, early reporting, and taking part in bad-actor reviews and RCAs.
    Types
    Reactive, preventive, predictive (vibration, oil analysis, thermography, ultrasonic), reliability-centred maintenance (RCM), operator-driven reliability.
    Components
    CMMS failure history, condition monitoring data, bad-actor list, RCA reports, spare parts, performance curves.
    Process / workflow
    Collect failures → rank bad actors (Pareto) → RCA on top items → implement CAPA → monitor MTBF.
    Refinery example
    A sour water pump had 6 seal failures in a year. RCA: running far left of its best efficiency point at low flow, causing vibration. Solution: smaller impeller and minimum-flow line — MTBF rose from 2 to 18 months.
    Common mistakes
    Repairing the same failure repeatedly without RCA; running pumps far from best efficiency point; skipping operator care rounds.
    Best practices
    Track bad actors monthly; involve operators in RCA; record failure modes accurately in the CMMS.
    🧪 Practice activity: Availability
    A compressor has MTBF 2,000 h and MTTR 50 h. What is its availability?
    Answer: 2,000 ÷ 2,050 = 97.6%.

    Understand how refineries prevent major accidents, how HAZOP and risk assessment work, and how operations coordinates shutdowns, turnarounds, startups and emergencies.

    🛡️ Process Safety

    Process Safety Management

    process safetyleadershipreporting
    Definition
    Process safety is the management of hazards from processes and equipment to prevent loss of primary containment (LOPC) — releases of hazardous material or energy that can lead to fires, explosions and toxic exposures.
    Why it matters
    Process safety incidents are low-frequency but high-consequence. A refinery can have an excellent personal-injury record and still be heading for a major accident.
    Explanation
    Process safety differs from occupational safety: it focuses on containment, design limits, safeguards and systems. Performance is measured with lagging indicators (Tier 1/Tier 2 LOPC events per API RP 754) and leading indicators (overdue inspections, safety-critical PM completion, bypassed safeguards, open MOCs, IOW exceedances).
    Types
    Tier 1 (most severe LOPC), Tier 2, Tier 3 (challenges to safety systems, e.g. relief valve lifts, SIS demands), Tier 4 (operating discipline and management system performance).
    Components
    Process safety information, PHA/HAZOP, procedures, training, mechanical integrity, MOC, PSSR, hot work, contractor management, incident investigation, emergency planning, audits, safety culture.
    Process / workflow
    Know the hazards → design and maintain barriers → operate inside limits → monitor barrier health → learn from incidents and weak signals.
    Refinery example
    BP Texas City (2005): during an isomerization unit startup, a raffinate splitter tower was overfilled; liquid released from a blowdown stack ignited, killing 15 people. Findings included level instruments that did not work, procedures not followed during startup, trailers too close to the unit and a focus on personal rather than process safety.
    Common mistakes
    Measuring only injury rates; normalising deviations (“it has always run like this”); accepting long-term bypassed safeguards.
    Best practices
    Track leading indicators monthly; treat every relief valve lift and SIS demand as a learning event; keep the override register short and time-limited.
    🧪 Practice activity: Leading or lagging?
    Classify: (a) 3 overdue PSV tests, (b) a hydrocarbon release causing a fire, (c) 12 open MOCs past due date, (d) an SIS trip on high pressure.
    Answer: (a) leading, (b) lagging (Tier 1), (c) leading, (d) Tier 3 — a challenge to a safety system (a warning signal).

    🔎 HAZOP Concepts

    HAZOP (Hazard and Operability Study)

    P&IDprocess safetydesign review
    Definition
    A HAZOP is a structured, team-based review of a process (using P&IDs) that applies guidewords to process parameters to find deviations, their causes and consequences, and whether safeguards are adequate.
    Why it matters
    It is the main Process Hazard Analysis (PHA) method for refinery units, required for new designs, major changes and periodic revalidation (commonly every 5 years).
    Explanation
    The P&ID is split into nodes. For each node: guideword + parameter = deviation (e.g. NO + FLOW = no flow) → causes → consequences (assuming safeguards fail) → existing safeguards → risk ranking → recommendations. Operators bring essential knowledge of how the plant really runs.
    Types
    Guidewords: NO/NOT, MORE, LESS, REVERSE, AS WELL AS, PART OF, OTHER THAN (plus EARLY/LATE, BEFORE/AFTER for batch steps). Related studies: What-If, checklist, FMEA, LOPA.
    Components
    Facilitator, scribe, process engineer, operations representative, instrument/control engineer, mechanical/inspection engineer, HSE; P&IDs, operating limits, cause-and-effect charts.
    Process / workflow
    Define scope & nodes → apply guidewords → record worksheet → rank risk → issue recommendations → track actions to closure (owner + due date).

    Common mistakes
    Taking credit for safeguards that are not maintained; no operations representative in the team; recommendations never closed.

    ⚖️ Risk Assessment & Layers of Protection

    Figure 6.1 · Layers of protection — each independent layer reduces the chance a cause becomes an incident

    Risk Assessment

    process safetysafetyadvanced technical analysis
    Definition
    Risk assessment identifies hazards, estimates risk as a combination of likelihood and severity, and decides whether existing controls reduce risk to an acceptable level (often “ALARP” — as low as reasonably practicable).
    Why it matters
    It decides which tasks need extra controls, which safeguards are safety-critical, and whether to proceed with non-routine operations.
    Explanation
    Risk matrix (e.g. 5×5) ranks risk. Bow-tie shows threats on the left, consequences on the right and barriers on both sides of a top event (e.g. loss of containment). LOPA (Layer of Protection Analysis) counts only independent protection layers (IPLs) that are effective, independent and auditable, to check whether risk is reduced enough or a SIS is needed.
    Types
    Task-level: JSA/JHA, toolbox risk assessment; process-level: HAZOP, What-If, LOPA, bow-tie, QRA (quantitative risk assessment); change-level: MOC risk review.
    Components
    Hazard list, risk matrix, barrier list, IPL credits, action plan, approval.
    Process / workflow
    Identify hazard → identify causes and consequences → list barriers → estimate residual risk → add controls if not tolerable → approve → monitor barrier health.
    Refinery example
    Request to bypass a compressor high-vibration trip for 4 hours while a faulty probe is replaced. Risk assessment: alternative protection (portable vibration monitoring, operator stationed locally, second probe still active), time limit, supervisor and engineer approval, logged in the override register.
    Common mistakes
    Counting the same operator twice as two layers; treating a risk assessment as paperwork done after the job is planned; not re-assessing when conditions change.
    Best practices
    Assess before planning is fixed; involve those who will do the work; re-assess if the scope, weather or conditions change.
    🧪 Practice activity: Rank the risk
    Using a 5×5 matrix: hot work near a tank farm drain — likelihood 2 (unlikely), severity 5 (fatality/major fire). Score and decision?
    Answer: 2 × 5 = 10 — typically “high/medium-high”: proceed only with added controls (cover drains, continuous gas monitoring, fire watch, fire equipment ready) and senior approval.

    ⏹️ Shutdown Coordination

    Unit Shutdown

    planningcoordinationcross-team coordinationprocess safety
    Definition
    A shutdown brings a unit from normal operation to a safe, cold, depressured, hydrocarbon-free state (planned), or to a safe state as quickly as possible (emergency).
    Why it matters
    Shutdowns are non-routine, high-risk operations: changing temperatures and pressures, many people, many line-ups. They affect neighbouring units, tanks and utilities.
    Explanation
    Typical planned sequence: reduce feed → cut heater firing → stop feed → cool down at controlled rates → pump out liquids to slop/tanks → depressure to flare → steam out / nitrogen purge → positive isolation with blinds → gas test → hand over to maintenance. Catalyst units may need special steps (hydrogen strip, cooling under recycle gas, protection against pyrophoric sulfides).
    Types
    Planned (normal) shutdown, emergency shutdown (ESD), partial shutdown (one section), hot standby/circulation.
    Components
    Shutdown procedure and checklist, blind list, flare load plan, slop tank space, utility plan (steam/N2), communication plan with neighbouring units and offsites.
    Process / workflow
    Pre-shutdown meeting → confirm tanks/flare/N2 availability → execute step-by-step with sign-offs → communicate at each milestone → final isolation and hand-over certificate.
    Refinery example
    Before shutting down the CDU, the Coordinator confirms with offsites that intermediate tanks have space for downstream units to keep running, with utilities that extra N2 is available, and with HSE that flare loads stay within permit.
    Common mistakes
    Cooling down too fast (flange leaks, thermal stress); pumping hot oil to tanks with water (boil-over); forgetting dead legs during steam-out; inadequate communication with neighbours.
    Best practices
    Use checklists with sign-offs; hold milestone briefings; verify every blind against the numbered blind list.
    🧪 Practice activity: Order the steps
    Put in order: depressure, steam out, stop feed, positive isolation, reduce feed, gas test, pump out.
    Answer: Reduce feed → stop feed → pump out → depressure → steam out → positive isolation → gas test.

    🏗️ Turnaround Coordination

    Turnaround (TA) Management

    planningschedulingresource allocationproject execution
    Definition
    A turnaround is a planned, periodic shutdown (typically every 3–6 years per unit) to inspect, repair, replace catalyst and install upgrades that cannot be done on-stream.
    Why it matters
    A TA can cost tens of millions and every extra day of downtime costs lost margin. Most of the work is decided by inspection findings and integrity needs.
    Explanation
    Phases: scope development and freeze (often 12–18 months ahead) → detailed planning (work packs, critical path, materials, contractors) → pre-TA work → shutdown → execution (24/7) → startup → post-TA review / lessons learned. Operations owns shutdown, isolation, permits, hand-over/hand-back and startup — the start and end of the critical path.
    Types
    Full refinery TA, unit TA, short planned outage (“pit stop”), catalyst change-out.
    Components
    Worklist, critical path schedule, blind list, permit plan, contractor plan, spares/long-lead items, TA KPIs (safety, schedule, cost, scope growth).
    Process / workflow
    Operations input: equipment problems list → scope review → shutdown/startup procedures → blind lists → permit office staffing → hand-over certificates → startup readiness (PSSR).
    Refinery example
    Scope growth: inspection finds heavy corrosion on overhead piping during the TA. Emergent work is assessed against the critical path; long-lead pipe was pre-ordered after the last thickness survey, so the extension is only one day.
    Common mistakes
    Late scope additions; poor blind management; rushing startup to recover schedule.
    Best practices
    Freeze scope on time; give operations a dedicated TA team; never compress startup steps to recover schedule.
    🧪 Practice activity: Who owns what?
    In a TA, which role from the source list typically builds the detailed schedule and tracks progress in Excel/ERP, and which supervises crews and permits?
    Answer: Refinery Operations Planner (planning, scheduling, resource allocation, progress tracking, Excel/ERP); Refinery Operations Supervisor (team supervision, work allocation, safety, quality, progress monitoring).

    ▶️ Startup & Readiness

    Startup Readiness & PSSR

    checklistsinspectionprocess safety
    Definition
    Startup readiness confirms that a unit is mechanically complete, safe and ready for hydrocarbons. A Pre-Startup Safety Review (PSSR) is the formal check before introducing hazardous material after new construction, modification or a turnaround.
    Why it matters
    Startups are among the most hazardous periods: incidents cluster during startup and shutdown because conditions are non-steady and many systems are disturbed.
    Explanation
    Readiness checks: all work permits closed, all blinds removed/installed per blind list, relief valves installed and lined up, instruments and trips function-tested, leak/tightness test done, equipment air-freed by N2 purge (O2 below the site limit) before hydrocarbon entry, procedures updated for MOCs, crew trained. Then: establish utilities → pressurise and leak test → circulate → heat up at controlled rate → introduce feed → bring products on-spec.
    Types
    Initial startup (new unit), normal startup (after TA/outage), restart after trip.
    Components
    PSSR checklist, blind list, permit log, function test records, punch list (A items must be closed before startup), startup procedure.
    Process / workflow
    Walkdown → punch list → close A-items → PSSR sign-off by team → leak test → purge → startup per procedure → stabilise → handover to normal operation.
    Refinery example
    During PSSR walkdown, a blind remains installed on a PSV inlet (left from the TA). Starting up would have left the column without overpressure protection. The blind list reconciliation catches it.
    Common mistakes
    Signing PSSR without a walkdown; skipping oxygen checks; starting up with trips bypassed “temporarily”.
    Best practices
    Physical walkdown by operations; reconcile every blind; restart after a trip only after cause is known and corrected.
    🧪 Practice activity: Readiness gap
    A heater is ready to light but the BMS purge timer was jumped out “to save time”. Proceed?
    Answer: No. The purge removes any fuel in the firebox; bypassing it risks a firebox explosion. Restore the BMS and complete the purge.

    🚒 Emergency Response

    Refinery Emergency Response

    safetycommunicationleadershipprocess safety
    Definition
    Emergency response is the planned, practised action taken to protect people, environment and assets when an incident occurs — fire, gas/toxic release, explosion, major utility failure.
    Why it matters
    The first minutes decide the outcome. Clear roles and practised actions prevent escalation.
    Explanation

    Expertise: Integrated Operations, Leadership & Decisions

    Think like a senior engineer or operations leader: see the refinery as one connected system, troubleshoot across units, make sound decisions under pressure, protect asset integrity and lead process safety.

    🕸️ Integrated Refinery Operations

    Figure 7.1 · The four refinery-wide balances an expert keeps in view

    Integrated (Refinery-wide) Operations

    advanced technical analysisleadershipprocess operations
    Definition
    Integrated operations means managing all units, utilities and offsites as one system, balancing hydrogen, sulfur, fuel gas, steam, intermediate tankage and product demand.
    Why it matters
    Local optimisation often creates refinery-wide losses. Experts prevent one unit's decision from becoming another unit's crisis.
    Explanation
    • Hydrogen: reformer and hydrogen plant supply; hydrotreaters/hydrocracker consume. Reformer outage → hydrotreaters must cut rate or severity.
    • Sulfur: sour crude raises H2S to amine and SRU; SRU capacity can cap crude rate.
    • Fuel gas: too much (cracking units) → flaring; too little → import natural gas/LPG.
    • Tankage: intermediate tanks decouple units; when full, upstream units must slow down.
    Types
    Daily production planning, weekly scheduling, monthly LP planning, real-time coordination via the shift superintendent.
    Components
    Refinery-wide material balance, LP model, scheduling tool, tank inventory system, utility balance, daily production meeting.
    Process / workflow
    Monitor balances → forecast constraints (24–72 h) → agree actions across units → communicate → verify.
    Refinery example
    One SRU train trips. Sulfur capacity drops 50%. Expert response: cut sour crude rate, shift to sweeter crude tank, reduce hydrotreater severity within spec, keep H2S out of fuel gas, inform environmental team — rather than letting H2S go to flare.
    Common mistakes
    Treating each unit as independent; ignoring tank inventories until they are critical.
    Best practices
    Daily review of all four balances; maintain a “what if unit X trips” playbook.
    🧪 Practice activity: Ripple effect
    The catalytic reformer trips. List three effects elsewhere in the refinery.
    Answer: Hydrogen supply falls (hydrotreaters must cut rate/severity, diesel sulfur at risk); gasoline octane pool drops (blending must change); naphtha has nowhere to go (intermediate tank fills, CDU may need to adjust naphtha cut).

    🧩 Advanced Troubleshooting

    Multi-unit & Complex Troubleshooting

    troubleshootingdesign reviewroot cause analysiscalculations
    Definition
    Advanced troubleshooting solves problems whose cause is distant from the symptom — in another unit, in a slow degradation, or in an interaction between control loops, equipment and chemistry.
    Why it matters
    The hardest and most expensive problems (repeat upsets, slow capacity loss, chronic off-spec) are rarely solved by local adjustments.
    Explanation
    Experts use: heat and material balance closure, hydraulic calculations (pump curves, tray loading), comparison with design and test-run data, fault-tree / cause-and-effect analysis, gamma scans for columns, thermography, and chemistry (corrosion products, salt analysis). They look for the time correlation between the start of the symptom and any change.
    Types
    Capacity loss, recurring trips, chronic quality issues, corrosion/fouling problems, control interaction.
    Components
    Long-term historian data, design data sheets, simulation model, lab archives, specialist tools (gamma scan, ultrasonic flow), vendor support.
    Process / workflow
    Frame the problem with data → check balance closure → build hypotheses across units → test with field measurements or simulation → confirm root cause → implement and verify.
    Refinery example
    FCC wet gas compressor repeatedly limits capacity in summer. Analysis: main fractionator overhead condenser is fouled on the cooling-water side, cooling tower approach worsened in summer → higher overhead temperature → more uncondensed gas. Fix: clean condenser, improve cooling-water treatment — compressor limit disappears.
    Common mistakes
    Accepting “it's always like this in summer”; replacing equipment without proving the cause.
    Best practices
    Use data over months, not hours; involve specialists early; document the causal chain.
    🧪 Practice activity: Causal chain
    CDU overhead exchangers keep failing with corrosion. Build a causal chain back to its likely source.
    Answer: Exchanger corrosion ← HCl in overhead ← chlorides hydrolysing in heater ← high desalted crude salt ← poor desalting (emulsion, low wash water) or organic chlorides in a new crude/slop. Check desalter performance and crude chloride analysis.

    🧭 Operational Decision Making

    Operational Decision Making Under Pressure

    leadershipproblem-solvingprocess safety
    Definition
    Operational decision making is choosing a course of action — continue, adjust, reduce, or shut down — when information is incomplete and time is limited.
    Why it matters
    Investigations of major incidents often find a moment where the safe decision (stop, shut down) was available but production pressure won.
    Explanation
    Use a fixed priority order: people → environment → asset → production/reputation. Apply conservative decision making: when in doubt, move to the safer state. Define in advance “trigger points” — limits or times at which you will reduce rate or shut down, so the decision is not made under emotional pressure.
    Types
    Routine (within procedure), tactical (rate/severity changes), strategic (shutdown, crude change), emergency.
    Components
    Facts (data), options, risks of each option, consequences, authority level, communication plan.
    Process / workflow
    Assess situation → list options (including “stop”) → evaluate risk of each → decide at the right authority level → communicate → monitor → re-evaluate at trigger points.
    Refinery example
    A small but growing leak on a hot oil line flange, with a ship waiting for product. Options: keep running with monitoring, hot-bolt (live repair), or shut down the section. Expert decision: leak growing + above auto-ignition temperature → isolate and shut down the section; the ship delay is a commercial loss, not a fatality risk.
    Common mistakes
    “One more hour” thinking; decisions made by the wrong level; no defined trigger points.
    Best practices
    Agree trigger points in the shift plan; support (never punish) operators who stop the process for safety.
    🧪 Practice activity: Set trigger points
    A heater TMT is creeping up 2 °C/day toward its IOW limit of 650 °C (now 630 °C). Define trigger points.
    Answer: e.g. at 635 °C — engineer review and reduce firing/rate; at 645 °C — further rate cut and plan decoking/spalling; at 650 °C (critical IOW) — immediate reduction to bring below limit or controlled shutdown per procedure.

    🏛️ Reliability & Asset Integrity

    Asset Integrity Management

    inspectionCMMSdesign reviewprocess safety
    Definition
    Asset integrity is the ability of equipment to perform its function safely and effectively over its life — the asset is designed, operated, inspected and maintained to keep hydrocarbons inside.
    Why it matters
    Corrosion, erosion, fatigue and creep cause many loss-of-containment events (e.g. the Chevron Richmond 2012 fire from sulfidation corrosion of a crude unit pipe).
    Explanation
    Corrosion loops group piping and equipment with the same damage mechanisms. IOWs set the operating limits that keep damage rates predictable. Risk-Based Inspection (RBI, API 580/581) focuses inspection on high-risk items. Fitness-for-service (API 579) decides if damaged equipment can continue to run. Operations' role: stay inside IOWs, report exceedances, protect safety-critical elements.
    Types
    Static equipment integrity (vessels, piping, tanks), rotating equipment reliability, instrumented safeguards integrity (SIS proof testing), structural integrity.
    Components
    Damage mechanism review, corrosion loops, IOW register, RBI plan, thickness monitoring, safety-critical element list, proof-test schedule, MOC.
    Process / workflow
    Identify damage mechanisms → set IOWs → inspect per RBI → assess findings → repair/replace or adjust operations → feed back into IOWs and RBI.
    Refinery example
    A crude switch raises TAN; naphthenic-acid corrosion risk in the hot sections increases. Integrity team adds temporary thickness monitoring locations and a TAN limit for the crude blend; operations blends to stay under the limit.
    Common mistakes
    Running outside IOWs without telling inspection; deferring safety-critical inspections; changing crude without a corrosion review.
    Best practices
    Review IOW exceedances monthly with inspection; treat safety-critical PM and proof tests as non-deferrable without risk assessment.
    🧪 Practice activity: IOW response
    Overhead pH (an IOW) was below its standard limit for 36 hours last week. What should operations do now?
    Answer: Record the exceedance, inform the corrosion/inspection engineer, review possible increased corrosion (extra thickness checks), find and fix the cause (neutraliser dosing, desalter), and include it in the monthly IOW review.

    🦁 Process Safety Leadership

    Process Safety Leadership & Culture

    leadershipteam supervisionprocess safety
    Definition
    Process safety leadership is the visible, consistent behaviour of supervisors, engineers and managers that makes safe operation the default — even when production pressure is high.
    Why it matters
    Culture decides what happens when nobody is watching: whether a bypass is reported, whether a procedure is followed at 3 a.m.
    Explanation
    Key ideas: normalisation of deviance (repeated small deviations become “normal”), chronic unease (healthy concern about what could go wrong), felt leadership (leaders in the field, asking questions), just culture (honest errors are learned from; reckless violations are not tolerated), Stop Work Authority backed by management.
    Types
    Leadership behaviours: field walks, safety conversations, recognition of good catches, challenging deviations, transparent reporting.
    Components
    Leading indicators, near-miss reporting rate, learning reviews, competency assurance, workload management, fatigue management.
    Process / workflow
    Set expectations → walk the plant → listen → act on weak signals → recognise → follow up visibly.
    Refinery example
    A supervisor notices a standing high-level alarm that operators “always ignore”. Instead of accepting it, they open a work order, assign the engineer to rationalise or fix the alarm, and talk with the crew about why standing alarms are dangerous.
    Common mistakes
    Rewarding throughput while ignoring how it was achieved; blaming individuals for system failures; leaders who only appear after incidents.
    Best practices
    Regular field walks; praise people who stop work; share lessons from other refineries' incidents.

    📉 Operational Risk Management

    Operational Risk Management

    advanced technical analysisreportingprocess safety
    Definition
    Operational risk management is the ongoing identification and control of the combined risk created by degraded equipment, bypassed safeguards, deferred maintenance, staffing gaps and simultaneous activities.
    Why it matters
    Individual degradations may each look acceptable, but together they can remove several layers of protection at once — the “Swiss cheese” holes line up.
    Explanation
    Tools: override/bypass register with risk assessment and expiry; deferred maintenance register; cumulative risk review per unit; SIMOPS (simultaneous operations) planning; barrier health dashboards.
    Types
    Barrier degradation, people risk (fatigue, competence, staffing), SIMOPS risk, commercial/operational risk.
    Components
    Risk register, barrier list, owners, review frequency, escalation thresholds.
    Process / workflow
    Log every degradation → assess individually → review cumulatively → apply compensating measures → escalate if threshold exceeded → remove degradation as priority.
    Refinery example
    On one unit: a gas detector out of service, a PSV on deferred test, a fire monitor isolated for repair and hot work planned nearby. Cumulative review stops the hot work until the gas detector and fire monitor are restored.
    Common mistakes
    Assessing each bypass alone; letting registers grow without expiry; no owner for closure.
    Best practices
    Weekly cumulative risk review by the senior engineer and supervisor; visible barrier dashboard in the control room.
    🧪 Practice activity: Cumulative review
    Why should a new bypass request be reviewed together with existing ones on the same unit?
    Answer: Because the risk depends on the remaining layers of protection. If other barriers on the same hazard are already degraded, the new bypass may leave no effective protection.

    Continuous Improvement in Refinery Operations

    data analysisRCACAPAExcel/Power BI
    Definition
    Continuous improvement is the systematic, ongoing effort to improve safety, reliability, efficiency and quality through small and large changes based on data.
    Why it matters
    Refinery margins are cyclical; the sites that survive downturns are those that continuously cut losses.
    Explanation
    Methods: PDCA, Six Sigma DMAIC (Define, Measure, Analyse, Improve, Control), Lean (remove waste — waiting, rework, excess inventory), loss accounting (gap between actual and potential margin, split by cause), lessons learned from incidents and TAs, benchmarking (e.g. Solomon studies).

    Improvement projects without baseline data; benefits never verified; changes made without MOC.

    Quantify losses in money; verify benefits after 3 months; standardise into procedures and training.

    🏭 Real-World Refinery Scenarios

    Scenario A · Crude change causes overhead corrosion spike
    Situation: After switching to a new opportunity crude, CDU overhead chlorides jump from 10 to 60 ppm and iron in overhead water triples.
    Expert response: Increase neutraliser and wash water within limits; check desalter salt; blend the new crude down with the regular crude; request crude organic chloride analysis; inform inspection to add thickness checks on the overhead system; put a crude-acceptance limit for chlorides into the crude evaluation procedure (MOC).
    Scenario B · Hydrogen shortage during reformer catalyst regeneration
    Situation: Reformer runs at low severity for regeneration; hydrogen supply drops 20%.
    Expert response: Prioritise hydrogen to units with the tightest product specs (diesel hydrotreater for 10 ppm S); reduce kerosene hydrotreater rate and store untreated kero in intermediate tankage; maximise hydrogen plant rate; plan the regeneration window with planning so that product liftings are protected.
    Scenario C · FCC afterburn and regenerator temperature rise
    Situation: Regenerator dilute-phase temperature rises sharply — afterburn (CO burning above the bed).
    Expert response: Reduce air rate / adjust CO promoter, check catalyst circulation and feed rate, follow FCC emergency procedure limits; protect cyclones and plenum from overheating; investigate whether a slide-valve or air-blower problem caused the imbalance.
    Scenario D · Flare overload during a site power dip
    Situation: A grid dip trips several motors; multiple units relieve to flare simultaneously, flare is smoking heavily.
    Expert response: Shift superintendent prioritises: stabilise the units relieving most (cut feed/firing), maximise flare steam, confirm flare KO drum level (liquid carry-over risk), communicate with HSE and authorities as required, then sequence restarts one unit at a time to avoid a second flare load.

    🧭 Interview: Basic & Fundamentals

    What does a refinery do, in simple terms?
    It converts crude oil into products like LPG, gasoline, jet, diesel and fuel oil by separating crude by boiling range, converting heavy molecules into lighter ones, removing impurities like sulfur, and blending components into products that meet specifications.
    What is the difference between sweet and sour crude?
    Sweet crude has low sulfur (below about 0.5 wt%); sour crude has more. Sour crude needs more hydrotreating, amine and sulfur recovery capacity, causes more corrosion and brings higher H2S exposure risk.
    Why is crude desalted before distillation?
    Salts, mainly chlorides, hydrolyse in the heater to form HCl, which corrodes the crude column overhead. Desalting also removes water and solids that foul exchangers and upset the column.
    How do you read a P&ID tag like FIC-205?
    F = flow, I = indicating, C = controller; 205 is the loop number. So FIC-205 is the flow indicating controller in loop 205, normally linked to transmitter FT-205 and control valve FV-205.
    What would you do if your H2S monitor alarms in the field?
    Stop work, move upwind/crosswind away from the source immediately, alert the control room and others nearby, and do not return without proper breathing apparatus and authorisation. Never rely on smell.

    🔧 Interview: Intermediate & Technical

    Why is a vacuum distillation unit needed?
    Atmospheric residue would crack and coke if heated enough to vaporise its gas oil at atmospheric pressure. Under vacuum, boiling points drop, so VGO is recovered at lower temperatures for the FCC or hydrocracker.
    How do you change over a centrifugal pump?
    Check the spare (lube, seal system, cooling, warm-up in hot service), open suction, vent/prime, start with discharge nearly closed, open discharge slowly while watching pressure and amps, then reduce and stop the running pump, and isolate it if handing over for maintenance.
    What causes column flooding and how do you recognise it?
    Excessive vapour or liquid traffic (high feed rate, high reflux, foaming, fouled trays). Signs: sharply rising differential pressure, unstable levels, heavy or dark distillate, loss of separation. Response: reduce rate or reflux and investigate.
    A product is off-spec. What are your first steps?
    Contain first: divert rundown away from the certified tank. Then correct the unit (e.g. stripping steam for flash point), resample to confirm, notify the quality engineer and planning, and record the event for RCA if it is significant or repeated.
    How do you prepare equipment for maintenance?
    Plan with a JSA, isolate (positive isolation where required, LOTO electrically), drain to closed system, depressure, flush/steam/purge, gas test, issue the permit at the job site, and verify isolation. After work: hand-back, leak/run test, remove isolations in order.

    🏆 Interview: Advanced, Expert & Behavioural

    Explain HAZOP and your role in one.
    HAZOP is a team review of P&IDs, node by node, applying guidewords (NO, MORE, LESS, REVERSE…) to parameters to find deviations, causes, consequences and safeguards, then making recommendations. As an operations representative I bring knowledge of how the plant actually runs, real failure modes and whether safeguards are really maintained.
    How do you decide whether to shut a unit down?
    Priority is people, environment, asset, then production. I compare the situation with procedure limits and pre-set trigger points; if the process cannot be brought back within limits in a defined time, or risk is rising, I move to a safe state — reduced rate, circulation or shutdown — and escalate to the right authority with facts and options.
    Which KPIs would you track for a refinery unit?
    Throughput and utilisation, yields vs plan, energy intensity and heater excess O2, mechanical availability and unplanned downtime, off-spec and giveaway, flaring, alarm rate, controllers in auto, IOW exceedances and process safety events (Tier 1/2 and leading indicators).
    How do you manage a request to bypass a safety trip?
    Only with a documented risk assessment, alternative protection (e.g. local monitoring, second independent instrument), defined time limit, authorisation at the correct level, entry in the override register, communication at handover — and a cumulative risk review with other bypasses on the same unit.
    Tell me about a time you handled an upset (behavioural — STAR).
    Use STAR: Situation (e.g. reflux pump trip at night), Task (keep the column stable and naphtha within limits), Action (started spare, cut firing and rate, informed the downstream board and supervisor, diverted off-spec naphtha), Result (no flaring, back on-spec in 40 minutes, RCA found a worn coupling; PM frequency changed).
    How do you build a strong process safety culture as a supervisor?
    Be in the field, follow procedures myself, stop work when unsure and support others who do, challenge normalised deviations like standing alarms, report and share near misses, keep overrides short and visible, and recognise good catches publicly.

    3️⃣ Scenario 3 · Process Deviation

    Situation: The vacuum heater pass-3 tube metal temperature (TMT) has risen from 590 °C to 628 °C over five days. IOW standard limit: 620 °C; critical limit: 650 °C. Pass-3 flow is 8% below the other passes.
    Q. What is the most appropriate response?
    • A Ignore it — it is below the critical limit
    • B Increase firing to raise vacuum residue cut point
    • C Turn off the TMT alarm because it is a nuisance
    • D Record the IOW exceedance, rebalance pass flows, reduce firing, inform the engineer and inspection, and set trigger points toward the critical limit
    ✍️ Why is low pass flow dangerous in a fired heater?
    Model answer: Less fluid absorbs the same radiant heat, so the oil film temperature and tube metal temperature rise. Oil cracks and lays down coke inside the tube, which insulates it and raises TMT further. Tube metal can creep, bulge and rupture — releasing hot oil into the firebox and causing a major fire.
