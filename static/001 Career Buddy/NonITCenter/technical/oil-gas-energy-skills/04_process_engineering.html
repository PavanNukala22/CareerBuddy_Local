    Process Engineering Fundamentals

    Start from zero: what process engineers do in oil and gas, the principles behind every calculation, how a project moves from idea to operating plant, the documents you will use every day, and the process safety basics.

    ⚗️ What is Process Engineering?

    Process Engineering

    process operationsengineeringtechnical design
    Definition
    Process engineering is the design, operation, control and optimisation of processes that change raw materials — crude oil, natural gas, water — into products, safely and economically, by applying chemistry, physics and engineering principles.
    Why it matters
    Process engineers decide what a plant does and under which conditions: flows, pressures, temperatures, equipment sizes, control and safety systems. Their decisions set the plant's capacity, product quality, energy use and safety envelope for decades.
    Explanation
    Two main work settings: design (new plants and modifications — simulation, sizing, P&IDs, safety studies) and operations support (plant process engineers — monitoring performance, troubleshooting, optimisation, MOC reviews, operating limits). Both rely on material and energy balances, thermodynamics, fluid flow, heat and mass transfer and process safety.
    Types
    Design/EPC process engineer, plant (operations) process engineer, process safety engineer, process control engineer, simulation specialist, technical services.
    Components
    Design basis, simulation models, heat & material balances, PFDs, P&IDs, equipment datasheets, control philosophy, cause-and-effect charts, relief studies, operating limits.
    Process / workflow
    Define requirements → simulate and balance → size equipment → produce PFD/P&ID → safety studies (HAZID/HAZOP) → support construction and commissioning → monitor and optimise in operation.
    O&G example
    A gas field produces more water than planned. The process engineer calculates new separator and produced-water loads, finds the water treatment unit is undersized, sizes an extra hydrocyclone package, updates the P&ID through MOC, and checks the change in a HAZOP.
    Common mistakes
    Designing with a single “normal” case only; ignoring startup, shutdown and upset cases; trusting simulation without checking the inputs.
    Best practices
    Close balances; consider all operating cases (min, normal, max, turndown, upset); document assumptions; involve operations early.
    🧪 Practice activity: Design or operations?
    Classify: (a) sizing a new separator, (b) finding why a column floods, (c) writing the design basis, (d) setting new operating limits after a crude change.
    Answer: (a) and (c) design; (b) and (d) operations support — both are process engineering.
    EXAMPLE · One change, many hands

    The Engineer calculates new flows; the Senior Engineer reviews the design; the Project Engineer delivers the modification; the Coordinator aligns HAZOP and MOC meetings; the Planner schedules the tie-in; the Supervisor runs the startup shift; the Inspector checks installed work against the P&ID; the Analyst tracks performance afterwards. All ten Process Engineering roles touch one change.

    📐 Core Engineering Principles

    The Principles Behind Every Calculation

    calculationsengineering
    Definition
    The physical laws process engineers use to describe and design processes.
    Why it matters
    Every flow sheet, equipment size and operating limit comes from these principles. Understanding them lets you check software results and solve problems in the field.
    Explanation
    • Conservation of mass: In − Out + Generation − Consumption = Accumulation.
    • Conservation of energy: heat and work in = heat and work out + change in stored energy; sensible heat Q = m·cp·ΔT.
    • Thermodynamics & phase equilibrium: how hydrocarbons split into vapour and liquid at a given pressure and temperature.
    • Fluid flow: pressure drop, pump head, compressor work.
    • Heat transfer: Q = U·A·LMTD.
    • Mass transfer: distillation, absorption, stripping.
    • Reaction kinetics: conversion, selectivity, heat of reaction.
    Types
    Steady-state vs dynamic; equilibrium vs rate-based.
    Components
    Physical properties, equations of state (e.g. Peng-Robinson), correlations, simulation software.
    Process / workflow
    Define the system boundary → list streams → apply balances → solve → check reasonableness.
    O&G example
    Cooling 10 kg/s of water from 60 to 40 °C needs Q = 10 × 4.18 × 20 = 836 kW — the duty the cooler and cooling medium must provide.
    Common mistakes
    Mixing units; forgetting latent heat when phase changes; wrong system boundary.
    Best practices
    Always write units; sketch the boundary; check the balance closes within a few percent.
    🧪 Practice activity: Simple balance
    A column receives 100 t/h feed. Overhead product is 35 t/h. With no accumulation, what is the bottoms flow?
    Answer: 100 − 35 = 65 t/h.

    🔄 Process Engineering Lifecycle

    Figure 1.2 · Process engineering through the asset lifecycle (FEED = Front-End Engineering Design)

    Project & Asset Lifecycle

    project executionplanningtechnical documentation
    Definition
    The stages a process facility passes through from idea to operation and eventually decommissioning.
    Why it matters
    The cheapest time to change a design is early; changes in construction or operation cost far more. Process engineers have the greatest influence in concept and FEED.
    Explanation
    Concept/feasibility: options, rough sizes, cost class. FEED: design basis, PFDs, H&MB, preliminary P&IDs, HAZID, cost estimate for investment decision. Detailed design: final P&IDs, datasheets, relief studies, HAZOP, SIL. Construction & commissioning: procedures, PSSR, startup. Operation: performance monitoring, MOCs, HAZOP revalidation.
    Types
    Greenfield (new), brownfield (modification of an existing plant), debottlenecking.
    Components
    Stage-gate reviews, deliverables lists, design reviews, safety studies.
    Process / workflow
    Stage gate → approval → next stage, with design freeze points.
    O&G example
    A missed operating case (low-pressure startup) found in HAZOP during detailed design costs a P&ID change. The same miss found during commissioning could mean new valves, piping and a delayed startup.
    Common mistakes
    Late operations input; design changes after freeze without control.
    Best practices
    Involve operations from FEED; freeze design formally; track changes.
    🧪 Practice activity: Which stage?
    In which stage are final P&IDs and the HAZOP normally completed?
    Answer: Detailed design (preliminary P&IDs and HAZID occur in FEED).

    🦺 Basic Process Safety Concepts

    Process Safety in Design and Operation

    process safetysafety
    Definition
    Process safety is preventing loss of containment of hazardous materials and energy — fires, explosions, toxic releases — through design, operation and maintenance.
    Why it matters
    Process engineers set pressures, temperatures, inventories and relief systems. A wrong assumption in design can stay hidden for years until the wrong conditions occur.
    Explanation
    • Hazards: flammability (LEL/UEL), toxicity (H2S), overpressure, high/low temperature (brittle fracture), runaway reactions.
    • Inherent safety (Trevor Kletz): minimise inventories, substitute safer materials, moderate conditions, simplify design.
    • Layers of protection: design → control → alarms → trips (SIS) → relief → mitigation → emergency response.
    Types
    Occupational vs process safety; prevention vs mitigation barriers.
    Components
    Design limits, relief devices, SIS, alarms, procedures, MOC, HAZOP.
    Process / workflow
    Identify hazards (HAZID/HAZOP) → design safeguards → verify (LOPA/SIL, relief study) → maintain → revalidate.
    O&G example
    BP Texas City (2005): a raffinate splitter overfilled during startup and hydrocarbons were released from an atmospheric blowdown stack, killing 15 people. One lesson for process engineers: relief and blowdown systems should route flammable releases to a closed system/flare, not to atmosphere near people.
    Common mistakes
    Designing only for normal operation; relying on operators for every safeguard.
    Best practices
    Apply inherent safety first; consider abnormal and startup cases; involve operations in safety studies.
    🧪 Practice activity: Inherent safety
    Give one inherently safer alternative to storing 500 m3 of an intermediate between two units.
    Answer: Minimise — reduce intermediate storage by better unit coupling/control, or store a less hazardous form; fewer tonnes means a smaller potential release.

    Core Process Calculations

    The everyday calculations of a process engineer: material and energy balances, pressure drop and line sizing, pump head and NPSH, compressor basics, heat-exchanger duty, and phase behaviour of hydrocarbons.

    ⚖️ Material Balances

    Material Balance

    calculationsdata analysis
    Definition
    Accounting of all mass entering and leaving a system: In − Out + Generation − Consumption = Accumulation (at steady state with no reaction, In = Out).
    Why it matters
    A balance that does not close means bad meters, wrong lab data or a real loss (leak, flaring). Accurate balances drive yield accounting, optimisation and troubleshooting.
    Explanation
    Balances can be done on total mass, on each component, or on elements (with reactions). Typical plant balances close within ~1–3%; larger gaps point to instrument problems. Data reconciliation adjusts measurements statistically to close balances.
    Types
    Overall, component, unit, plant-wide; steady state vs dynamic.
    Components
    Flow meters, densities, lab compositions, tank inventories, system boundary.
    Process / workflow
    Draw boundary → list streams → collect consistent-period data → convert to mass → compute closure → investigate gaps.
    O&G example
    Separator inlet 1,000 t/d; gas 180 t/d, oil 620 t/d, water 150 t/d → out 950 t/d → closure 95%. A 5% gap leads the engineer to find a water meter reading low due to fouling.
    Common mistakes
    Mixing volume and mass; different time periods; ignoring tank level changes.
    Best practices
    Work in mass; same time window; include inventory change.
    🧪 Practice activity: Closure
    Feed 500 t/h; products 300 t/h and 185 t/h. Closure %?
    Answer: 485 ÷ 500 = 97% — investigate the 3% gap (meters, flaring, leaks).

    🔥 Energy Balances

    Energy Balance

    calculationsengineering
    Definition
    Accounting of energy into and out of a system: heat, work and enthalpy of streams.
    Why it matters
    Energy balances size heaters, coolers, condensers and reboilers, and reveal energy losses — often the biggest operating cost after feed.
    Explanation
    Sensible heat Q = m·cp·ΔT; latent heat Q = m·λ; for gases and mixtures, use enthalpies from a simulator. Fired heater efficiency = heat absorbed ÷ fuel fired (LHV).
    Types
    Unit energy balance, utility balance (steam, fuel, power), site energy balance.
    Components
    Stream enthalpies, duties, utility meters, efficiencies.
    Process / workflow
    Boundary → enthalpy in and out → duties → check against utility consumption.
    O&G example
    Heating 50 t/h of crude (cp ≈ 2.2 kJ/kg·K) from 120 to 220 °C: Q = (50,000/3,600) × 2.2 × 100 ≈ 3.06 MW absorbed. At 88% efficiency, fuel fired ≈ 3.47 MW.
    Common mistakes
    Forgetting phase change; using wrong cp units (kJ vs kcal); per-hour vs per-second errors.
    Best practices
    Use consistent SI units; check duty against measured utility use.
    🧪 Practice activity: Cooler duty
    Cool 10 kg/s water from 60 °C to 40 °C (cp 4.18 kJ/kg·K). Duty?
    Answer: 10 × 4.18 × 20 = 836 kW.

    💧 Fluid Flow & Hydraulics

    Pressure Drop & Line Sizing

    calculationstechnical designP&ID
    Definition
    Calculating pressure losses and choosing pipe sizes so fluids move at acceptable velocity and pressure drop.
    Why it matters
    Undersized lines limit capacity and cause erosion, noise and vibration; oversized lines waste money and can cause liquid slugging or deposition.
    Explanation
    Darcy–Weisbach: ΔP = f·(L/D)·ρv²/2, with friction factor f from Reynolds number and roughness (Moody chart). Line sizing criteria: maximum ΔP per 100 m and velocity limits (liquids typically ~1–3 m/s in pump discharge; gases higher). For two-phase lines, the erosional velocity guideline from API RP 14E is Ve = C/√ρm. Also check elevation (static head) and fittings losses.
    Types
    Single-phase liquid, gas (compressible), two-phase, gravity flow.
    Components
    Flow, density, viscosity, pipe ID, length, fittings, elevation, roughness.
    Process / workflow
    Get stream data from H&MB → choose trial size → calculate velocity and ΔP → compare with criteria → select size → record in line list.
    O&G example
    Increasing a crude export rate by 25% raises velocity by 25% and friction pressure drop by roughly 56% (ΔP ∝ v²). The pump can no longer deliver; the debottleneck needs a bigger impeller or line loop.
    Common mistakes
    Ignoring elevation; using nominal instead of internal diameter; single-phase methods for two-phase flow.
    Best practices
    Use company line-sizing criteria; check all cases (max flow, startup); document in the line list.
    🧪 Practice activity: Scaling
    If flow doubles in the same pipe (turbulent flow, roughly constant f), how does friction pressure drop change?
    Answer: About 4 times (ΔP ∝ v²).

    🌀 Pumps & Compressors: Process View

    Pump Head, NPSH & Compressor Basics

    calculationsequipment/process operationtechnical design
    Definition
    The process calculations that specify pumps and compressors: required head/pressure, flow, NPSH and power.
    Why it matters
    Wrong specification causes cavitation, surge, overload or capacity shortfall — expensive to fix after purchase.
    Explanation
    • Pump head: H = ΔP/(ρ·g). Example: 5 bar on water → 500,000 ÷ (1,000 × 9.81) ≈ 51 m.
    • Hydraulic power = Q·ΔP; shaft power = hydraulic ÷ efficiency.
    • NPSH available = suction pressure + static head − friction − vapour pressure (as head); must exceed NPSH required with margin.
    • Compressors: compression ratio, polytropic head, discharge temperature (limit often ~150–175 °C), surge and stonewall limits, staging with intercooling.
    Types
    Centrifugal and positive-displacement pumps; centrifugal, reciprocating, screw compressors.
    Components
    System curve, pump/compressor curve, datasheet, operating point.
    Process / workflow
    System curve (static + friction) → required duty at all cases → select machine → check operating point near best efficiency → NPSH margin.
    O&G example
    A new low-pressure separator lowers pump suction pressure; NPSH available falls below required at high temperature. Raising the separator elevation or adding a booster pump is evaluated.
    Common mistakes
    Ignoring vapour pressure at max temperature; designing only at rated point; no minimum-flow protection.
    Best practices
    Check all operating cases; keep operating point near BEP; include minimum flow and anti-surge.
    🧪 Practice activity: Head
    A pump raises pressure by 10 bar on liquid with density 800 kg/m3. Head?
    Answer: 1,000,000 ÷ (800 × 9.81) ≈ 127 m.

    🔁 Heat Transfer

    Heat Exchanger Design & Rating

    calculationstechnical designtrend analysis
    Definition
    Calculating the area and performance of heat exchangers using Q = U·A·LMTD.
    Why it matters
    Exchangers recover energy and control temperatures; fouling and poor design limit capacity and raise energy cost.
    Explanation
    LMTD uses the temperature differences at each end; a correction factor F applies for multi-pass. U depends on film coefficients, wall and fouling resistance. Rating checks an existing exchanger at new conditions; design sizes a new one (e.g. with HTRI/Aspen EDR). Monitoring U over time shows fouling.
    Types
    Shell-and-tube (TEMA types), air coolers, plate, double-pipe; condensers, reboilers.
    Components
    Duty, temperatures, U, area, fouling factors, pressure drop, TEMA datasheet.
    Process / workflow
    Duty from energy balance → LMTD → assume U → area → detailed rating → check ΔP and vibration.
    O&G example
    A gas cooler's U fell from 450 to 300 W/m²·K over a year. Outlet temperature rose and limited compressor capacity in summer. The engineer justifies cleaning by comparing lost production with cleaning cost.
    Common mistakes
    Temperature cross in single-shell design; ignoring fouling; ignoring air-cooler summer ambient.
    Best practices
    Design for hot-day ambient; trend U; plan cleaning economically.
    🧪 Practice activity: Area
    Duty 1,000 kW, U = 500 W/m²·K, LMTD = 25 K. Area?
    Answer: 1,000,000 ÷ (500 × 25) = 80 m² (before margins and correction factor).

    ❄️ Phase Behaviour & Separation

    Hydrocarbon Phase Behaviour, Hydrates & Separation

    engineeringcalculationsprocess safety
    Definition
    How hydrocarbon mixtures split into gas and liquid with pressure and temperature, and how this is used to separate them.
    Why it matters
    Separation, dew-point control, hydrate prevention and relief sizing all depend on phase behaviour.
    Explanation
    • Phase envelope: bubble-point and dew-point curves meeting at the critical point; inside is two-phase.
    • Flash: reducing pressure vaporises light components; letting gas down through a valve cools it (Joule–Thomson effect).
    • Hydrates form with free water at high pressure and low temperature; prevented by heating, dehydration or inhibitors (MEG, methanol).
    • Separator sizing: gas velocity limited by the Souders–Brown relation v = K·√((ρL − ρV)/ρV).
    • Distillation: relative volatility, reflux, stages.
    Types
    Two-phase and three-phase separators, flash drums, columns, absorbers, strippers.
    Components
    Equation of state, composition, phase envelope, hydrate curve, internals (demisters, weirs).
    Process / workflow
    Define composition → phase envelope and hydrate curve → set conditions → size equipment for vapour and liquid loads.
    O&G example
    Gas at 90 bar is let down to 40 bar through a choke and cools by roughly 20–30 °C (JT). On a winter night it enters the hydrate region; the engineer specifies a line heater upstream or methanol injection.
    Common mistakes
    Ignoring JT cooling; sizing separators on normal flow only (slugs!); wrong K factor for internals.
    Best practices
    Check hydrate margin at all cases including startup and depressuring; size for slugs and turndown.
    🧪 Practice activity: Hydrate prevention
    Name three ways to prevent hydrates in a gas line.
    Answer: Keep temperature above hydrate temperature (heating, insulation), remove water (dehydration, e.g. TEG), inject inhibitor (MEG or methanol); also operate at lower pressure where possible.

    🛢️ Oil, Gas & Water Separation

    Figure 3.1 · Horizontal three-phase separator with weir (simplified)

    Separator Design & Operation

    technical designcalculationsprocess operations
    Definition
    Separators split well fluids into gas, oil and water using gravity, residence time and internals.
    Why it matters
    Poor separation sends liquid to compressors, water to oil export and oil to water discharge (environmental breach).
    Explanation
    Gas capacity is set by Souders–Brown velocity (with mist eliminator K values); liquid capacity by residence time (often 2–5 min for oil, longer for emulsions) and droplet settling. Levels: normal and alarm levels (LAH/LAL), trips (LAHH/LALL). Inlet devices, weirs, coalescers and demisters improve separation. Multi-stage separation at falling pressures increases stable oil recovery.
    Types
    Two/three-phase; horizontal/vertical; slug catchers; test separators; inlet, production, LP stages.
    Components
    Inlet device, weir, demister, vortex breakers, level instruments, PSV, sand jetting.
    Process / workflow
    Define flows and slugs → size gas and liquid sections → set levels and controls → PSV case → check turndown.
    O&G example
    Water cut rises from 20% to 60% over years. The water section residence time halves; oil-in-water rises above discharge limits. The engineer proposes changing the weir height and adding a coalescer pack through MOC.
    Common mistakes
    Sizing for average flow without slugs; ignoring foaming; one level transmitter for a trip.
    Best practices
    Design for future water cut and slugs; independent trip instruments; account for emulsions.
    🧪 Practice activity: Residence time
    Oil section liquid volume 12 m³, oil flow 240 m³/h. Residence time?
    Answer: 12 ÷ 240 h = 0.05 h = 3 minutes.

    💨 Gas Dehydration

    TEG Dehydration

    process operationstroubleshootingcalculations
    Definition
    Removing water vapour from natural gas, most often by absorption into triethylene glycol (TEG), or by molecular sieves for very low dew points.
    Why it matters
    Water causes hydrates, corrosion and pipeline specification failures (water dew point or lb/MMscf limits).
    Explanation
    Wet gas contacts lean TEG in a contactor; rich TEG is flashed, filtered, heated and regenerated in a reboiler/still column (~200 °C; TEG degrades above ~204 °C). Lean TEG purity (often 98.5–99.9 wt%, higher with stripping gas) and circulation rate set the dew point. Problems: foaming, glycol losses, hydrocarbon contamination, BTEX emissions from the still vent.
    Types
    TEG absorption, molecular sieve adsorption, MEG/methanol injection (inhibition rather than dehydration).
    Components
    Inlet scrubber, contactor, flash drum, filters, lean/rich exchanger, still column, reboiler, surge tank, pumps.
    Process / workflow
    Monitor outlet dew point, lean glycol purity, circulation rate, reboiler temperature, losses → adjust.
    O&G example
    Export gas dew point goes off-spec. Data show reboiler temperature normal but lean TEG purity low — stripping gas valve stuck. Fixing it restores purity and dew point.
    Common mistakes
    Overheating TEG; hydrocarbon liquids carried into the contactor (foaming); poor filtration.
    Best practices
    Good inlet scrubbing; trend glycol quality; control reboiler temperature.
    🧪 Practice activity: Diagnose
    Contactor ΔP is unstable and glycol losses are high. Likely cause?
    Answer: Foaming — often from hydrocarbon or chemical contamination; check inlet scrubber, filters, and glycol quality.

    🧪 Gas Sweetening

    Amine Sweetening

    process safetyengineeringtroubleshooting
    Definition
    Removing acid gases (H2S and CO2) from natural gas using regenerable amine solutions (e.g. MDEA, DEA).
    Why it matters
    H2S is highly toxic and corrosive; sales gas often must contain only a few ppm H2S; CO2 lowers heating value and causes corrosion.
    Explanation
    Gas contacts lean amine in an absorber; rich amine is flashed, heated and stripped in a regenerator with a steam reboiler; acid gas goes to a sulfur recovery unit or disposal. MDEA is selective for H2S. Key parameters: amine strength, lean loading, circulation, reboiler duty, lean amine temperature (kept ~5 °C above gas to avoid hydrocarbon condensation and foaming).
    Types
    Primary (MEA), secondary (DEA), tertiary (MDEA), formulated amines; physical solvents; membranes.
    Components
    Absorber, flash drum, lean/rich exchanger, regenerator, reboiler, reflux, filters, amine sump.
    Process / workflow
    Monitor treated gas H2S, lean loading, amine strength, foaming signs, heat-stable salts.
    O&G example
    Treated gas H2S rises. Lean loading is high because reboiler steam was cut to save energy. Restoring duty brings H2S back on spec — an optimisation that went too far.
    Common mistakes
    Lean amine colder than gas; ignoring heat-stable salts; over-cutting reboiler duty.
    Best practices
    Trend loadings; antifoam only as a short-term fix; filter well.
    🧪 Practice activity: Temperature
    Why is lean amine kept slightly warmer than the incoming gas?
    Answer: To avoid condensing heavy hydrocarbons in the absorber, which cause foaming and amine carry-over.

    🌀 Gas Compression & Dew Point Control

    Compression & Hydrocarbon Dew Point Control

    calculationstechnical designprocess operations
    Definition
    Raising gas pressure for export or reinjection, and removing heavy hydrocarbons so they don't condense in pipelines.
    Why it matters
    Compressors are often the largest power users and the main production bottleneck; dew point control prevents liquids in sales gas lines.
    Explanation
    Compression is staged (ratio per stage ~3–4) with intercoolers and scrubbers to limit discharge temperature and remove condensate. Anti-surge control protects centrifugal machines. Dew point control: chill gas by JT valve, turbo-expander or refrigeration, separate condensed NGLs (which become valuable products).
    Types
    Export, booster, reinjection, gas lift compression; JT, refrigeration, turbo-expander dew pointing.
    Components
    Suction scrubbers, compressor stages, coolers, anti-surge valves, chillers, low-temperature separators.
    Process / workflow
    Define flows and pressures → stage design → cooler duties → dew point process → simulate all cases incl. low reservoir pressure in later life.
    O&G example
    As reservoir pressure declines, compressor suction pressure falls and the compression ratio rises beyond one stage's capability. The engineer evaluates re-wheeling or adding a booster stage.
    Common mistakes
    Designing only for early-life pressures; inadequate scrubbers; ignoring hot-day cooler limits.
    Best practices
    Design for field life; robust liquid removal; anti-surge for all cases.
    🧪 Practice activity: Stages
    Gas must go from 5 to 125 bar(a). Overall ratio 25. With equal ratios, how many stages if the limit is ~3.5 per stage?
    Answer: 3 stages (25^(1/3) ≈ 2.9 per stage); 2 stages would need 5 per stage — too high.

    🔥 Relief, Blowdown & Flare Systems

    Overpressure Protection & Flare

    process safetycalculationsdesign review
    Definition
    Systems that protect equipment from overpressure (relief valves, rupture discs), reduce inventory in emergencies (blowdown/depressuring) and dispose of releases safely (flare).
    Why it matters
    They are the last engineered line of defence before rupture. Under-sized relief or flare systems can fail exactly when needed.
    Explanation
    Standards: API 520 (sizing), API 521 (scenarios, depressuring, flare). Overpressure scenarios: blocked outlet, fire, cooling failure, power failure, control valve failure, tube rupture (API 521 “10/13 rule” for when it must be considered), thermal expansion. Each PSV is sized for its governing case; the flare is sized for the worst simultaneous relief (e.g. site-wide power failure). Depressuring typically aims to reduce pressure quickly in a fire (a common guideline is to 50% of design pressure or 6.9 barg within ~15 minutes, depending on wall thickness). Watch low-temperature effects during depressuring.
    Types
    Spring/pilot PSVs, rupture discs, blowdown valves, elevated/ground flares, closed vs atmospheric disposal.
    Components
    Relief study, PSV datasheets, flare network model, KO drum, flare tip, purge gas.
    Process / workflow
    Identify scenarios per equipment → relief loads → size PSV → network back-pressure check → flare/KO drum capacity → document.
    O&G example
    A debottleneck increases throughput 20%. The MOC review finds the column PSV's blocked-outlet relief load now exceeds its capacity and the flare header back-pressure is too high. A larger PSV and a header revamp are added to the project scope.
    Common mistakes
    Forgetting to re-check relief after capacity increases; ignoring back-pressure; atmospheric vents for flammable releases.
    Best practices
    Keep the relief study live with every MOC; check simultaneity; route flammable releases to flare.
    🧪 Practice activity: Scenario list
    Name four overpressure scenarios for a distillation column.
    Answer: Blocked outlet (overhead), loss of cooling/reflux, external fire, reboiler control failure (excess heat), power failure, tube rupture in reboiler.

    Definition

    🚀 Debottlenecking & Optimization

    Debottlenecking & Optimization

    advanced technical analysiscalculationsdesign review
    Definition
    Debottlenecking raises capacity by removing the equipment limits that constrain it; optimization adjusts operating conditions for the best economic result within constraints.
    Why it matters
    Debottlenecks are often the cheapest way to add capacity; optimization delivers value daily without capital.
    Explanation
    Build a bottleneck chart: each equipment's capacity at the new rate (pumps, compressors, exchangers, columns, lines, PSVs, flare, utilities). Fix the first constraint, then the next appears. Optimization: find the active constraint and the economics (e.g. NGL recovery vs compression power).
    Types
    Operational (no hardware), minor modifications, major revamp.
    Components
    Calibrated model, test-run data, equipment ratings, cost estimates, economics.
    Process / workflow
    Test run → calibrated model → rate increments → list constraints → options and costs → select → MOC and safety studies.
    O&G example
    Target +15% gas throughput. Bottleneck list: (1) inlet separator gas velocity at 108% — new demister; (2) compressor at 112% — re-wheel; (3) export line ΔP OK; (4) PSV on separator inadequate — larger PSV; (5) flare OK. Cost and schedule built from this list.
    Common mistakes
    Forgetting relief/flare, utilities and instruments; fixing only the first bottleneck.
    Best practices
    Check every item in the system at the new rate; include safety systems.
    🧪 Practice activity: Missing items
    Name three items often forgotten in a debottleneck study.
    Answer: Relief valves and flare capacity, control valves (Cv at higher flow), utilities (cooling, power), instrument ranges, product storage/export.

    ♻️ Energy Efficiency & Heat Integration

    Energy Efficiency & Pinch Analysis

    calculationsengineeringKPI tracking
    Definition
    Reducing fuel, power and steam per unit product; pinch analysis finds the minimum heating and cooling a process needs by matching hot and cold streams.
    Why it matters
    Energy is a major cost and source of CO2; flaring and venting also affect licence to operate.
    Explanation
    Composite curves of hot and cold streams; the closest approach (ΔTmin) defines the pinch. Rules: don't transfer heat across the pinch, don't use cooling above it or heating below it.     Types
    Operational energy optimisation, heat integration projects, electrification, flare reduction.
    Components
    Stream data, composite curves, utility costs, energy KPIs.
    Process / workflow
    Extract stream data → targets → compare with actual → identify violations → options → economics.
    O&G example
    A gas plant flares low-pressure gas from flash drums. A small flare gas recovery compressor returns it to fuel — cutting flaring and fuel gas purchases.
    Common mistakes
    Steam letdowns without use; cooling hot streams that could preheat feed; ignoring flaring.
    Best practices
    Energy KPIs in daily reports; periodic pinch reviews.
    🧪 Practice activity: Pinch rule
    Using cooling water to cool a stream above the pinch breaks which rule, and what is the effect?

    🔎 HAZID & HAZOP

    Hazard Identification Studies

    P&IDdesign reviewprocess safety
    Definition
    HAZID is a high-level hazard identification early in design; HAZOP is a detailed, node-by-node review of P&IDs using guidewords (NO, MORE, LESS, REVERSE, AS WELL AS, PART OF, OTHER THAN) applied to parameters (flow, pressure, temperature, level, composition).
    Why it matters
    Most design-related hazards are found and fixed here, before construction.
    Explanation
    For each deviation: causes → consequences (assuming safeguards fail) → existing safeguards → risk ranking → recommendations. Process engineers present the design intent and answer technical questions; operations bring practical experience. Revalidation typically every 5 years and for significant changes.
    Types
    HAZID, HAZOP, What-If, checklist, FMEA, procedural HAZOP for startup/shutdown.
    Components
    Facilitator, scribe, process, operations, instrument, mechanical, safety; P&IDs, C&E, design data.
    Process / workflow
    Prepare nodes and design intent → sessions → worksheet → actions with owners → close-out with evidence.
    O&G example
    MORE PRESSURE on an LP separator — cause: gas blow-by from an upstream HP separator when its level control valve fails open. Consequence: overpressure beyond the LP separator's PSV capacity. Recommendation: LALL trip on HP separator and resize LP PSV for blow-by.
    Common mistakes
    No operators in sessions; vague actions; consequences judged with safeguards working.
    Best practices
    Clear design intent per node; SMART actions; independent close-out check.
    🧪 Practice activity: Gas blow-by
    What is gas blow-by and why is it a key HAZOP case?
    Answer: High-pressure gas passing into a downstream low-pressure vessel when liquid level is lost and the level control valve is open. The gas flow can be far larger than the liquid design, causing severe overpressure.

    ⚖️ LOPA & SIL

    Figure 6.1 · Independent protection layers

    Layer of Protection Analysis & Safety Integrity Level

    advanced technical analysiscalculationsprocess safety
    Definition
    LOPA is a semi-quantitative method that estimates the frequency of a consequence using initiating event frequency and the probability of failure on demand (PFD) of each independent protection layer (IPL). SIL (IEC 61511) is the required reliability of a safety instrumented function.
    Why it matters
    It decides whether existing safeguards are enough or a SIL-rated trip is required, and how often it must be proof-tested.
    Explanation
    Mitigated frequency = initiating frequency × PFD1 × PFD2 × … Compare with the tolerable target. Typical credits: BPCS loop PFD 0.1; operator response to alarm 0.1 (if enough time); PSV 0.01. SIL bands (low demand): SIL 1 PFD 0.1–0.01, SIL 2 0.01–0.001, SIL 3 0.001–0.0001. IPLs must be effective, independent and auditable.
    Types
    LOPA, risk graph, fault tree, QRA.
    Components
    Scenario, initiating event, IPLs, conditional modifiers, target frequency, SIL verification.
    Process / workflow
    Select HAZOP scenarios → LOPA → gap → specify SIF and SIL → design and verify → proof test.
    O&G example
    Initiating event (level control failure) 0.1/yr; alarm + operator 0.1; PSV 0.01 → 0.1 × 0.1 × 0.01 = 1×10−4/yr. Target 1×10−5/yr → gap factor 10 → a SIL 1 trip (PFD ≤ 0.1) is required.
    Common mistakes
    Crediting the BPCS loop that is the initiating cause; double-counting operators; no proof testing.
    Best practices
    Strict independence rules; documented assumptions; verify SIL with real test intervals.
    🧪 Practice activity: Calculate
    Initiating frequency 0.2/yr; IPLs with PFD 0.1 and 0.01. Mitigated frequency?
    Answer: 0.2 × 0.1 × 0.01 = 2×10−4 per year.

    🌱 Inherent Safety & Risk Assessment

    Inherent Safety & Risk-Based Design

    technical designprocess safetyengineering
    Definition
    Designing hazards out rather than adding protection, and assessing remaining risk against criteria (e.g. ALARP).
    Why it matters
    Removing a hazard is more reliable than any layer of protection that must be maintained.
    Explanation
    Inherent safety principles: minimise inventory, substitute less hazardous materials, moderate conditions (lower pressure/temperature), simplify design and operation. Risk tools: risk matrix, bow-tie, consequence modelling (dispersion, fire, explosion), QRA, facility siting (control room and occupied buildings).
    Types
    Qualitative, semi-quantitative, quantitative risk assessment.
    Components
    Hazard register, consequence models, risk criteria, siting studies.
    Process / workflow
    Identify hazards early → apply inherent safety options → assess risk → add protection → document ALARP.
    O&G example
    Instead of storing large volumes of methanol for hydrate inhibition, the design uses a line heater and dehydration so only a small methanol supply is needed for startups — minimise and substitute.
    Common mistakes
    Adding layers instead of removing hazards; siting occupied buildings near hazards.
    Best practices
    Inherent safety review at concept stage; siting studies early.
    🧪 Practice activity: Four principles
    Match: reduce LPG storage from 1,000 to 200 m³; replace a toxic solvent with a less toxic one; operate at 20 bar instead of 60 bar; remove an unnecessary bypass.
    Answer: Minimise; substitute; moderate; simplify.

    Definition
    Bringing a new or modified process from mechanical completion to stable operation, including the Pre-Startup Safety Review (PSSR).
    Why it matters

    PSSR as paperwork only; startup with open A-items; procedures written after startup.
    Best practices
    Field walkdown; procedural HAZOP of startup; engineers on shift during startup.

    🕸️ Integrated Design & Operations

    Whole-Facility Process Thinking

    advanced technical analysisprocess operationsleadership
    Definition
    Seeing the facility as one system — reservoir/feed, process units, utilities, flare, export — over its whole life.
    Why it matters
    Local optimisation often moves the problem elsewhere; designs that ignore operations create decades of difficulty.
    Explanation
    Experts check interactions: a change in separator pressure affects compressor power, flare loads and dew point; more water production loads water treatment and injection; utility margins limit every expansion. They also feed operating experience back into new designs (“lessons learned register”).
    Types
    Integrated asset models (reservoir to export), site utility models, life-of-field studies.
    Components
    Calibrated models, operating data, lessons-learned database, constraint register.
    Process / workflow
    Map interactions → model whole system → test changes → agree with stakeholders.
    O&G example
    Lowering first-stage separator pressure increases well deliverability, but raises compression power and flash gas to the LP flare. The integrated model shows a net gain only if a flare gas recovery compressor is added.
    Common mistakes
    Unit-only thinking; designs without operations input.
    Best practices
    System models; operations in design reviews; lessons-learned loops.
    🧪 Practice activity: Ripple
    Water cut doubles over three years. Name three systems affected.
    Answer: Separator water section/residence time, produced-water treatment and disposal/injection capacity, heating (more water to heat), chemical consumption, pump power.

    🧩 Advanced Troubleshooting

    Complex & Chronic Process Problems

    troubleshootingadvanced technical analysisRCA
    Definition
    Solving problems with distant or interacting causes — slow capacity loss, intermittent instability, recurring off-spec.
    Why it matters
    These problems cost the most and are often accepted as “normal”.
    Explanation
    Tools: calibrated simulation to separate measurement errors from real changes, dynamic simulation for oscillations, gamma scans for columns, tracer studies for residence time, CFD for separators, long-term data correlation (ambient, feed, operating modes), lab fingerprinting of contaminants.
    Types
    Hydraulic, phase-behaviour, contamination, control interaction, fouling.
    Components
    Historian, models, specialist diagnostics, vendor expertise.
    Process / workflow
    Frame with data → hypotheses across systems → specialist tests → confirm cause → fix through MOC → verify.
    O&G example
    An amine absorber foams every few weeks. Correlation shows it follows compressor wash cycles — wash chemicals carried over in gas. A better inlet coalescer and changed wash procedure end the foaming.
    Common mistakes
    Antifoam as permanent fix; short data windows.
    Best practices
    Look for time correlation with other events; fix the source.
    🧪 Practice activity: Intermittent problem
    A separator level oscillates every 20 minutes. Name two possible causes.
    Answer: Poorly tuned or sticky level control valve; terrain slugging from the flowline; interaction with a downstream control loop.

    🧭 Technical Decision Making

    Decisions Under Uncertainty

    problem-solvingcalculationsprocess safety
    Definition
    Choosing between technical options — operate, modify, invest, shut down — with incomplete data and competing objectives.
    Why it matters
    Process engineers' recommendations shape capital spending and safety margins.
    Explanation
    Fixed priority: safety → environment → asset integrity → production/economics. Use options analysis with value, cost, risk and uncertainty (e.g. NPV, payback, sensitivity to feed forecasts). Never trade design limits or safeguards for production without formal assessment.
    Types
    Operating decisions, investment decisions, safety decisions.
    Components
    Options, economics, risk assessment, stakeholder input, authority levels.
    Process / workflow
    Frame → options (incl. “do nothing”) → evaluate → recommend → decide at right level → review outcome.
    O&G example
    Two options to handle more gas: re-wheel the compressor (cheap, +10%) or add a new compressor (expensive, +30%). Production forecasts show the extra gas lasts only 3 years — the re-wheel plus temporary well management gives the best value.
    Common mistakes
    Ignoring forecast uncertainty; skipping the “do nothing” case.
    Best practices
    Sensitivity analysis; document assumptions and basis of decision.
    🧪 Practice activity: Payback
    A heat-recovery project costs $2.4 M and saves $0.8 M/year in fuel. Simple payback?
    Answer: 2.4 ÷ 0.8 = 3 years.

    🏭 Real-World Process Engineering Scenarios

    Scenario A · Hydrate blockage after a cold snap
    Situation: A flowline blocks during unusually cold weather.
    Expert response: Safe depressuring on both sides of the plug (never one-sided — a moving plug can be a projectile), inhibitor injection, check heater and insulation, revise hydrate management philosophy and winter operating procedures.
    Scenario B · Undersized PSV found in MOC review
    Situation: A debottleneck review shows a PSV undersized for blocked outlet.
    Expert response: Immediate risk assessment and interim measures (rate limit, additional instrumented protection), then PSV replacement; audit other PSVs affected by past MOCs.
    Scenario C · Sales gas off-spec after new well
    Situation: Heating value and dew point exceed spec.
    Expert response: Adjust well mix, maximise dew-point unit chilling, update model with new composition, evaluate refrigeration upgrade.
    Scenario D · Startup of a modified compressor train
    Situation: Re-wheeled compressor ready for startup.
    Expert response: Verify new surge line in anti-surge controller, PSSR with updated C&E, staged startup with vendor, performance test to confirm new curve.

    🧭 Fundamentals

    What is the difference between a PFD and a P&ID?
    A PFD shows main equipment, main streams and key controls with stream data references; a P&ID shows every line, valve, instrument, interlock, relief device, vent and drain needed to build and operate the plant.
    What is a heat and material balance used for?
    It defines flow, composition, pressure, temperature and phase for every stream in each design case — the basis for sizing equipment, lines, valves and relief systems.
    What does a process engineer do in operations?
    Monitors performance, troubleshoots, optimises, sets operating limits, reviews MOCs, supports startups, test runs and incident investigations, and keeps process safety information current.
    What are the four principles of inherent safety?
    Minimise (smaller inventories), substitute (less hazardous materials), moderate (milder conditions), simplify (fewer opportunities for error).

    📐 Calculations & Design

    How do you size a separator?
    Gas capacity from Souders–Brown velocity with a suitable K factor for the internals; liquid capacity from residence time and droplet settling; include slugs, future cases and turndown; set levels, alarms, trips and PSV case.
    What is NPSH and why does it matter?
    Net positive suction head — the margin of suction pressure above vapour pressure. If NPSH available is below NPSH required, the pump cavitates, losing performance and damaging the impeller.
    How do you size a relief valve?
    Identify all overpressure scenarios (API 521) — blocked outlet, fire, cooling loss, control valve failure, tube rupture, etc. — calculate relief loads, select the governing case, size per API 520, and check inlet loss and back-pressure in the flare network.
    How do you check a simulation is reliable?
    Correct thermodynamic package and components, converged recycles, closed balances, physically sensible results, comparison with hand calculations and plant data, documented assumptions.
    How do you prevent hydrates?
    Keep temperature above hydrate temperature (heating, insulation), remove water (TEG/mol sieve), inject MEG or methanol, avoid unnecessary pressure; check startup and depressuring cases where JT cooling is strong.

    🧰 Operations & Troubleshooting

    Export gas water dew point is off-spec. How do you troubleshoot?
    Check gas temperature and flow vs design, lean TEG purity (reboiler temperature, stripping gas), circulation rate, contactor ΔP/foaming, glycol losses and analyser validity — then fix the cause.
    A column is flooding. What do you check?
    Feed and reflux rates vs design, ΔP profile, foaming tendency, fouling/damaged trays (gamma scan), reboiler duty, pressure — and reduce load while investigating.
    How would you approach a 15% debottleneck?
    Test run and calibrated model, then check every system at the new rate: equipment, lines, control valves, instruments, utilities, PSVs and flare — fix constraints in order and estimate costs.
    What is a test run and how do you run one?
    A planned stable period with verified instruments and extra sampling to measure performance; data is reconciled and compared with design or model to guide decisions.

    🛡️ Process Safety & Expert

    Explain HAZOP.
    A structured team review of P&IDs node by node, applying guidewords to parameters to find deviations, causes, consequences, safeguards and recommendations.
    Explain LOPA with a simple example.
    Initiating frequency × PFD of each independent protection layer = mitigated frequency; compare with target. E.g. 0.1/yr × 0.1 (alarm/operator) × 0.01 (PSV) = 1×10⁻⁴/yr; if target is 1×10⁻⁵, a SIL 1 function is needed.
    What is gas blow-by?
    When a separator loses liquid level and its level valve stays open, high-pressure gas flows to the downstream lower-pressure vessel, potentially far exceeding its relief capacity — a key HAZOP scenario.
    What lesson does Texas City (2005) give process engineers?
    Startups are high-risk; level instruments and procedures must work; flammable releases should go to closed systems/flare rather than atmospheric blowdown stacks; occupied buildings must be sited safely.
