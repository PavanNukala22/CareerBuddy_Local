    The Role & Project Controls Hiring Basics

    Before you screen a single planner or cost controller, understand what an EPC Planning & Controls Talent Specialist owns, how a requirement travels from client to candidate, and the words every project controls conversation uses.

    🧭 What does this specialist do?

    An EPC Planning & Controls Talent Specialist finds, screens and presents the people who keep a large project on time and on budget: planners and schedulers (usually working in Primavera P6), cost controllers, earned value (EVM) analysts and schedule risk analysts. You do not build schedules yourself. You make sure the person the client hires really can.

    🔎
    Source
    Find planners, cost controllers and risk analysts on LinkedIn, job boards, AACE/PMI communities, referrals and your own database.
    🧪
    Screen
    Check P6 depth, EVM understanding, cost reporting, project size, sector, certifications, rate and availability.
    📝
    Test
    Arrange a short practical exercise (build or update a schedule, read a cost report) marked by the client's controls lead.
    📤
    Submit & close
    Send an evidence-based write-up, coordinate interviews, negotiate and mobilize.
    💡
    Example. A client says: "We need a Senior Planner (P6) and a Cost Controller for our refinery expansion, both with EVM reporting, starting in 4 weeks." Your job is to turn that sentence into a short list of verified, tested, available people within the agreed turnaround time (TAT).

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Client side

    • Project controls manager: leads planners and cost controllers; usually the hiring manager.
    • Planning lead / cost lead: judges technical depth and marks practical tests.
    • Project manager: uses the reports to make decisions.
    • HR / contracts: approves rates and contracts.

    🧑‍💻 Candidate side

    • Permanent staff: employed by contractors, owners or consultancies.
    • Freelance contractors: day-rate planners and cost engineers who move between projects.
    • Consultancy staff: project controls or claims consultancies.
    • Referrals: planners know other planners.
    📝
    Tip. Ask the planning lead to share a short, anonymised practical exercise and its marking guide. It lets you test candidates the way the client would, before you submit them.
    Example: a useful planning-lead chat
    The planning lead says: "The last planner could build activities but didn't understand why float went negative after the update." You add "What does negative float tell you, and what do you do about it?" to every planner screen. Your next two submittals both pass the client interview.

    📡 Signal, not software names

    Almost every controls CV says "Primavera P6". That is a software name, not a skill level. Signal is evidence of what they did with it: schedule size, project type, decisions, problems found and fixed.

    🔴 Low signal

    "Proficient in Primavera P6 and MS Project. Responsible for planning and scheduling activities and preparing reports."

    🟢 High signal

    "Owned the 8,000-activity construction schedule for a $600M gas plant in P6. Ran weekly updates, found that late cable deliveries had moved the critical path, and proposed re-sequencing that recovered 3 weeks."

    ✅
    Rule of thumb: ask how big, which phase, what did you own, and what went wrong. Real controls people answer all four with numbers.
    Example: the four questions in action
    CV: "Cost control for EPC project."
    How big? $350M budget. Which phase? Construction. What did you own? Monthly cost report and forecast for civil and mechanical. What went wrong? Steel prices rose 18%; I flagged it as a trend early and the change was approved before it hit the forecast. That is someone who has actually done it.

    Module 02 · Industry Literacy

    Project Controls Fundamentals

    A plain-English map of how large projects are planned, measured and forecast, so every line on a controls CV has a place to live in your head.

    🎛️ What is project controls?

    Think of a long car journey. You plan the route and stops (schedule), set a fuel budget (cost), check where you are every hour (progress), predict when you will arrive (forecast) and think about traffic jams that might happen (risk). Project controls does the same for a project worth hundreds of millions of dollars.

    🗓️
    Planning & scheduling
    What happens, in what order, and when.
    💰
    Cost control
    Budget, commitments, actual spend and final cost forecast.
    📈
    Progress & EVM
    How much work is really done, and is it worth what we spent?
    🎲
    Risk & change
    What could go wrong, how likely, and how changes are approved.
    💡
    Why clients pay for good controls staff. A weak planner can hide a 3-month delay until it is too late to recover. A strong one shows it early, while there is still time to act.

    🔁 The controls cycle

    Project controls repeats the same cycle every week or month. Each role on your req list owns part of it.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row. The cycle runs every week (schedule) or month (cost).
    Example: one month of the cycle
    The planner updates P6 with progress (step 3) and sees piping is 2 weeks late (step 4). The cost controller forecasts the extra labour cost (step 5). The project manager approves overtime (step 6). Controls puts it all in the monthly report (step 7).

    🪤 Common beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "A planner and a cost controller are the same job."
    • "P6 on the CV means they can plan."
    • "MS Project experience equals P6 experience."
    • "A bigger title means a bigger schedule."

    ✅ Reality

    • Planners manage time; cost controllers manage money. Some do both, most specialise.
    • Knowing the buttons is not knowing the logic. Test it.
    • The concepts transfer, but large EPC clients usually require P6.
    • Ask for activity counts, project values and phases.
    Example: avoiding the title trap
    "Senior Project Controls Engineer" at a small firm: schedules of 300 activities. "Planner" at a major contractor: 15,000-activity integrated schedule. For a mega-project req, the Planner is the stronger match.

    Module 03 · Primavera P6 Planner Evaluation

    Evaluating Primavera P6 Planners

    What a planner really does in P6, the concepts to listen for, and a fair practical test that separates real planners from button-pushers.

    🗓️ P6 concepts in plain English

    ConceptPlain-English meaning
    WBS (Work Breakdown Structure)The project split into a tree of smaller parts: area → system → discipline.
    ActivityOne piece of work with a duration, e.g. "Install pump P-101 (5 days)".
    MilestoneA zero-duration event, e.g. "Mechanical completion".
    LogicThe links that say which activity must happen before another.
    CalendarWorking days and hours, e.g. 6 days a week, 10 hours a day.
    Data dateThe cut-off date: progress is recorded up to here; everything after is forecast.
    Total floatHow long an activity can slip without delaying the project finish.
    Critical pathThe chain of activities with the least float; any delay here delays the finish.
    BaselineA saved copy of the approved plan, used to compare progress.
    Resource loadingAdding people, hours or cost to activities, to plan workforce and cash flow.
    Example: float in daily life
    You must catch a 9:00 train. Breakfast takes 20 minutes and the walk takes 15. If you wake at 8:00, you have 25 minutes of float. If breakfast runs 30 minutes late, float becomes negative (−5): you will miss the train unless you walk faster. Planners read float the same way.

    🔗 Logic & relationships

    P6 links activities with four relationship types. A planner should explain these instantly.

    TypeMeaningExample
    FS Finish-to-StartB starts after A finishes (most common)Pour foundation → then set pump
    SS Start-to-StartB starts after A startsStart excavation → start formwork a few days later
    FF Finish-to-FinishB finishes after A finishesFinish welding → finish weld inspection
    SF Start-to-FinishB finishes after A starts (rare)New shift starts → old shift can finish

    Lags add waiting time on a link (e.g. "FS + 7 days for concrete curing"). Constraints force a date (e.g. "must finish by 1 May"). Both are legitimate, but too many of them hide the true logic.

    Example: a constraint hiding a problem
    A planner puts a "start on" constraint on "Install compressor" for 1 March. The real compressor delivery is late, but the constraint keeps the date fixed, so the schedule looks on time. A strong planner links installation to the delivery activity instead, so the delay shows up honestly.

    🪜 Schedule levels

    LevelDetailAudience
    Level 1One-page summary: key phases and milestonesOwner, executives
    Level 2Summary by area or major facilitySenior management
    Level 3Integrated control schedule across E, P and CProject management, controls
    Level 4Detailed working schedule per discipline/areaDiscipline leads, site supervisors
    Level 5Very detailed short-term look-aheadForemen, daily work planning
    Example: level tells you seniority
    A planner who only updates Level 5 look-aheads is usually junior to mid-level. A planner who builds and maintains the Level 3 integrated schedule and presents the critical path to management is senior.

    ✅ Schedule quality checks

    Experienced planners check schedule health with standard tests. A widely used set is the DCMA 14-Point Assessment, and tools such as Deltek Acumen Fuse automate these checks. You do not need to run them; you need to hear whether the candidate does.

    CheckWhat it catches
    Missing logicActivities with no predecessor or successor ("open ends")
    Leads & lagsToo many hidden waiting times
    Hard constraintsForced dates that override real logic
    High floatActivities with unrealistic slack, often missing links
    Negative floatForecast is later than a required date
    High durationActivities too long to control properly
    Critical path testDelaying a critical activity really moves the finish date
    Example: a strong quality answer
    "Before each baseline I run a DCMA check in Acumen. On the last project 9% of activities had open ends; I fixed them before submitting the baseline to the owner." The candidate names a method, a number and an action.

    💬 Evaluating a P6 planner

    AskStrong answer containsWeak answer
    "Describe the biggest schedule you owned."Activity count, project value, phase, level, update frequency"A big refinery schedule."
    "How do you do a weekly update?"Collect progress, set data date, update remaining durations, reschedule, check critical path and float changes, report"I put in the percentages."
    "Float went negative after the update. What next?"Find the driving path, explain the cause, propose recovery options (re-sequencing, extra resources)"I add a constraint to fix the date."
    "When do you re-baseline?"Only after an approved major scope or plan change, following the change procedure"Whenever we are late."
    "How do you check schedule quality?"DCMA-style checks, open ends, constraints, lags, Acumen or similar"The software checks it."
    ⚠️
    Red flags: "I fix delays with constraints", "I re-baseline every month", cannot say how many activities, or every example is "updating percentages" with no analysis.

    📝 The practical test

    A short, fair exercise is the best evidence for planner roles. Agree it with the client's planning lead and use the same test for every candidate.

    45-minute planner exercise (example)Given: a 40-activity P6 file (XER) and a progress sheet for week 12

1. Set the data date and enter the progress          (10 min)
2. Reschedule and identify the new critical path     (10 min)
3. Find 3 schedule quality issues and fix one        (10 min)
4. Write 5 lines for the PM: status, cause, recovery (15 min)

Marked by the client planning lead out of 20, using a written guide.
    ✅
    Fairness rules: same file, same time, same marking guide for everyone. Keep it short and relevant to the real job, and tell candidates in advance what software they will need.
    Example: test result in a submittal
    "Planner exercise: 17/20. Correctly identified that the cable-pulling path became critical; found an open end and a hard constraint; wrote a clear 5-line status note." This line is often what gets the interview.

    Module 04 · Earned Value Management

    Earned Value Management (EVM)

    The three numbers behind every controls report, the formulas in plain English, and how to tell whether a candidate really uses EVM or only mentions it.

    📊 What is earned value?

    Spending money does not prove progress. EVM compares three numbers at the same date:

    📅
    PV · Planned Value
    The budget value of work we planned to have done by now.
    🏗️
    EV · Earned Value
    The budget value of work we have actually done.
    💸
    AC · Actual Cost
    What we have actually spent to do it.
    🎯
    BAC · Budget at Completion
    The total approved budget for all the work.
    Example: painting a fence
    You budget $1,000 to paint 10 fence panels ($100 each) over 10 days. On day 5 you planned 5 panels (PV = $500). You have painted 4 (EV = $400) and spent $480 (AC = $480). You are behind (400 < 500) and over budget (spent 480 for 400 worth of work).

    📈 The S-curve

    Plot the three numbers over time and you get the classic S-curve: slow start, fast middle, slow finish. Here is a project with a $100M budget, reported at month 6.

    Cumulative $M by month. Hover a point for its value. At month 6: PV $57M, EV $50M, AC $56M.
    Month12345678910
    PV ($M)3816284257718393100
    EV ($M)2613233650––––
    AC ($M)2715264056––––

    🧮 The formulas

    MeasureFormulaMonth-6 exampleMeaning
    Schedule variance (SV)EV − PV50 − 57 = −7$7M of planned work not yet done
    Cost variance (CV)EV − AC50 − 56 = −6$6M more spent than the work is worth
    SPIEV ÷ PV50 ÷ 57 = 0.88Progressing at 88% of the planned rate
    CPIEV ÷ AC50 ÷ 56 = 0.89Getting $0.89 of work for every $1 spent
    EAC (simple CPI method)BAC ÷ CPI100 ÷ 0.893 ≈ 112Forecast final cost about $112M
    ETCEAC − AC112 − 56 = 56About $56M still to spend
    VACBAC − EAC100 − 112 = −12Forecast overrun of about $12M
    📝
    Memory trick: EV always comes first. Variance = EV minus something; index = EV divided by something. Negative variance or index below 1.0 = bad.

    🚦 Reading the indices

    ResultSPI saysCPI says
    Above 1.0Ahead of scheduleUnder budget
    Exactly 1.0On scheduleOn budget
    Below 1.0Behind scheduleOver budget
    ⚠️
    An advanced point to listen for. Classic SPI is based on money, so near the end of a late project EV catches up with PV and SPI drifts back towards 1.0, even though the finish is late. Strong EVM candidates know this and check the schedule itself (or use "earned schedule" methods) as well.
    Example: explaining the numbers to a PM
    Candidate: "SPI 0.88 and CPI 0.89 mean we are both late and over budget. Piping productivity is the main cause. If nothing changes, we finish around $112M. I recommend a productivity review and re-sequencing of the pipe racks." Numbers, cause, forecast and action: owned EVM.

    📏 Measuring progress

    EV is only as good as the progress measurement behind it. On EPC projects, progress is usually measured with agreed rules of credit: fixed percentages earned at each step.

    Piping step (example rules)Credit earned
    Spool received at site10%
    Erected in place40%
    Welded30%
    Tested and reinstated20%
    Example: why rules of credit matter
    Without rules, a supervisor says "we are about 70% done" from feeling. With rules, 1,000 metres of pipe erected and 600 welded gives an exact, auditable number. Ask EVM candidates: "How was EV measured on your project?" A strong answer describes rules of credit or weighted milestones.

    💬 Screening EVM roles

    AskStrong answer containsWeak answer
    "How did you calculate EV?"Rules of credit or weighted milestones, quantities, link to the baseline"From the percent complete in P6."
    "Your CPI is 0.85. What do you do?"Find which areas drive it, explain causes, update the forecast, propose actions"Report it to the PM."
    "How do you forecast EAC?"Several methods (CPI-based, bottom-up), compare and explain the chosen one"Budget plus some extra."
    "What EVM standard or system did you work under?"Client procedures, ANSI/EIA-748 style requirements, monthly EVM reportingCannot name any
    ✅
    Senior signal: talks about the performance measurement baseline, explains the SPI drift problem, and links EVM numbers to real decisions.

    Module 05 · Cost Controller Vetting

    Vetting Cost Controllers

    How project money is tracked from budget to final forecast, and the questions that reveal whether a cost controller can really keep a large budget under control.

    💰 What a cost controller does

    A cost controller (or cost engineer) keeps track of every dollar on the project and, most importantly, predicts the final cost early so management can act.

    Control budgetCost breakdown structureCommitmentsActualsAccrualsForecast / EACTrends & changesCash flowMonthly cost report
    Example: a cost controller's month
    Week 1: collect commitments from procurement and actuals from finance. Week 2: update forecasts with discipline leads. Week 3: review trends and pending changes. Week 4: issue the monthly cost report showing budget, committed, spent and forecast for each area.

    📚 The cost vocabulary

    TermMeaningExample
    Original budgetThe budget approved at project start$100M
    Current (control) budgetOriginal budget plus approved changes$100M + $8M approved = $108M
    CommitmentMoney promised through signed POs and subcontracts, not yet all paid"$60M committed"
    Actual costMoney already invoiced or paid"$40M spent"
    AccrualCost of work done but not yet invoiced"$2M accrued for September site work"
    TrendAn early warning of a possible cost change"Steel prices up: trend of $1.5M"
    EAC / forecastExpected final cost"EAC $112M"
    CBS / code of accountsThe structure used to classify all costs"Code 4200 = mechanical labour"
    Example: why accruals matter
    A subcontractor finished $2M of work in September but invoices in November. Without an accrual, September's actual cost looks $2M too low and the project looks healthier than it is. A good cost controller always asks about accruals.

    🔄 Budget, change & forecast

    Budgets change only through a formal change management process. Strong cost controllers protect that process.

    StageWhat happensEffect on numbers
    TrendPossible change identified earlyMay be shown in the forecast as a risk
    Pending changeChange request raised, awaiting approvalShown separately; not yet in the budget
    Approved changeSigned change order or variationAdded to the current budget
    Example: calculating the current budget
    Original budget $100M. Approved changes $8M. Pending changes $5M. Current budget = $108M. The $5M pending is reported separately until it is approved. A candidate who adds the pending $5M to the budget is making a classic mistake.

    🛡️ Contingency vs management reserve

    🧰 Contingency

    Money inside the project budget for known risks and uncertainty within the agreed scope (e.g. quantities growing). Usually controlled by the project manager and drawn down as risks happen.

    🏦 Management reserve

    Money held outside the project budget by senior management for unforeseen events. Released only with management approval.

    Example: the right drawdown
    Rock is found during excavation, a risk listed in the risk register. The extra $0.8M comes from contingency. A new government regulation nobody foresaw adds $3M: management may release management reserve.

    💬 Vetting questions

    AskStrong answer containsWeak answer
    "What budget size did you control?"Value, scope (whole project or disciplines), phase, contract type"A big budget."
    "Explain commitments, actuals and accruals."Clear differences with an exampleMixes them up
    "How do you build an EAC?"Actuals + accruals + remaining commitments + estimate for uncommitted work + risks/trends"Budget times a factor."
    "Tell me about a cost overrun you spotted early."Trend raised, numbers, action taken, result"Finance found it."
    "Which systems did you use?"ERP (e.g. SAP), cost tools (e.g. EcoSys, Unifier), Excel, Power BI"Excel only" for a mega-project req
    ⚠️
    Watch out: "Cost controller" is sometimes used for accounts or invoice-processing roles. Ask whether they produced forecasts and cost reports, not only processed invoices.

    Module 06 · Schedule Risk Analyst Recruitment

    Recruiting Schedule Risk Analysts

    The rarest controls profile. Learn what probabilistic schedule analysis is, the words that prove real experience, and how to find these specialists.

    🎲 What is schedule risk analysis?

    A normal schedule gives one finish date. Reality is uncertain: activities may take longer, and risks may happen. A schedule risk analyst uses probability to answer: "How likely are we to finish by this date?"

    Example: the commute
    Your commute usually takes 30 minutes, but traffic varies from 25 to 50. If you need to arrive by 9:00 with 80% confidence, you leave earlier than if you only want a 50% chance. Owners do the same with project dates and budgets.

    ⚙️ How a QSRA works

    A Quantitative Schedule Risk Analysis (QSRA) follows these steps:

    1. Check the schedule quality first. Bad logic gives meaningless results.
    2. Add uncertainty with three-point estimates (optimistic, most likely, pessimistic) on durations.
    3. Add risk events from the risk register, with probability and impact (e.g. "30% chance of a 6-week compressor delay").
    4. Run a Monte Carlo simulation: the software runs the schedule thousands of times with random values.
    5. Report results: a probability curve, P50/P80 dates, and a "tornado" chart of the biggest drivers.
    Example: a risk event
    Risk register: "Heavy-lift crane availability", probability 40%, impact 3–8 weeks. The analyst links it to the "Lift reactor" activity. In some simulation runs it happens, in others it does not, and the results show how much it pushes the finish date.

    📐 P50, P80 & merge bias

    TermMeaningExample
    P5050% chance of finishing on or before this date"P50 mechanical completion: 10 March"
    P8080% chance of finishing on or before this date"P80: 28 April", often used for funding decisions
    Deterministic dateThe single date from the normal schedule"P6 says 1 February"
    Tornado chartRanks which risks or activities drive the delay most"Compressor delivery is the top driver"
    Merge biasWhen several parallel paths must all finish before the next step, the chance that at least one is late is higher, so the realistic date moves later"Five systems must all be ready before start-up"
    Example: why P6 dates are optimistic
    P6 says 1 February. The QSRA says the deterministic date has only a 15% chance of being met; P50 is 10 March and P80 is 28 April. The owner sets the public target at P80 and keeps the gap as schedule contingency. A risk analyst who can explain this clearly to executives is very valuable.

    🧰 Risk tools

    📊
    Primavera Risk Analysis
    Oracle's tool, formerly known as Pertmaster; works with P6 schedules.
    🎯
    Safran Risk
    Integrated schedule and cost risk analysis.
    🔍
    Deltek Acumen Risk
    Schedule quality plus risk analysis in one suite.
    📈
    @Risk / Excel models
    Monte Carlo add-ins, often used for cost risk.
    ⚠️
    The concepts transfer between tools. Ask the client: "Would you accept a strong Safran Risk analyst if your project uses Primavera Risk Analysis?" A "yes" can double your talent pool.

    🔬 Worked example

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Calibration, Verification & Metrics

    What separates a good recruiter from a trusted controls talent partner: aligning with the controls lead, reading the market, catching inflated CVs and proving results with numbers.

    🎯 Calibrating with the controls lead

    1. Pick 3 profiles: one clear yes, one clear no, one maybe.
    2. Ask the controls lead for a 1-line verdict on each.
    3. In a 20-minute call ask "What made the maybe a maybe?"
    4. Update your scorecard and test, and repeat after the first 5 interviews.
    💡
    Example. The lead says the "maybe" had only updated schedules, never built a baseline. You add "Have you built and submitted a baseline for owner approval?" to your screen, and interview rejections drop.

    📈 Market & rates

    Never quote numbers from memory; use current data from your firm, job postings and recent placements.

    FactorPushes rate upPushes rate down
    SpecialismSchedule risk, forensic delay analysis, EVM leadsJunior planners, data entry
    ScaleMega-projects, integrated schedulesSmall, single-discipline projects
    CertificationAACE PSP / CCP / DRMP, verifiedNone
    LocationRemote sites, rotation, short noticeHome-office roles
    Example: being honest about budget
    A client wants a senior QSRA analyst at a mid-level planner rate. You show three recent placements and two current postings. The client either raises the budget or changes the scope to "planner with QSRA support". Either way, you save weeks of wasted sourcing.

    🕵️ Verification & red flags

    Warning signHow to verify
    Huge schedules claimed with very few years' experienceAsk what they personally owned vs supported; take a reference from the planning lead
    Every CV bullet copies software feature namesUse the practical test
    Certificate cannot be foundCheck the issuer's verification service; ask the candidate politely
    Cannot explain their own reported numbersAsk: "What did SPI 0.9 mean on that project, and what did you do?"
    Shares a client's confidential schedule as a "sample"Do not use it; treat it as a judgement concern and tell the candidate
    📝
    Be fair. Supporting a large schedule is real experience too. The goal is accuracy about the level of ownership, not catching people out. Use the same checks for everyone.

    📊 Metrics that matter

    MetricFormulaExample
    Test pass ratePassed tests ÷ tests taken6 ÷ 10 = 60%
    Submittal-to-interviewInterviews ÷ submittals5 ÷ 8 = 63%
    Interview-to-offerOffers ÷ interviews2 ÷ 5 = 40%

    Example: diagnosing a month
    Test pass rate is only 25%. Your phone screen is letting through people with P6 on the CV but little real planning. Add two logic questions (negative float, re-baselining) to the call and re-measure next month.

    📖 Full case study

    Req: Mega-project controls team: 1 planning lead, 4 planners, 3 cost controllers, 1 schedule risk analyst. Start within 6 weeks.

    1. Intake: separate brief per role; P6 and EcoSys mandatory; Safran or Primavera Risk acceptable.
    2. Source: separate Boolean searches; 260 profiles; 45 shortlisted by scorecard.
    3. Screen: 26 calls; 18 invited to tests; 12 pass.
    4. Risk analyst: no pure analysts available; a senior planner with 3 QSRAs is accepted after the client interview.
    5. Submit: 12 candidates with test scores within 10 days.
    6. Result: 9 offers, 9 acceptances; all started by week 6.
    ✅
    Why it worked: one brief per role, a fair practical test, honest use of adjacent profiles for the scarce role, and test scores in every write-up.
