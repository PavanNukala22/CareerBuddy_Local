    The Role & Camp Basics

    Before you allocate a single bed, understand what a Camp & Site Services Coordinator owns, how a camp supports a project from first arrival to final departure, and the words used in every camp meeting.

    🧭 What does this role do?

    Large EPC projects are often far from towns. Hundreds or thousands of workers need somewhere to sleep, eat, wash, rest and travel to work. A Camp & Site Services Coordinator makes sure that happens safely, fairly and on budget, every day of the project.

    The role has four core skills, and this course covers each one in depth:

    🏕️
    Industrial camp management
    Running daily camp operations: housekeeping, utilities, maintenance, safety and security.
    🤝
    Worker welfare governance
    Setting and checking standards for living conditions, rights, health and complaints.
    🍽️
    Catering & transport logistics oversight
    Making sure meals are safe and sufficient and buses run safely and on time.
    🛏️
    Site accommodation allocation
    Giving every person the right room, fairly, and tracking every bed.
    💡
    Example. A gas plant in a desert has 900 workers on rotation and 600 beds. Every evening you must know: who is arriving tomorrow, which beds are free, how many meals to cook, and how many buses leave at 05:15.

    🔄 The camp lifecycle

    A camp follows the project. It is planned before the first worker arrives and closed after the last one leaves.

    "Occupancy at 96%; 40 arrivals Thursday on 28/28; FM says Block D AC down; two grievances on food."

    👥 Who you work with

    🏗️ Project side

    • Project / Site Manager: sets budget and priorities.
    • HSE team: fire safety, food safety, health and heat.
    • HR & mobilization: arrival and departure lists.
    • Construction supervisors: shift times and crew lists.

    🏢 Client & service side

    • Client / lenders: welfare standards and audits.
    • Catering contractor: meals and mess hall.
    • Transport contractor: buses, drivers, schedules.
    • Workers & welfare committee: the people you serve.
    📝
    Tip. Walk the camp every day at meal times. You will learn more in 30 minutes in the mess hall than in a week of reports.
    Example: what a walk reveals
    At breakfast you notice a 25-minute queue. Workers then rush to the 05:15 bus without eating. You ask the caterer to open a second serving line and a grab-and-go counter. Queue time drops to 8 minutes.

    💚 Welfare is a project result

    Tired, hungry or unhappy workers make more mistakes, have more accidents and leave early. A well-run camp directly supports safety, productivity and retention.

    🔴 Poor camp

    Hot rooms, noisy blocks, long queues, late buses. Result: poor sleep, fatigue incidents, 12% of workers leave in month one.

    🟢 Good camp

    Cool, quiet rooms, fast meals, reliable buses, complaints answered. Result: fewer incidents, 3% early leavers, better reputation for re-hiring.

    ✅
    Rule of thumb: every worker should have a safe bed, good food, a reliable ride and a voice. If any one is missing, fix it first.
    Example: the four basics in action
    Weekly check: Bed all blocks cooled and clean. Food no food safety issues, menu survey 4.1/5. Ride 98% of buses on time. Voice 9 grievances raised, 8 closed within 7 days. A clear picture of camp health.

    Module 02 · Camp Literacy

    Camp Fundamentals

    What a project camp is made of, how to work out how many beds you need, and the standards a good camp must meet.

    🏕️ Types of camps

    TypeWhat it isUsed when
    Modular campFactory-built units assembled on site; removed laterMost remote EPC projects
    Permanent campBuilt to last; may serve the plant's operators laterLong projects, or owner wants to keep it
    Leased housing / hotelsRooms rented in a nearby townProjects near towns; managers; overflow
    Satellite (fly) campSmall temporary camp near a remote work frontPipelines, power lines moving along a route
    Example: mixing camp types
    A pipeline project uses a main modular camp for 800 workers, two satellite camps of 80 beds that move every few months along the route, and leased hotel rooms in town for managers and short visits.

    🏘️ Camp facilities

    Think of a camp as a small town. It needs homes, food, water, power, health care, rest areas and rules.

    🛏️
    Accommodation blocks
    Rooms, toilets, showers, laundry.
    🍽️
    Kitchen & mess hall
    Food storage, cooking, dining.
    💧
    Utilities
    Water, power, sewage treatment, waste.
    🏥
    Clinic
    First aid, nurse or doctor, medical evacuation plan.
    ⚽
    Recreation
    Gym, sports, TV room, Wi-Fi, prayer rooms.
    🛡️
    Security & safety
    Gate control, fire systems, muster points.
    Example: a missing facility
    A new camp opens without enough washing machines: 1 for every 60 workers. Clothes pile up and workers wash by hand late at night. Adding machines and a laundry service improves sleep and hygiene within a week.

    🧮 Sizing a camp

    Not every worker is on site at the same time, because of rotation. Use the on-site factor:

    formulaOn-site factor = days on ÷ (days on + days off)

Beds needed   = total workforce × on-site factor + buffer (often 5–10%)
    RotationOn-site factor900 workers → on site
    28/2828 ÷ 56 = 1/2450
    56/2856 ÷ 84 = 2/3600
    42/2142 ÷ 63 = 2/3600
    Resident (no rotation)1900
    💡
    Why a buffer? Rotations overlap at changeover, visitors arrive, and rooms go out of service for repairs. A 5–10% buffer stops you running out of beds on changeover days.
    Example: a full calculation
    Workforce 900 on 56/28 → 900 × 2/3 = 600 on site. Add a 10% buffer → 660 beds. If the camp has 600 beds, you need 60 extra (hotel rooms or a temporary block) before the peak.

    📏 Accommodation standards

    Standards come from local law, the client's requirements and, on many internationally financed projects, guidance such as the IFC/EBRD paper "Workers' accommodation: processes and standards". They cover space per person, people per room, ventilation, temperature, sanitation, privacy and safety.

    GradeTypical room (illustrative)
    Senior managersSingle room, en-suite
    Engineers & supervisorsSingle room, en-suite or shared bathroom
    ForemenSingle or twin room
    Craft workersTwin or small shared rooms, within the agreed maximum
    ⚠️
    Always use the project's written standard. Room sizes, maximum occupants and facility ratios differ by client and country. Never add beds beyond the standard to solve a shortage.
    Example: a standard in practice
    The client standard says craft rooms hold at most 2 people with lockable personal storage. During the peak, a supervisor asks to "add a third bed for two weeks". You refuse and arrange 20 hotel rooms instead, keeping the camp compliant.

    🗂️ Camp organisation

    RoleResponsibility
    Camp Manager / Camp BossOverall camp operations and contractors
    Camp & Site Services CoordinatorAllocation, welfare, catering and transport oversight, reports
    Accommodation / front desk staffCheck-ins, keys, room register
    Catering manager (contractor)Menus, kitchen, food safety
    Transport supervisor (contractor)Buses, drivers, schedules
    Welfare officerGrievances, surveys, worker support
    Example: who handles what
    A broken shower → maintenance via the FM contractor. A room swap request → front desk under the allocation policy. A complaint about a supervisor in camp → welfare officer through the grievance process. Knowing the right owner gets things fixed faster.

    🪤 Common beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "Beds needed = total workforce."
    • "Camp is just about beds."
    • "Contractors run the camp, so it's not my problem."

    ✅ Reality

    • Use the on-site factor and a buffer.
    • Food, transport, health, rest and fair treatment all count.
    • You oversee contractors and are accountable for results.
    Example: the "total workforce" trap
    A new coordinator orders 1,200 beds for a 1,200-person workforce on 28/28. Only about 600 are ever on site. The project pays for 500+ empty beds for a year. The on-site factor would have avoided it.

    Example: a daily camp report

    💧 Utilities & maintenance

    Power & ACGenerator fuel, AC faultsHot rooms mean poor sleep and fatigue
    SewageTreatment plant working, no overflowsHealth and environmental risk

    Set priority levels for repair requests:

    PriorityExampleTarget (illustrative)
    UrgentNo water, AC failure in heat, electrical hazardWithin 4 hours
    HighBroken shower, door lockWithin 24 hours
    RoutinePaint, loose shelfWithin 7 days
    Example: measuring maintenance
    200 repair requests this month; 180 closed within target. Closure rate = 180 ÷ 200 = 90%. The late ones were all AC parts, so you ask the FM contractor to keep spare compressors on site.

    🔥 Camp safety & security

    • Fire safety: working alarms, extinguishers, clear exits, regular fire drills, no cooking in rooms.
    • Food safety: kitchen hygiene, temperature control, food handler health checks.
    • Health: clinic access, heat stress controls, pest control, disease outbreak plans.
    • Security: gate control, ID badges, visitor rules, lighting, safe walkways at night.
    • Emergency response: muster points, head counts, evacuation and medical evacuation plans.
    Example: a fire drill
    A night-time drill shows Block F takes 9 minutes to muster against a 5-minute target. One exit was blocked by stored mattresses. You clear it, brief the block leaders and repeat the drill: 4 minutes.

    📑 Managing FM contractors

    Catering, cleaning, maintenance and transport are usually done by contractors. You manage them through a clear scope, KPIs and regular reviews.

    ServiceExample KPI
    HousekeepingRooms cleaned on schedule; inspection score ≥ 90%
    MaintenanceRepairs closed within target ≥ 90%
    CateringFood safety audit score; satisfaction survey ≥ 4/5
    TransportOn-time departures ≥ 98%; zero preventable incidents
    Example: a monthly contractor review
    The cleaning contractor's inspection score falls from 92% to 81%. In the review, they explain 6 cleaners left. You agree a recovery plan: 6 replacements in 2 weeks and daily spot checks until the score is back above 90%.

    💡

    Example: an inspection finding
    Room C-112 has a portable cooking stove. You remove it, explain the fire risk, record it, and ask the caterer to extend late-night snack hours, because workers were cooking after missing dinner.

    🔬 Worked example

    situationSummer heat. 3 AC units fail in Block D (48 workers).
Temperature in rooms reaches 35 °C. Night shift sleeps there during the day.
    1. Treat as urgent: heat and poor sleep cause fatigue incidents.
    2. Move night-shift workers to cool spare rooms or the buffer block today.
    3. FM contractor repairs within 4 hours or brings portable units.
    4. Inform HSE and supervisors; record it in the daily report.
    ✅
    Result: night shift sleeps in cool rooms the same day, AC repaired next morning, and spare compressors are now stored on site.

    Module 04 · Worker Welfare Governance

    Worker Welfare Governance

    Welfare is more than kindness. It is a set of standards, checks and reports that protect workers and the project. Learn how to run it.

    🏛️ What "welfare governance" means

    Governance means having clear rules, owners and checks. For welfare, that means: written standards for how workers live and are treated, people responsible for each standard, regular checks, and action when something is wrong.

    📜
    Standards
    Written rules: rooms, food, hours of rest, rights.
    👤
    Owners
    A named person responsible for each area.
    🔍
    Checks
    Inspections, surveys, audits, grievance data.
    🛠️
    Action
    Corrective plans with dates, verified as closed.
    Example: governance in one line
    Standard: "Drinking water tested weekly." Owner: camp HSE officer. Check: test record reviewed every Monday. Action: if a test fails, stop use, supply bottled water, treat and re-test.

    📏 Welfare standards

    AreaWhat the standard covers
    Living conditionsSpace, ventilation, temperature, sanitation, privacy, storage
    Food & waterSafe drinking water, enough nutritious food, dietary and religious needs
    RestRest days, quiet hours, separation of day and night shift
    RightsWorkers keep their own passports and documents; freedom of movement; no recruitment fees
    HealthClinic access, heat stress controls, mental health support
    VoiceGrievance mechanism, welfare committee, no retaliation
    ⚠️
    Passport retention and recruitment fees are serious breaches. The ILO treats document retention as an indicator of forced labour. Escalate any case immediately under company policy.
    Example: finding a rights issue
    During a room inspection, workers say their subcontractor supervisor keeps their passports "for safety". You escalate it the same day. The passports are returned within 24 hours, and lockable personal storage is confirmed in every room.

    📮 The grievance mechanism

    Workers need a safe, simple and trusted way to raise problems, and to see that problems get fixed.

    1. Receive: several channels (box, phone line, welfare officer, app), in workers' languages, including anonymous options.
    2. Log: record every grievance with a number and date.
    3. Acknowledge: confirm receipt quickly (for example within 2 days).
    4. Investigate and act: route to the right owner (HR, catering, transport).
    5. Respond and close: tell the worker the outcome; close only when resolved.
    6. Learn: look for patterns every month.
    📝
    No retaliation. If workers fear punishment for complaining, they stop complaining and problems grow in silence.
    Example: a grievance outside camp scope
    A worker complains about unpaid overtime. This is not a camp issue, but you still log it, keep it confidential, route it to HR and payroll, and track it to closure. The worker sees that the system works.

    🩺 Health & wellbeing

    🌡️ Physical

    • Heat stress plans: cool rest areas, water, rest breaks
    • Hygiene and pest control
    • Outbreak plans (food poisoning, flu)
    • Clinic and medical evacuation

    🧠 Mental

    • Long time away from family; reliable Wi-Fi and calls home
    • Recreation, sport and social events
    • Confidential support or counselling access
    • Watch for isolation, conflict and fatigue
    Example: a small change, big effect
    Survey: workers' top complaint is weak Wi-Fi that makes video calls home impossible. Upgrading the network costs less than replacing a handful of early leavers. Satisfaction rises from 3.1 to 4.0 out of 5.

    🤲 A fair and respectful camp

    • Religion and culture: prayer spaces, adjusted meal times during fasting periods, suitable food.
    • Women workers: separate, secure accommodation and facilities, and safe reporting channels.
    • Equal standards: rooms and services follow grade and policy, never favouritism.
    • Code of conduct: clear rules on behaviour, harassment, alcohol and visitors, applied to everyone.
    Example: planning for a fasting month
    Before a fasting month, you agree with the caterer: pre-dawn meals, evening meals at sunset, packed meals for workers on site at sunset, and adjusted bus times. Workers who are not fasting keep their normal meal service.

    📊 Audits & reporting

    Clients, lenders and company HSE teams audit camps against the agreed standards. Be ready with evidence: registers, inspection records, test results, grievance logs and corrective action plans.

    FindingCorrective actionOwnerDueStatus
    AC faulty in 3 blocksRepair; portable units meanwhileFM contractor15 OctIn progress
    Grievance box only in one languageAdd notices in 4 worker languagesWelfare officer20 OctClosed
    Fire drill overdue in Block FHold drill; set monthly scheduleCamp HSE14 OctClosed
    Example: closing an audit finding properly
    "AC repaired" is not enough evidence. You attach the repair job number, a temperature reading after repair, and a photo. The auditor accepts the closure on first review.

    Module 05 · Catering & Transport Logistics Oversight

    Catering & Transport

    Every day, hundreds of meals and dozens of bus trips must happen safely and on time. Learn to plan them, check them and fix problems fast.

    🍽️ Catering oversight

    You do not cook. You make sure the catering contractor delivers what the contract and the workers need.

    AreaWhat you oversee
    MenusVariety, nutrition for heavy work, dietary and religious options, a menu cycle (for example 4 weeks)
    QuantityEnough food for every shift, including night shift and late returns
    ServiceMess hall opening times, queue times, packed lunches
    HygieneKitchen cleanliness, food handler health, pest control
    FeedbackSurveys, comment cards, monthly food committee
    Cost & wasteCost per meal, food waste levels
    Example: a menu fix
    Workers from one region rate food 2.5/5. You meet their representatives and agree two regional dishes per week with the caterer. Next month their score is 3.9/5, and food waste falls because more food is eaten.

    🧼 Food safety

    Food poisoning can stop a project: dozens of sick workers in a single day. Caterers usually work to a food safety system based on HACCP (Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points): identifying the points where food can become unsafe and controlling them.

    ✅ What good looks like

    • Fridge and freezer temperatures logged daily
    • Hot food kept hot, cold food kept cold
    • Raw and cooked food stored apart
    • Food samples kept for each meal (for investigations)
    • Handwashing and health checks for food handlers

    🚩 Red flags

    • Cooked food left out at room temperature
    • Missing temperature records
    • Leftovers re-served for days
    • Pests in storage areas
    • Sick staff handling food
    Example: responding to a food complaint
    Several workers feel sick after dinner. You: get them to the clinic, inform HSE, ask the caterer for the retained food samples from that meal, and stop serving the suspect dish until tests and the investigation are complete.

    🔢 Meal planning & counts

    Plan meals from the expected occupancy and shift pattern, then check against actual counts.

    daily meal plan exampleOn site tonight: 600 (day shift 450, night shift 150)

    💡
    Share arrivals and departures with the caterer every day. A 40-person arrival that the kitchen does not know about means 40 people without dinner.
    Example: comparing plan and actual
    Planned 1,800 meals; served 1,690; 110 portions wasted. The waste was mainly breakfast on rest days. You ask the caterer to reduce weekend breakfast batches by 15%, saving cost without anyone going hungry.

    🚌 Transport planning

    Buses must get each shift to site before start time and back after finish.

    formulaBuses needed = workers per trip ÷ seats per bus  (round up) + spare bus(es)
    ShiftWorkersBus seatsCalculationBuses
    Day54050540 ÷ 50 = 10.8 → 11, + 1 spare12
    Night15050150 ÷ 50 = 3, + 1 spare4
    Example: staggering departures
    All 12 buses leaving at 05:15 cause a 30-minute jam at the site gate. You stagger them in three waves (05:00, 05:10, 05:20) by work area. Gate time drops to 8 minutes and work starts on time.

    🛣️ Transport safety

    Road incidents are a major risk on remote projects. Oversee:

    • Drivers: licence checks, training, medical fitness, limits on driving hours, fatigue checks.
    • Vehicles: daily pre-trip inspections, maintenance records, seat belts for every seat.
    • Journeys: journey management plans for long or night trips, speed limits, approved routes.
    • Passengers: seat belt rule, head counts, no standing.
    • Monitoring: in-vehicle monitoring (speed, harsh braking) where available.
    Example: preventing fatigue
    Monitoring shows a night bus drifting across lanes at 02:30. The driver had worked a double shift. You enforce driving hour limits with the transport contractor, add a fatigue check before night trips, and add a relief driver to the rota.

    🔬 Worked example

    situationTomorrow: 60 new workers arrive at 18:00 on 2 flights, 30 depart at 06:00.
Night shift grows from 150 to 190 from next week.
    1. Catering: +60 dinners tomorrow; adjust night meals from next week (190 per meal).
    2. Transport: 2 buses for airport arrivals; 1 bus for departures; night shift needs 190 ÷ 50 = 3.8 → 4 buses + 1 spare = 5.
    3. Accommodation: rooms for 60 ready and inspected; night-shift block expanded by 40 beds.
    ✅
    Result: arrivals checked in and fed on the first night, departures on time, and the night shift has enough buses and meals from day one.

    Module 06 · Site Accommodation Allocation

    Accommodation Allocation

    Giving every person the right bed, fairly and safely, and always knowing who is in which room.

    📐 Allocation rules

    A written allocation policy stops arguments and favouritism. Typical factors:

    FactorWhy it matters
    GradeRoom type follows the standard for each grade
    ShiftDay and night workers in separate blocks so night workers can sleep
    Crew / employerCrews near each other simplifies buses and communication
    GenderSeparate, secure areas for women
    Medical needsGround floor or near facilities when needed
    FairnessSame rules for everyone; exceptions approved and recorded
    Example: allocating a new crew
    A 24-person electrical crew arrives on day shift: 1 supervisor, 2 foremen, 21 electricians. Supervisor: single room in Block A. Foremen: single rooms in Block C. Electricians: 11 twin rooms in Block C, day-shift wing, plus 1 single. Same bus, same block, quiet nights.

    📝 Booking & check-in

    1. Pre-arrival booking: HR or mobilization sends name, ID, employer, grade, shift and dates.
    2. Room assignment: made under the policy before arrival; room inspected and ready.
    3. Check-in: ID check, key or card, register entry.
    4. Camp induction: rules, fire exits, muster point, meal and bus times, grievance channels.
    💡
    No booking, no bed? Never leave a tired worker without a bed at 22:00. Keep a few buffer rooms for unbooked arrivals, then fix the booking process the next morning.
    Example: an unbooked arrival
    Eight workers arrive whose bookings were never sent. You place them in buffer rooms that night, then find the gap: a subcontractor was not on the booking email list. You add them and the issue does not repeat.

    📋 The room register

    The room register (often in a camp management system) is the single source of truth. It matters for planning, billing and above all emergency head counts.

    Example: why accuracy matters
    During a fire alarm, the register shows 46 people in Block E, but the muster count is 44. Two workers had swapped rooms without telling the front desk. Searchers spent 15 minutes checking empty rooms. Now every swap must be approved and recorded.

    🔁 Rotation & bed sharing

    On rotation projects, two workers with opposite rotations can be assigned the same bed at different times (often called back-to-back allocation). This saves beds, but only works with clear rules.

    ✅ Acceptable when

    • The client standard allows it
    • Full room clean and fresh linen between users
    • Each worker has their own lockable storage
    • Changeover dates are tracked in the register

    ❌ Never

    • Two people using one bed at the same time
    • Shift workers "hot-bedding" in the same day
    • No cleaning between occupants
    Example: a smooth changeover
    Worker A (28/28) checks out on 30 Oct at 06:00. Housekeeping cleans the room and changes linen by 14:00. Worker B checks in at 19:00. Each has a separate locker, so personal items stay secure.

    🔀 Changes & requests

    RequestGood response
    "I want to move rooms."Check the reason and policy; approve and record, or explain why not
    "My roommate snores / we argue."Try to resolve; move if possible; involve welfare if it is conflict
    "I moved to night shift."Move to the night-shift block as soon as possible
    "My manager wants a better room."Apply grade rules; exceptions need recorded approval
    Example: a fair answer
    A foreman asks for a single en-suite room reserved for engineers. Policy says foremen get single rooms with shared bathrooms. You explain the rule politely and put him on the waiting list for a quieter single room in his grade.

    🚪 Check-out

    1. Departure list received from HR or mobilization.
    2. Key or card returned; locker emptied.
    3. Room inspected for damage; cleaned and marked ready.
    4. Register updated the same day.
    Example: catching damage early
    The check-out inspection finds a broken window. Because it is recorded at check-out, the repair is booked immediately and the next occupant arrives to a safe room, instead of finding it on arrival at night.

    Module 07 · Workflows & Tools

    Camp Workflows & Tools

    Repeatable routines, templates and tools that keep beds, meals, buses and welfare under control.

    🗺️ End-to-end workflow

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: the workflow on one day
    08:00 forecast shows 32 arrivals. 10:00 rooms allocated. 11:00 caterer and transport briefed. 19:00 check-ins. Next day: operations, a block inspection, the daily report, and 18 check-outs.

    📅 Weekly planning

    1. Get the 2–4 week arrival and departure forecast from HR and mobilization.
    2. Compare forecast beds with available capacity (minus out-of-service rooms).
    3. Update meal and bus numbers by shift.
    4. Plan inspections, drills and contractor reviews for the week.
    5. Review last week's grievances and KPIs.
    Example: spotting a shortage early
    The 3-week forecast shows 640 people on site in week 3; capacity is 600 with 8 rooms out of service. You book 50 hotel rooms and speed up the repairs, two weeks before the peak, not on the night it happens.

    Advanced: Peaks, Crises & Cost

    What separates a camp administrator from a trusted site services lead: planning for the peak, handling crises calmly, and controlling cost without harming welfare.

    ⛰️ Capacity planning at peak

    Compare the bed demand forecast (from the manpower plan and rotations) with camp capacity month by month.

    An illustrative 12-month forecast. Hover a bar to see beds needed.
    OptionGood forWatch out for
    Temporary modular blockLong overflow (several months)Lead time for delivery and utilities
    Hotels / leased housingShort peaks, managers, visitorsExtra transport and cost per night
    Staggered arrivalsSmoothing short spikesMust match the work schedule
    Example: planning the overflow
    The forecast shows up to 80 extra beds needed in months 7–9. In month 4 you book a 60-bed temporary block (lead time 10 weeks) and 30 hotel rooms for the month-8 spike. When the peak arrives, nobody is placed beyond the room standard.

    🚨 Crisis management

    CrisisFirst actions
    Food poisoning outbreakMedical care, isolate cases, inform HSE and client, keep food samples, deep clean, investigate
    Water contaminationStop use, bottled water, inform HSE, test, treat, re-test before restoring
    FireAlarm, evacuate, muster and head count from the register, emergency services
    Worker unrestKeep everyone safe, listen through representatives, escalate quickly, communicate facts
    Severe heat / power lossCool shelters, water, generators, move vulnerable workers
    ⚠️
    Never hide a crisis. Clients and lenders lose trust far faster over a hidden incident than over one that was reported and handled well.
    Example: handling unrest
    200 workers gather at the mess hall over late wages and hot rooms. You keep the area calm, invite the welfare committee to meet the Site Manager and HR that afternoon, and share a written update by evening: payment date confirmed, AC repairs scheduled. The gathering ends peacefully.

    💰 Cost without cutting welfare

    ✅ Smart savings

    • Reduce food waste with accurate meal counts
    • Energy saving: AC timers in empty rooms, efficient lighting
    • Close empty blocks during low occupancy
    • Renegotiate contracts using KPI data

    ❌ False savings

    • Fewer meals per day
    • More people per room than the standard
    • Cutting AC, water or recreation
    • Cheaper buses with poor safety
    Example: a 20% cost target
    Management wants camp costs cut 20%. You find: food waste 9% (fix meal counts), AC running in 60 empty rooms (timers), two half-empty blocks (consolidate and close). Savings reach 17% with no welfare cuts. You present the remaining 3% options with their welfare risks so management can decide.

    📖 Full case study

    Project: Remote desert gas plant. 1,000 workers on 56/28. Main camp 600 beds. Peak in months 7–9. Lender welfare standards apply.

    1. Sizing: 1,000 × 2/3 ≈ 667 on site at peak; with buffer, about 730 beds needed.
    2. Overflow: 120-bed temporary block ordered in month 3; hotel rooms for managers.
    3. Welfare: gap assessment against the lender standard; grievance mechanism in 5 languages.
    4. Catering: 4-week menu cycle with regional dishes; daily meal counts from the register.
    5. Transport: staggered departures; driver fatigue checks; 99% on-time rate.
    6. Result: no room-standard breaches at peak, grievance closure 92%, early leavers fell from 9% to 4%.
    ✅
    Why it worked: early capacity planning, clear standards, a trusted grievance system and daily data shared with every contractor.
