    The Role & Mobilization Basics

    Before you book a single flight, understand what a Workforce Mobilization Manager owns, where the job sits inside an EPC project, and the words every site conversation uses.

    🧭 What does this role do?

    A Workforce Mobilization Manager makes sure the right workers arrive at the right site, on the right date, with the right documents, a bed to sleep in and a completed safety induction. The recruiter selects people. You deliver them to site ready to work.

    On large EPC (Engineering, Procurement and Construction) projects such as refineries, power plants or pipelines, hundreds or thousands of workers travel from many countries. One missing visa or one full camp can stop a whole crew. Your job is to stop that from happening.

    🚚
    Logistics management
    Transport, vendors, PPE issue, airport pickups and the budget behind them.
    📅
    Site onboarding schedules
    Planning medicals, safety induction, badging and the first days on site.
    ✈️
    Flight & accommodation
    Booking travel, managing rotations and allocating camp or hotel rooms.
    🛂
    Visa turnaround monitoring
    Tracking every work permit from documents to approval, and acting when it stalls.
    💡
    In one sentence: you turn a list of names into people standing on site, inducted, badged and productive, on the date construction needs them.
    Example: a typical request
    The construction manager emails: "We need 60 pipe fitters and 20 welders on site by 1 March." Recruitment has selected the people. You now plan their medicals, visas, flights, airport buses, camp rooms, PPE and induction so all 80 can start work on 1 March.

    🏗️ Where mobilization fits in an EPC project

    An EPC project runs in overlapping phases. Engineering designs the plant, Procurement buys materials and equipment, Construction builds it, and Commissioning tests and starts it up. Mass mobilization peaks during Construction, when the site needs the most hands.

    Mobilization starts weeks before construction because visas and travel take time.
    📝
    Key idea: mobilization must start before the work does. If construction needs 300 people in June and visas take 4 weeks, your work begins in April.
    Example: reading the phases
    On a fictional gas plant, Project Falcon, steel structures arrive in Month 8. Construction needs riggers and steel erectors from Month 8, so their mobilization (documents, visas, flights) starts in Month 6.

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Inside the project

    • Construction manager: sets need dates.
    • Planning team: owns the manpower histogram.
    • HSE team: runs induction and medical rules.
    • Camp manager: beds, meals, welfare.
    • Security: badges and gate access.
    • Recruitment & HR: selected candidates and contracts.

    🌍 Outside the project

    • Travel agency: flights and changes.
    • Visa / immigration agent: files permits.
    • Medical centres: fitness tests.
    • Transport contractor: buses and airport pickups.
    • Overseas sourcing agencies: supply workers.
    • Client: approves headcount and site access rules.
    💡
    Tip. Hold a short daily call with the camp manager, security and HSE. Ten minutes on "who is arriving tomorrow?" prevents most first-day problems.
    Example: a useful daily call
    You say 45 people land tomorrow at 22:00. The camp manager says Block C beds are still being cleaned. Security says badge printing stops at 18:00. Together you agree: rooms in Block D tonight, induction at 07:00, badges printed by 11:00. No one waits at the gate.

    🔀 Mobilization vs recruitment

    🔎 Recruiter

    Finds, screens and selects the worker. Answers: "Who should we hire?" Job ends at offer acceptance.

    🚀 Mobilization manager

    Moves the selected worker to site, legally and safely. Answers: "How and when do they get there, ready to work?" Job ends when the worker is productive.

    ✅
    Rule of thumb: a hire only counts when the worker is on site and working. An accepted offer with no visa is not a hire yet.
    Example: why the handover matters
    Recruitment reports "100 electricians hired." You check: 22 have expired passports and 9 failed medicals. Only 69 can mobilize now. Reporting this early lets recruitment source replacements before the need date, not after.

    Module 02 · Planning

    Manpower Planning & the Mobilization Plan

    Learn to read the headcount plan, work backwards from the date people are needed, and write a mobilization plan everyone can follow.

    📊 The manpower histogram

    The manpower histogram is a bar chart from the planning team showing how many workers the project needs each month (or week). It is the starting point of every mobilization decision.

    Your workload is highest on the steep ramp-up (M3–M6), not at the peak itself.
    PhaseYour main focus
    Ramp-upVolume mobilization: visas, flights, camp beds, induction capacity.
    PeakRotations, replacements for leavers, keeping POB stable.
    Ramp-downDemobilization, final flights, redeploying good workers to other projects.
    Example: reading the jump
    Headcount goes from 320 in M4 to 520 in M5: 200 new arrivals in one month, about 10 every working day. That tells you how many visas to file in M3, how many seats to book, and how many induction places you need daily.

    ⏪ Need dates & backward scheduling

    Start from the need date (when work must begin) and subtract the time each step takes. This is called backward scheduling. It tells you the latest date each step must start.

    backward schedule · one overseas workerNeed date (start work)           1 July
- Induction & badging  (2 days)  -> arrive on site by     28 June
- Flight booking buffer (7 days) -> visa in hand by       21 June
- Visa processing      (21 days) -> visa filed by         31 May
- Docs & medical       (10 days) -> start documents by    21 May

Latest safe start: 21 May  (about 6 weeks before the need date)
    ⚠️
    Always add a buffer. Real visa times vary, flights get cancelled, and medicals sometimes need a repeat test. A plan with zero buffer will be late.
    Example: a late request
    On 10 June, construction asks for 30 riggers by 1 July. Your backward schedule says documents should have started on 21 May. You reply the same day: "Overseas hires cannot make 1 July. Options: 10 riggers transferred from our other site by 25 June, and 20 overseas hires by 20 July."

    ⏱️ Lead times by stage

    Keep a table of typical durations for your project. Update it every month with real figures from your tracker.

    StageTypical range (illustrative)What changes it
    Document collection3–10 daysPassport validity, certificate attestation, worker responsiveness
    Pre-employment medical2–7 daysClinic capacity, repeat tests
    Visa / work permit1–6 weeksCountry, nationality, quota approvals, public holidays
    Flight booking2–10 daysSeason, group size, routing
    Arrival to productive1–3 daysInduction capacity, badge printing, PPE stock
    📝
    These ranges are examples only. Visa rules and processing times differ by country and change often. Always confirm current timings with your immigration agent or the official authority.
    Example: local vs overseas lead time
    A local electrician who already has residency: medical 2 days + induction 1 day = 3 days. An overseas electrician: documents 7 + medical 3 + visa 21 + travel 5 + induction 2 = 38 days. Same trade, very different plan.

    🗂️ The mobilization plan

    The mobilization plan is a short document that tells everyone how people will get to site. Clients often ask for it in the first weeks of the contract.

    1
    Headcount & dates
    Trades, numbers and need dates linked to the histogram.
    2
    Sourcing mix
    Local vs overseas, countries, agencies used.
    3
    Process & lead times
    Documents, medical, visa, travel, induction steps.
    4
    Logistics & housing
    Camps, hotels, transport, PPE issue.
    5
    Roles & contacts
    Who does what, escalation path.
    6
    Risks & reporting
    Key risks, mitigations, KPIs, report frequency.
    Example: one line of a plan
    "Mechanical trades (420 workers), 70% overseas from two source countries, 30% local. Visas filed in weekly batches of 60 from Week 10. Arrivals Tuesdays and Thursdays only, 30 per flight group, housed in Camp A Blocks 3–6."

    ✅ Readiness checklist

    Before a worker is cleared to travel, every item must be green.

    👤 Worker ready

    • Passport valid for the full contract period (plus margin)
    • Signed contract
    • Medical: fit to work
    • Visa / work permit approved
    • Trade certificates verified

    🏗️ Site ready

    • Bed allocated
    • Airport pickup booked
    • Induction slot reserved
    • PPE sizes in stock
    • Supervisor knows the arrival date
    ✅
    Rule: "Ready to fly" means both the worker and the site are ready. A worker with a visa but no bed is not ready.
    Example: catching a gap
    Twelve welders have visas and flights for Thursday. Your checklist shows the induction room is booked for a client audit that day. You move induction to Friday 07:00 and tell the welders' supervisor, before anyone lands.

    Module 03 · Logistics Management

    Logistics Management

    Everything that physically moves people and supplies them: vendors, buses, PPE, and the money behind it.

    🚚 What logistics covers

    Logistics is the physical side of mobilization. If planning answers "when", logistics answers "how do they physically get there and what do they need".

    🛫
    Travel
    Flights, transit hotels, travel documents.
    🚌
    Ground transport
    Airport pickups, daily camp-to-site buses.
    🦺
    Equipment
    PPE, tools, SIM cards, welcome kits.
    🛏️
    Housing
    Camp beds, hotels, meals and laundry.
    Example: the logistics behind one arrival
    One worker arriving means: a flight seat, a bus seat from the airport, a bed, a meal card, a PPE set in the right boot size, a SIM card, and a seat in tomorrow's induction. Multiply that by 200 for a busy week.

    🤝 Vendors & service contracts

    You rarely do the logistics yourself. You manage vendors who do it, against clear service levels.

    VendorProvidesService level to agree
    Travel agencyFlights, changes, group faresTicket issued within 24 h of request; 24/7 emergency line
    Transport contractorBuses, airport pickupsVehicle at airport within 30 min of landing
    Camp operatorRooms, catering, cleaningRoom ready on arrival; agreed occupancy standard
    Medical providerFitness testsResults within 48 h
    Visa / immigration agentPermit filing and follow-upWeekly status report per application
    💡
    Write the service level down. "Fast service" cannot be measured. "Ticket in 24 hours" can.
    Example: holding a vendor to account
    The tracker shows 18% of airport pickups were late last month. Because the contract says "within 30 minutes", you can show the data, agree a fix (one extra standby van on arrival days) and track it next month.

    🚌 Ground transport & airport pickups

    1. Send the transport contractor the arrival manifest (names, flight, time) 48 hours before.
    2. Give each worker the driver's contact and a meeting point in the pre-arrival pack.
    3. Match bus size to group size, with one spare seat in five for late changes.
    4. Driver confirms headcount at pickup and drop-off; missing people are reported at once.
    arrival manifestDate: 14 Mar  | Flight: XX 512  | ETA 21:40  | Terminal 2
Group: Mechanical batch 7  | Pax: 28  | Destination: Camp A
Bus: 1 x 35-seater  | Driver: +00 000 000 000
Escort: M. Khan (logistics coordinator)
    Example: a missing passenger
    The driver counts 27 of 28 at the airport. The coordinator checks: one worker is held at immigration for a document check. The bus leaves with 27; a car waits for the last worker. Nobody is left alone at a foreign airport at night.

    🦺 PPE, tools & welcome kits

    Workers cannot enter site without PPE. Collect sizes before travel so the store is ready.

    HelmetSafety boots (size)Coverall (size)GlovesSafety glassesHearing protectionHi-vis vestSIM cardMeal card
    ⚠️
    A shortage of one boot size can keep a whole crew off site. Track PPE stock against the arrival forecast, not against last month's use.
    Example: forecasting PPE
    Next month 240 workers arrive. From size data, 60 need boot size 42 but the store has 15. You order 50 more two weeks ahead, so every worker gets boots on Day 1.

    💰 Cost control

    Mobilization is expensive. Know your cost per mobilization: the full cost of getting one worker productive on site.

    Cost itemIllustrative amount (USD)
    Flight (one way)650
    Visa & permit fees400
    Medical tests120
    Airport transfer40
    Transit hotel night90
    Induction day (paid, non-productive)150
    Cost per mobilization1,450
    📝
    The biggest avoidable costs are usually last-minute flight changes, no-shows and workers sent home after failing a site medical. Fix these and the budget follows.
    Example: the cost of one failure
    A worker fails the site medical after arriving. You paid 1,450 to bring him, plus another flight home (650) and a replacement (1,450). One failed medical cost 3,550. Doing the medical before departure would have avoided most of it.

    Module 04 · Flight & Accommodation Mobilization

    Flights & Accommodation

    Getting people there and giving them a bed: bookings, rotations, crew changes, camps and the welfare standards that keep workers safe and willing to stay.

    ✈️ Travel booking basics

    Every booking needs four things confirmed: the right person, a valid travel document, a valid visa, and an agreed arrival date.

    CheckWhy
    Name exactly as on passportName mismatch can mean boarding is refused
    Visa approved and valid on arrival dateAirlines check entry documents at check-in
    Transit rules for the routeSome transit countries require their own visa
    Arrival time vs pickup and camp check-inLate-night arrivals need staff on duty
    Baggage allowanceWorkers often carry tools and months of clothing
    Example: a name mismatch
    The ticket says "Mohd Salim", the passport says "Mohammed Saleem". Your pre-travel check catches it two days before departure, the agency reissues the ticket, and the worker flies on time.

    🔄 Rotations & crew change

    A rotation is a repeating pattern of days on site and days off. A crew change is the day one group leaves and its replacement arrives.

    RotationMeaningTypical use
    28/2828 days on, 28 days offKey supervisors with a back-to-back partner
    14/1414 on, 14 offRemote sites, specialists
    6/1 or 10/4Short work weeks with short breaksLocal or domestic workforce
    Long contractMonths on site, then annual leave flightOverseas craft workers
    ⚠️
    Avoid "everyone leaves on the same day". If all your key QA inspectors rotate out together, site work stops. Stagger rotations and use back-to-back pairs for critical roles.
    Example: back-to-back pairs
    Site has two HSE managers on 28/28: Anna and Ben. Anna flies in on the 1st, Ben flies out on the 2nd after a one-day handover. The site always has one HSE manager, and the handover day avoids gaps.

    🧳 Group bookings & disruption

    For volume mobilization, book groups (often 10–40 people) on the same flight. Groups make pickups, check-in and induction easier, and often reduce fares.

    🟢 Good practice

    • Fixed arrival days (for example, Tuesday and Thursday)
    • Group leader or escort per batch
    • Book 2–4 weeks ahead where visas allow
    • Agree a change cut-off date with the agency

    🔴 Common problems

    • Daily random arrivals that overload induction
    • Booking before the visa is approved
    • No plan for missed connections
    • No escort for first-time travellers
    Example: a missed connection
    A group of 40 misses its connection because of a storm. The emergency line is called, the agency rebooks them on the next morning's flight, a transit hotel and meals are arranged, the camp and induction team are told the new ETA, and families receive a message. Total delay: one day, no one stranded.

    🏕️ Accommodation options

    OptionBest forWatch out for
    Man-camp (site camp)Remote sites, large crewsFixed capacity; takes months to expand
    HotelsShort stays, senior staff, overflowExpensive at scale; daily transport
    Rented apartments / villasMid-term staff near townsLeases, furniture, maintenance
    Floating or modular unitsCoastal or temporary surgesApprovals and set-up time
    Example: choosing housing
    A desert site 200 km from the nearest town houses craft workers in a 1,500-bed camp. Twelve visiting vendor specialists for a two-week startup stay in a hotel 40 km away, with a dedicated van.

    🛏️ Room allocation & bed planning

    Compare the bed forecast (people expected) with bed capacity every week, at least eight weeks ahead.

    bed plan · weeklyWeek   Forecast POB   Camp beds   Gap    Action
W18        540           600       +60    OK
W20        610           600       -10    Hotel overflow 10
W22        680           600       -80    Add modular block (ordered W12)
W24        650           680       +30    New block live
    • Group rooms by shift (day and night shift should not share a room) and by crew.
    • Keep a small reserve (around 3–5%) for late arrivals and emergencies.
    • Release beds on demobilization the same day, so the tracker stays true.
    Example: spotting a gap early
    In Week 12 the bed plan shows an 80-bed gap in Week 22. A modular block needs about 10 weeks. You raise it in the weekly meeting and it is ordered the same week, in time.

    💚 Welfare standards

    Good housing is not a luxury. It affects safety, productivity and retention. Many clients and lenders audit camps against worker-welfare standards.

    🛌
    Space & rest
    Agreed maximum per room, own bed, quiet hours, air conditioning where needed.
    🍽️
    Food & water
    Safe drinking water, varied meals, dietary and religious needs.
    🏥
    Health
    Clinic access, hygiene, laundry, pest control.
    📞
    Voice
    Grievance channel, Wi-Fi to call family, recreation.
    Example: welfare and retention
    After Wi-Fi and a mess-hall menu committee were added at Camp B, early resignations in the first month dropped from 9% to 4%. Fewer resignations meant fewer replacement mobilizations.

    Module 05 · Visa Turnaround Monitoring

    Visa Turnaround Monitoring

    Visas are the slowest and least predictable step. Learn the pipeline, the documents, how to track turnaround, and how to act before a delay becomes a site problem.

    🛂 Visa & work permit basics

    A work visa or work permit is official permission for a foreign worker to enter a country and work there. Without it, the worker cannot legally work, and the company can face fines or site shutdowns.

    TermMeaning
    Work visa / permitPermission to enter and work for a named employer
    Business / visit visaFor meetings or short visits, not for productive work
    Quota / block approvalAdvance approval for a number of foreign workers by trade
    AttestationOfficial confirmation that a certificate is genuine
    Residence permitCard issued after arrival allowing the worker to stay
    ⚠️
    Never use a visit visa for site work to save time. It breaks immigration law and puts the worker and company at risk. Rules differ by country and change often, so always confirm with your licensed immigration agent or the official authority.
    Example: the right visa
    A vendor engineer flies in for two days of meetings: a business visa may be enough. The same engineer staying four weeks to supervise installation is doing work, so a work permit is needed. Ask the immigration agent, not the traveller.

    🔁 The visa pipeline

    Visa TAT is measured from Step 3 (filed) to Step 5 (issued). Steps 1–2 are your preparation time.

    Track each worker by stage, not just "pending". "Pending" hides where the problem is.

    Example: why stages matter
    The weekly report says "85 visas pending". Broken down by stage: 40 waiting for documents, 15 at medical, 30 under review. The real issue is documents, which is in your control, not the authority's.

    📄 Document checklist

    Incomplete files are the number-one cause of visa delays. Use a fixed checklist and check every file before it is filed.

    📘 Identity & employment

    • Passport with enough validity and blank pages
    • Photos to the required size and background
    • Signed offer or contract
    • Employer sponsorship letter or quota reference

    🎓 Qualifications & checks

    • Trade or education certificates (attested if required)
    • Medical fitness report
    • Police clearance certificate (where required)
    • Previous visa history (if asked)
    💡
    Check passport expiry first. Many countries require several months of validity beyond the stay. Renewing a passport can take weeks, so find this on Day 1, not Day 20.
    Example: a rejected photo
    Twelve applications are returned because photos had a coloured background. Each loses 6 days. After this, you add "photo meets spec" as a tick-box in the file checklist, and the problem stops.

    📈 Monitoring turnaround

    A visa tracker shows every application, its stage, and how many days it has been there. Review it daily during ramp-up.

    visa tracker · extractName        Trade     Nationality  Filed    Stage          Days  Need date  Status

    Example: acting on the tracker
    J. Santos has been under review for 22 days against a normal TAT of 19 and needs to be on site 1 June. You ask the agent to follow up today, warn the supervisor, and line up a local backup rigger in case the visa misses the date.

    🚧 Common bottlenecks & fixes

    BottleneckFix
    Workers slow to send documentsClear checklist in their language; agency deadline; daily chase list
    Certificate attestation takes weeksStart attestation at offer stage, in parallel with medicals
    Quota not approved for a tradeApply for quotas early from the histogram; re-assign trades where allowed
    Public holidays close officesMark holidays in the plan; file earlier before long breaks
    Rule change adds a stepRe-baseline lead times for the affected nationality; tell planning
    Example: planning around a holiday
    A ten-day public holiday is coming in the host country. You file 120 applications two weeks earlier than normal, so they are decided before offices close. Arrivals stay on plan.

    ⚖️ Ethics & compliance

    • Employer pays. Good practice (and the law in many places) says workers should not pay recruitment or visa fees to get the job.
    • Workers keep their passports. Collect them only for processing, return them promptly, and never hold them to stop people leaving.
    • Contract in a language they understand, signed before departure, with the same terms on arrival.
    • Protect personal data. Passports and medical results are sensitive. Share only with people who need them.
    📝
    Clients and lenders on large projects increasingly audit ethical recruitment. A compliant process protects workers and protects the contract.
    Example: a fee discovered
    During arrival interviews, several workers say an overseas agency charged them a "processing fee". You report it, pause new orders with that agency, investigate, and arrange reimbursement in line with company policy.

    Module 06 · Site Onboarding Schedules

    Site Onboarding Schedules

    The last mile: turning a new arrival into a safe, badged, equipped worker standing with their crew, as quickly as possible.

    🎒 What onboarding covers

    Site onboarding is everything that happens between arrival and the first productive shift. The goal is safe and fast: never skip safety, never waste days.

    🦺
    HSE induction
    Site hazards, rules, emergency routes, permit-to-work basics.
    🩺
    Medical & fitness
    Site medical check where required, e.g. fitness for work at height.
    🪪
    Badge & access
    Photo ID, area access, biometrics where used.
    🧰
    Competency check
    Trade test or certificate check (e.g. welder qualification).
    Example: safe and fast
    On a well-run site, a welder arriving Monday night is inducted Tuesday, passes the weld test Wednesday morning, and is welding with his crew Wednesday afternoon: arrival to productive in under two days.

    📅 Day 1 to Day 3 schedule

    WhenActivityOwner
    Arrival nightAirport pickup, camp check-in, meal, welcome packLogistics + camp
    Day 1 AMDocument check (passport, visa, contract), site medical if requiredMobilization + HR + clinic
    Day 1 PMHSE induction and test; PPE issueHSE + store
    Day 2 AMBadge photo and access card; trade test if neededSecurity + QA/QC
    Day 2 PMHandover to supervisor; toolbox talk with crewConstruction
    Day 3First full productive shift; buddy assigned for first weekSupervisor
    💡
    Publish an onboarding calendar one week ahead showing each batch, its arrival date and induction slot. HSE, security and supervisors then plan around it instead of being surprised.
    Example: an onboarding calendar line
    "Batch 14 · 32 pipe fitters · land Tue 21:40 · Camp A Block 4 · Induction Wed 07:00 Room 2 · Badges Thu 09:00 · Handover to J. Ortiz Thu 13:00."

    🧮 Induction capacity planning

    Induction rooms, trainers, clinic slots and badge printers all have daily limits. If arrivals exceed them, people wait in camp, paid but not working.

    capacity checkPlanned arrivals next week:          260
Induction room capacity:             40 per session x 1 session/day x 5 days = 200
Shortfall:                           60

Fix options:
  a) add an afternoon session on 2 days   -> +80 capacity
  b) shift 60 arrivals to the next week   -> check need dates first
    Example: idle-day cost
    Sixty workers wait one extra day for induction. At 150 per day in wages and camp costs, that is 9,000 wasted in one day. An extra trainer session costs far less.

    🪪 Badging & site access

    A badge proves that a person has completed induction and is allowed in specific areas. No induction, no badge. No badge, no entry.

    • Badge data should match the passport and visa (name, number, validity).
    • Badge expiry should not go beyond visa or contract expiry.
    • Restricted areas (for example, live process units) need extra training before access is added.
    • On demobilization, the badge is collected and deactivated the same day.
    Example: linking badge and visa
    A worker's visa expires 30 September. The badge is set to expire 30 September too. On 15 September the tracker flags it: either the visa is renewed or the worker is demobilized. No one keeps working on an expired permit.

    🏢 Subcontractor onboarding

    Subcontractors often bring their own workers. They may handle their own travel, but site rules apply to everyone.

    ✅ You require from them

    • Worker list with need dates
    • Valid visas and documents for checking
    • Proof of medicals and trade certificates
    • Camp bed requests in advance

    🛡️ You provide or control

    • Induction slots
    • Badge issue after document check
    • Camp allocation (if shared camp)
    • Welfare standards they must meet
    Example: a surprise crew
    A scaffolding subcontractor brings 25 workers to the gate with no notice. Security refuses entry until documents are checked and induction is done. You fit them into tomorrow's session and remind the subcontractor of the 5-day notice rule.

    Module 07 · Tracking, KPIs & Reporting

    Tracking, KPIs & Reporting

    If it is not tracked, it is not managed. Build one reliable tracker, measure the right numbers, and report them so leaders can act.

    🗃️ The mobilization tracker

    The tracker is your single source of truth: one row per worker, one column per stage, updated daily and shared with everyone who needs it.

    Column groupFields

    Advanced: Surges, Risk & Recovery

    What separates a coordinator from a trusted mobilization lead: finding bottlenecks before they bite, planning for failure, and linking projects together.

    ⚡ Surge mobilization & bottlenecks

    During a surge, the whole chain moves at the speed of its slowest step. Check every capacity against the planned arrivals.

    surge check · grow from 300 to 900 in 6 weeksNeed: +600 workers in 30 working days  = 20 per day

Induction:  60 per day      -> enough  (60 > 20)
Flights:    3 groups/week x 40 seats = 120/week = 24/day -> enough, tight
Visas:      pipeline holds 480 filed  -> short by 120  🟠
Camp beds:  600 total for 900 people  -> short by 300  🔴  <- main bottleneck
    💡
    Fix the biggest bottleneck first. Adding more flights will not help if there are no beds.
    Example: solving the bed bottleneck
    You propose: 150 local hires who live at home (bus daily), 100 beds in nearby hotels, and a 50-bed modular block. The surge goes ahead without overcrowding the camp.

    🛡️ Risk & contingency

    RiskEarly warningMitigation
    Visa delays or rejectionsAgeing applications, rising TATFile early, backup candidates, diversify source countries
    Flight disruptionWeather, strikes, route changes24/7 agency line, transit hotel plan, flexible fares for key staff
    Camp shortageBed plan gap 8+ weeks outOverflow hotels, modular units, local hires
    Failed medicals on arrivalRising site rejection ratePre-departure medicals at approved clinics
    No-showsAgency or route trendStandby pool, pre-departure briefing, agency review
    Rule or policy changesAgent alerts, government noticesRe-baseline lead times, tell planning at once
    Example: a standby pool
    Your no-show rate is around 6%. For every batch of 50, you prepare 3 extra workers with visas ready. When 3 fail to travel, the standbys fill the seats and site headcount stays on plan.

    🔗 Linking mob, demob & redeployment

    Mobilization and demobilization are two ends of the same pipe. Good workers released by one project can be redeployed to another, saving time and cost.

    🔻 Project A (ramping down)

    • Releasing 120 mechanical workers in M10
    • Visas still valid for 4 months
    • Good performance records

    🔺 Project B (ramping up)

    • Needs 100 mechanical workers in M10–M11
    • Same country, same camp operator
    • Short of time for new visas
    ✅
    Result: transferring workers (where the rules allow a change of project or sponsor) avoids new flights, new medicals and weeks of visa waiting. Always confirm the transfer rules with the immigration agent first.
    Example: aligning dates
    You set Project A's demob dates one week before Project B's need dates. Workers move camps by bus on a Sunday, attend Project B's induction Monday, and start Tuesday. 80 transfers saved about 116,000 in mobilization costs (80 × 1,450).

    📣 Escalation & stakeholders

    1. Escalate early with data, not feelings: "Visa TAT is 30 days; need date is in 20."
    2. Bring options, not just problems: transfer, phase, re-sequence, local hire.
    3. Agree an owner and date for every action.
    4. Close the loop: confirm in writing when the risk is resolved.
    Example: an impossible request
    The client asks for 150 scaffolders in 10 days; visa TAT is 25. You reply: "10 days is not possible for overseas hires. Options: 40 local scaffolders in 7 days, 30 transfers from Project A in 10 days, 80 overseas in 35 days. Construction can re-sequence scaffold-heavy areas to match."

    📖 Full case study

    Situation: Project Falcon must go from 300 to 760 workers in 10 weeks for the peak construction phase.

    1. Plan: Histogram reviewed with planning; 460 workers needed by trade and week.
    2. Backward schedule: 38-day overseas lead time; first visa batches filed in Week 1.
    3. Capacity check: beds short by 120 in Weeks 7–9; induction short on two days.
    4. Actions: 60 hotel beds, 60 modular beds, extra induction sessions, pre-departure medicals.
    5. Tracking: daily tracker, weekly RAG report, 6% standby pool.
    6. Result: 742 on site by Week 10 (98% of plan), on-time arrival 91%, arrival to productive 1.7 days.
    ✅
    Why it worked: early planning from the histogram, honest capacity checks, one shared tracker, and early escalation with options.
