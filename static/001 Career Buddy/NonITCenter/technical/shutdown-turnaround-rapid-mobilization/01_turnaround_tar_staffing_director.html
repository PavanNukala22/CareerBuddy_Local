    Course Overview

    Turnaround (TAR) Staffing Director: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a Turnaround (TAR) Staffing Director plans, fills and manages the temporary workforce for refinery and petrochemical turnarounds. It covers four skills: high-intensity timeline staffing, refinery and petrochemical TAR lifecycle comprehension, mobilization and ramp-up planning, and the client interface.

    The role matters because a turnaround shuts a plant that may earn hundreds of thousands of dollars a day. Every day of overrun costs the client heavily, and every missing welder or fitter can hold up the critical path. Done well, the right crafts arrive safe, qualified and on time. Done badly, the plant restarts late, costs rise and safety slips.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples in the main lessons. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures are illustrative and use US dollars. Labour law, safety rules and client contracts differ by country and site, so always confirm the current rules with your safety, legal and commercial teams.

    Who this course is for

    • Recruiters and staffing coordinators moving into industrial shutdown work
    • Account managers at industrial staffing agencies
    • Maintenance and turnaround planners who want to understand the labour side
    • Site HR and contractor management staff at refineries and chemical plants

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Explain how turnaround staffing differs from normal recruitment and why time is the main constraint.
    2. Calculate headcount from planned man-hours, shift length and productivity.
    3. Build a sourcing funnel and work out how many candidates must enter it.
    4. Describe each phase of the TAR lifecycle, from scope freeze to demobilization.
    5. Link the staffing plan to the critical path and the shutdown schedule.
    6. Build a mobilization curve that ramps crews up and down by day.
    7. Plan travel, accommodation, induction and badging capacity for a peak workforce.
    8. Calculate fill rate, no-show rate, overtime cost and cost per man-hour.
    9. Run daily client meetings and report staffing KPIs clearly.
    10. Price a staffing bid and manage rate changes and variations.
    11. Apply the main data protection, safety, fraud and contract controls used in turnaround staffing.

    A turnaround needs 12,000 man-hours of welding. Each welder works 12 hours a day. How many welder-days is that?

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What high-intensity timeline staffing is and why it matters

    High-intensity timeline staffing means hiring a large, skilled, temporary workforce in a very short window for a fixed start date. In a turnaround the plant stops on a set day, so every craft must be on site, inducted and badged before the first permit is issued.

    It matters because the cost of a late or missing worker is not just their wage. One missing coded welder can stop a whole crew, delay a critical path job and push back the restart. A good director plans headcount from the work, builds a sourcing pipeline weeks ahead and keeps a reserve for no-shows.

    TermMeaning
    Turnaround (TAR)Planned shutdown of a plant unit for inspection, repair and upgrade
    CraftA trade such as welder, pipefitter, scaffolder or rigger
    Man-hourOne person working for one hour
    HeadcountNumber of workers needed on a shift or day
    PeakThe day with the highest headcount
    No-showA booked worker who does not arrive
    Headcount = Planned man-hours ÷ (Hours per shift × Shift days × Productivity factor)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Pipefitting needs 24,000 man-hours over 20 shift days. Each shift is 12 hours and productivity is 0.85. Headcount = 24,000 ÷ (12 × 20 × 0.85) = 24,000 ÷ 204 = 117.6, so plan 118 pipefitters for that shift pattern.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning headcount from the client's wish list instead of the man-hour estimate
    • Ignoring productivity loss from permits, weather and waiting for scaffolds
    • Starting sourcing only after the contract is signed

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Welding needs 9,600 man-hours over 16 shift days of 12 hours with a productivity factor of 0.8. How many welders are needed?

    Show answer
    12 × 16 × 0.8 = 153.6 productive hours per welder. 9,600 ÷ 153.6 = 62.5, so plan 63 welders.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Craft mix, shift patterns and the sourcing funnel

    A turnaround needs a craft mix, not a single job title. Typical crafts are pipefitters, coded welders, boilermakers, scaffolders, riggers, mechanical fitters, electricians, instrument technicians and safety watchers. Each craft has its own pool, pay rate and lead time.

    Most turnarounds run two 12-hour shifts, day and night, often 7 days a week. The sourcing funnel shows how many candidates must enter at the top to deliver the number you need at the gate, after screening, test failures, offer declines and no-shows.

    Funnel stageTypical pass rate
    Candidates contacted100 percent
    Interested and available50 percent
    Pass screening and references80 percent
    Pass trade test or weld test75 percent
    Accept offer85 percent
    Arrive on day one92 percent
    Candidates needed = Workers required ÷ Product of stage pass rates
    🧪
    Practical Example: You need 60 coded welders. Pass rates are 50 percent interested, 80 percent screening, 70 percent weld test, 90 percent accept and 95 percent arrive. Product = 0.5 × 0.8 × 0.7 × 0.9 × 0.95 = 0.2394. Candidates needed = 60 ÷ 0.2394 = 250.6, so contact at least 251 welders.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using one funnel rate for all crafts when weld tests fail far more people than scaffold checks
    • Planning only day shift and forgetting night shift needs its own supervisors
    • Forgetting safety watchers, fire watch and hole watch roles

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    You need 40 scaffolders. Pass rates are 60 percent interested, 85 percent screening, 90 percent test, 90 percent accept and 95 percent arrive. How many must you contact?

    Show answer
    Product = 0.6 × 0.85 × 0.9 × 0.9 × 0.95 = 0.3924. 40 ÷ 0.3924 = 101.9, so contact 102.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Building the staffing plan step by step

    The staffing plan turns the scope into names on a roster. Start with the man-hour estimate by craft from the planning team, convert it to headcount by shift and day, then set the sourcing start date by working back from the first day on site.

    Add a reserve for no-shows and early leavers, usually 5 to 10 percent per craft. Set the order of release so long-lead crafts such as coded welders and specialist technicians are sourced first. Record every assumption so the client can see how the numbers were built.

    StepOutput
    1. Get man-hours by craftLabour estimate table
    2. Convert to headcount by shiftDaily headcount by craft
    3. Add reserveBooked headcount with buffer
    4. Set lead timesSourcing start date per craft
    5. Build the funnelCandidates to contact per craft
    6. Assign recruitersRecruiter workload plan
    Booked headcount = Required headcount × (1 + Reserve percent)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The plan needs 118 pipefitters at peak and the reserve is 8 percent. Booked = 118 × 1.08 = 127.4, so book 128 pipefitters. If the no-show rate stays near 5 percent, about 121 arrive, which still covers the peak.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Applying no reserve because the client does not want to pay for spare workers
    • Giving every craft the same sourcing start date
    • Keeping the plan in a recruiter's head instead of a shared tracker

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Peak need is 75 boilermakers and the reserve is 6 percent. How many do you book?

    Show answer
    75 × 1.06 = 79.5, so book 80 boilermakers.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Staffing KPIs: fill rate, time to fill and no-shows

    During sourcing you measure progress every day. Fill rate shows how many required roles have a confirmed worker. Time to fill shows how fast recruiters close roles. No-show rate shows how many booked workers fail to arrive on their start date.

    Track these by craft and by shift, not just in total. A 95 percent total fill rate can hide a 70 percent fill rate for coded welders, which may be the craft that controls the critical path.

    KPIWhat it showsTypical target
    Fill rateConfirmed roles ÷ Required roles100 percent by T-7 days
    Time to fillDays from order to confirmed workerUnder 10 days
    No-show rateBooked workers not arrivingUnder 5 percent
    Early leaver rateWorkers leaving before releaseUnder 3 percent
    Test pass rateCandidates passing trade testTracked per craft
    Fill rate = Confirmed workers ÷ Required workers × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The plan needs 420 workers. With 10 days to go, 378 are confirmed. Fill rate = 378 ÷ 420 × 100 = 90 percent. The welder line shows 48 of 63 confirmed, or 76 percent, so welding gets two more recruiters and a referral bonus.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reporting only total fill rate and hiding the weak crafts
    • Counting verbal yeses as confirmed workers
    • Not tracking no-shows by source, so poor sources keep being used

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    250 workers were booked and 238 arrived on day one. What is the no-show rate?

    Show answer
    No-shows = 12. 12 ÷ 250 × 100 = 4.8 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: surge staffing, rate pressure and labour market risk

    At expert level you plan for a market where several plants shut down in the same season and compete for the same crafts. You build talent pools months ahead, agree priority with partner agencies and use retention bonuses that are paid only when the worker completes the job.

    You also manage rate pressure. When a craft is scarce, rates rise. You model the cost of paying more against the cost of a delay, and you agree rate change rules with the client in advance so decisions are fast and documented.

    RiskControl
    Competing shutdowns in the regionEarly pools, partner agencies, completion bonuses
    Craft shortageTravelling crews from other regions, approved rate uplift
    Early leaversCompletion bonus, good camp conditions, fair rotas
    Poor quality workersTrade tests, references, first-shift supervisor review
    Disputes on ratesRate card and change rules signed before mobilization
    Completion bonus cost = Workers × Completion rate × Bonus per worker
    🧪
    Practical Example: You offer a $600 completion bonus to 120 welders. You expect 92 percent to finish. Cost = 120 × 0.92 × 600 = $66,240. One day of delay costs the client $250,000, so the bonus is easily justified if it prevents early leavers.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Raising rates for one craft without telling the client or other crafts
    • Paying bonuses up front instead of on completion
    • Ignoring other shutdowns in the same region and season

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    You offer a $500 completion bonus to 80 riggers and expect 95 percent to finish. What is the bonus cost?

    Show answer
    80 × 0.95 × 500 = $38,000.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What a turnaround is and why plants need them

    A turnaround, also called a shutdown or outage, is a planned stop of a process unit so work that cannot be done while it runs can be completed. This includes inspection of pressure vessels, cleaning of heat exchangers, catalyst change, valve repair and tie-ins for new equipment.

    Plants run for years between turnarounds, so each event is large, costly and tightly planned. Staffing must understand the phases because the craft mix and headcount change sharply from shutdown to execution to start-up.

    TermMeaning
    ShutdownSafely stopping, draining and isolating the unit
    DecontaminationCleaning out hydrocarbons and chemicals before work
    ExecutionThe main repair, inspection and replacement work
    Critical pathThe chain of jobs that sets the total duration
    Start-upReassembling, testing and bringing the unit back online
    Scope freezeThe date after which new work is not accepted
    Daily cost of downtime = Lost production per day × Margin per unit
    🧪
    Practical Example: A unit produces 40,000 barrels a day with a margin of $8 a barrel. Daily cost of downtime = 40,000 × 8 = $320,000. A two-day overrun costs about $640,000 in lost margin alone.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Thinking a turnaround is just a large maintenance job with no fixed date
    • Ignoring the decontamination phase when planning arrival dates
    • Not knowing which jobs are on the critical path

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A unit produces 25,000 barrels a day with a margin of $6 a barrel. What is the daily cost of downtime?

    Show answer
    25,000 × 6 = $150,000 a day.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🔄 Core Concepts: TAR phases from scope to demobilization

    A turnaround has a long planning tail and a short, intense execution. Planning starts 12 to 24 months ahead with scope selection. Scope freezes a few months before the event. Then come pre-shutdown work, shutdown and decontamination, execution, start-up and demobilization.

    Each phase needs different people. Planners and schedulers come early. Scaffolders arrive before shutdown to build access. Fitters, welders and inspectors peak during execution. Start-up needs operators, instrument technicians and fewer crafts. Demobilization needs tool and site clearance teams.

    PhaseTimingMain staffing need
    Scope and planningT-24 to T-6 monthsPlanners, schedulers, estimators
    Scope freezeAbout T-4 monthsFinal labour estimate
    Pre-shutdownT-4 to T-0 weeksScaffolders, prefabrication welders
    Shutdown and decontaminationDays 1 to 3Operators, cleaning crews, safety watchers
    ExecutionMain block of daysFitters, welders, boilermakers, inspectors
    Start-up and demobilizationLast daysInstrument techs, reduced crews, clearance teams
    Execution days = Total TAR days − Shutdown days − Start-up days
    🧪
    Practical Example: A turnaround lasts 30 days with 3 days for shutdown and decontamination and 4 days for start-up. Execution days = 30 − 3 − 4 = 23 days, so the peak craft workforce is planned for 23 days.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Bringing all crafts on day one when the unit is still being decontaminated
    • Accepting new scope after scope freeze without changing the staffing plan
    • Releasing scaffolders too early when they must strike scaffolds at the end

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A turnaround lasts 35 days with 4 days for shutdown and 5 days for start-up. How many execution days are there?

    Show answer
    35 − 4 − 5 = 26 execution days.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🎯 Intermediate Concepts: Reading the schedule and labour histogram

    The planning team produces a schedule in a tool such as Primavera P6 and a labour histogram that shows required headcount by craft and day. The staffing director reads both to know when each craft must arrive, peak and leave.

    Look for the critical path jobs and their crafts, the peak day for each craft and any gaps where crews would be idle. Feed back to the planners when the histogram has spikes that the labour market cannot fill, so the schedule can be levelled.

    Histogram featureWhat it tells staffing
    Peak heightMaximum headcount to source
    Ramp slopeHow fast workers must arrive
    Flat topSteady crews and stable rotas
    Sudden spikePossible levelling opportunity
    Long tailPartial crews for start-up and clearance
    Peak-to-average ratio = Peak headcount ÷ Average headcount
    🧪
    Practical Example: The welder histogram has a peak of 90 and an average of 60 across execution. Peak-to-average ratio = 90 ÷ 60 = 1.5. A ratio above about 1.3 suggests the planners should try to level the welding work to reduce the number of short-stay welders.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reading only the total histogram and not each craft
    • Ignoring night shift columns in the histogram
    • Accepting spikes without asking whether jobs can move

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The fitter histogram peaks at 140 with an average of 112. What is the peak-to-average ratio?

    Show answer
    140 ÷ 112 = 1.25.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📡 Intermediate Concepts: Critical path, overruns and emergent work

    Emergent work is new work found once equipment is opened, such as corrosion that needs extra welding. It often adds 10 to 25 percent to man-hours. The staffing plan needs a contingency pool so emergent work does not delay the critical path.

    Track schedule performance every shift. If the critical path slips, decide with the client whether to add crews, add shifts or extend hours. Each choice has a cost and a fatigue risk, so it must follow the site's hours rules.

    MeasureMeaning
    Planned progressMan-hours planned to date
    Earned progressMan-hours of work actually completed
    Schedule performance index (SPI)Earned ÷ Planned
    Emergent work percentNew man-hours ÷ Original man-hours
    FloatDays a job can slip without delaying the end
    Schedule performance index = Earned man-hours ÷ Planned man-hours
    🧪
    Practical Example: By day 10, 52,000 man-hours were planned and 46,800 were earned. SPI = 46,800 ÷ 52,000 = 0.90. The work is 10 percent behind, so the client agrees to add 20 fitters on the critical exchanger jobs.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Measuring progress by hours worked instead of work completed
    • Having no emergent work pool, so crews are stolen from other jobs
    • Adding crews without checking camp, tools and permit capacity

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Planned man-hours are 30,000 and earned man-hours are 31,500. What is the SPI and what does it mean?

    Show answer
    SPI = 31,500 ÷ 30,000 = 1.05, so work is 5 percent ahead of plan.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🏁 Advanced Concepts: Expert: lessons learned, benchmarking and the next TAR

    At expert level you close each turnaround with a lessons-learned review and use the data to win and plan the next one. Compare actual man-hours, peak headcount, no-shows, overtime and safety results with the plan and with industry benchmarks.

    Keep a craft performance record so good workers are invited back first. Share findings with the client planning team early, because the next turnaround's scope work often starts within a year of the last restart.

    Lesson areaData to keep
    Labour estimatePlanned vs actual man-hours by craft
    SourcingFunnel rates, time to fill, best sources
    AttendanceNo-shows, early leavers and reasons
    CostOvertime percent, cost per man-hour
    SafetyIncidents, near misses, hours worked
    Man-hour variance = (Actual man-hours − Planned man-hours) ÷ Planned man-hours × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The plan was 180,000 man-hours and the actual was 198,000. Variance = (198,000 − 180,000) ÷ 180,000 × 100 = 10 percent over. Most of the variance came from emergent exchanger repairs, so the next estimate adds a larger emergent allowance for exchangers.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Holding no review because everyone is tired after restart
    • Keeping no record of which workers performed well
    • Losing the data when the project team is released

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The plan was 150,000 man-hours and the actual was 141,000. What is the man-hour variance?

    Show answer
    (141,000 − 150,000) ÷ 150,000 × 100 = −6 percent, so 6 percent under plan.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    🚚 Fundamentals: What mobilization is and why ramp-up matters

    Mobilization is everything needed to move a worker from offer to productive work on site: travel, accommodation, medicals, induction, site passes, PPE and tools. Ramp-up is the daily build of headcount from the first arrivals to the peak.

    A good ramp-up matches the histogram. Too slow and the critical path starts late. Too fast and workers sit idle in camp, costing money, while induction rooms and badge desks are overloaded.

    TermMeaning
    MobilizationGetting workers ready and on site
    Ramp-upThe rising headcount curve before peak
    Ramp-downThe falling curve after peak work ends
    InductionSite safety training before work
    BadgingIssuing site access passes
    Gate-readyInducted, badged and equipped to work
    Daily arrivals needed = (Target headcount − Current headcount) ÷ Days left to reach target
    🧪
    Practical Example: You have 60 workers on site and need 300 in 6 days. Daily arrivals = (300 − 60) ÷ 6 = 40 workers a day. The induction room seats 30, so you add a second induction session each day.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning arrivals without checking induction and badge capacity
    • Bringing everyone on the same day to save travel effort
    • Forgetting that night shift workers also need induction before their first shift

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    You have 120 workers and need 360 in 8 days. How many must arrive each day?

    Show answer
    (360 − 120) ÷ 8 = 30 workers a day.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧩 Core Concepts: Mobilization checklist and lead times

    Each worker must clear a checklist before they are gate-ready. Some items take a day, others take weeks. Medicals, drug and alcohol tests, background checks, work visas and specialist certificates often have the longest lead times.

    Work backwards from the first shift date to set the latest start for each item. Track every worker against the checklist in a shared tracker, so you can see who is stuck and why.

    ItemTypical lead time
    Offer and contract1 to 2 days
    Medical and drug test3 to 5 days
    Background or security check5 to 15 days
    Certificates and weld coupons verified2 to 5 days
    Travel and accommodation booked2 to 7 days
    Site induction and badge1 day on arrival
    Latest start date = First shift date − Longest lead time in days
    🧪
    Practical Example: The first shift is on 20 March. The longest item is a 15-day security check. Latest start = 20 March − 15 days = 5 March. Any worker who has not started the check by 5 March cannot be gate-ready in time.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Starting background checks only after travel is booked
    • Assuming every worker's certificates are valid without checking expiry dates
    • Tracking items by email instead of a single tracker

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The first shift is on 10 June and the longest lead item is a 12-day check. What is the latest start date?

    Show answer
    10 June − 12 days = 29 May.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    📝 Intermediate Concepts: Building the mobilization curve step by step

    The mobilization curve shows planned and actual arrivals by day for each craft. Build it from the histogram, then shift each craft's arrival one or two days earlier to allow induction and tool issue before their first productive shift.

    Add capacity limits for travel, camp beds, induction seats, badge printing and PPE issue. The tightest limit sets the real ramp. Share the curve with the client and with logistics so everyone works to the same numbers.

    StepOutput
    1. Take histogram by craftRequired headcount by day
    2. Offset for inductionArrival day per craft
    3. Check capacity limitsMaximum arrivals per day
    4. Level arrivalsSmoothed arrival plan
    5. Book travel and bedsConfirmed arrivals by day
    6. Track actual vs planDaily mobilization report
    Induction sessions needed = Arrivals per day ÷ Seats per session (round up)
    🧪
    Practical Example: On the busiest day 95 workers arrive and the induction room seats 30. Sessions = 95 ÷ 30 = 3.17, so run 4 sessions. At 3 hours each, that is 12 hours of induction, so two trainers must share the day.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Building the curve from the total histogram only
    • Ignoring camp bed limits when arrivals spike
    • Not updating the curve when the shutdown date moves

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    140 workers arrive on one day and each induction session seats 35. How many sessions are needed?

    Show answer
    140 ÷ 35 = 4 sessions.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Mobilization KPIs and cost of idle time

    Measure whether workers arrive on time and become productive quickly. On-time mobilization shows the share of workers gate-ready on their planned day. Idle cost shows money spent on workers waiting on site with no work.

    Idle time usually comes from arriving too early, slow induction, missing permits or missing tools. Report it daily so the client and planners can fix the cause.

    KPIFormula or meaning
    On-time mobilizationGate-ready on plan day ÷ Planned arrivals
    Idle costIdle hours × Hourly cost
    Induction throughputWorkers inducted per day
    Badge cycle timeHours from arrival to badge
    Travel cost per headTravel spend ÷ Workers mobilized
    Idle cost = Idle workers × Idle hours × Loaded hourly rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: On day 2, 40 fitters wait 5 hours for scaffolds. The loaded rate is $68 an hour. Idle cost = 40 × 5 × 68 = $13,600. The report shows scaffolding as the cause, so scaffolders get an earlier start for the next units.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reporting arrivals but not gate-ready status
    • Hiding idle time because it looks bad for the agency
    • Not linking idle cost to its root cause

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    25 welders wait 4 hours with a loaded rate of $72 an hour. What is the idle cost?

    Show answer
    25 × 4 × 72 = $7,200.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🔐 Advanced Concepts: Expert: ramp-down, demobilization and remobilization

    At expert level you plan the ramp-down as carefully as the ramp-up. Release crafts as their work ends, but keep enough people for emergent work, testing and scaffold strike. Release in a set order agreed with the client, and give workers notice so travel can be booked.

    Plan remobilization for clients with several units or phased turnarounds. Keeping good crews together and moving them to the next unit saves sourcing time and induction cost and protects quality.

    Ramp-down riskControl
    Releasing too earlyRelease only after the planner confirms work complete
    Releasing too lateDaily release review against the histogram
    Lost good workersRemobilization list and early offers for the next job
    Unreturned tools and PPESign-off checklist before final pay
    Travel chaos on last dayStaggered release dates by craft
    Ramp-down saving = Workers released early × Days saved × Daily loaded cost
    🧪

    • Releasing whole crafts at once with no emergent cover
    • Paying final wages before tools are returned
    • Letting good workers leave with no plan for the next job

    📘 Fundamentals: What the client interface is and why it matters

    The client interface is how the staffing director works with the plant owner's turnaround manager, contracts team and site HR. It includes the bid, the rate card, daily meetings, reports, change requests and the final invoice.

    It matters because trust decides whether you are invited back. The client needs fast, honest numbers about headcount, risks and costs. A director who hides problems until they explode loses the account, even if most of the work went well.

    TermMeaning
    Client TAR managerOwner's leader for the turnaround
    Rate cardAgreed hourly rates by craft and shift
    MarkupAgency margin added to worker pay
    Change orderApproved change to scope, rates or headcount
    Daily reportHeadcount, progress, issues and safety summary
    KPI scorecardAgreed measures used to judge the agency
    Bill rate = Pay rate × (1 + Markup percent)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A coded welder is paid $45 an hour and the markup is 38 percent. Bill rate = 45 × 1.38 = $62.10 an hour. The markup must cover payroll taxes, insurance, recruitment, mobilization and margin.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Quoting rates without checking all costs inside the markup
    • Reporting good news first and hiding risks at the end
    • Agreeing changes verbally with site staff and not the contracts team

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A scaffolder is paid $32 an hour with a 40 percent markup. What is the bill rate?

    Show answer
    32 × 1.4 = $44.80 an hour.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Bids, rate cards and overtime rules

    Turnaround staffing is usually priced as a rate card: an hourly bill rate per craft for straight time, with higher rates for overtime and night shift. The bid also states mobilization fees, per diems, travel, camp costs and any completion bonuses.

    Overtime is common because shifts are 12 hours. Many sites pay the first 8 or 10 hours at straight time and the rest at time and a half, or pay overtime after 40 hours a week. Read the client contract and local law carefully, because overtime can be a large share of the bill.

    Bid itemHow it is charged
    Straight-time ratePer hour by craft
    Overtime rateUsually 1.5 times straight time
    Night shift premiumFixed amount or percent per hour
    Per diemDaily allowance for travelling workers
    Mobilization feePer worker or lump sum
    Completion bonusPaid only on finishing the job
    Weekly worker cost = (Straight hours × Rate) + (Overtime hours × Rate × 1.5)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A fitter works 84 hours a week. The first 40 are straight time at $58 and the rest are overtime. Cost = 40 × 58 + 44 × 58 × 1.5 = 2,320 + 3,828 = $6,148 a week.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Pricing only straight time when most hours will be overtime
    • Forgetting per diems and travel in the bid
    • Not stating which rules apply when the schedule extends

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A welder works 72 hours a week at $60 an hour, with overtime after 40 hours at 1.5 times. What is the weekly cost?

    Show answer
    40 × 60 = 2,400. 32 × 60 × 1.5 = 2,880. Total = $5,280.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running daily client meetings step by step

    During execution the director attends the daily turnaround meeting. Report headcount by craft and shift against plan, arrivals and releases, no-shows, safety events, open requests and risks for the next 48 hours.

    Keep the report short and the same every day so the client can compare. Bring options, not just problems. For each risk, state the impact, the action you are taking and any decision you need from the client.

    Headcount vs plan412 of 420 on day shift

    🧪

    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: KPI scorecards, invoices and cost per man-hour

    Clients judge the agency on an agreed scorecard, often with fill rate, no-show rate, safety, quality and invoice accuracy. Some contracts link part of the margin to these scores. Know the targets and report against them every week.

    Invoices must match approved timesheets, the rate card and approved change orders. Cost per man-hour lets the client compare your total cost with other agencies and with their own estimate.

    Scorecard KPITypical target
    Fill rate at start98 percent or more
    No-show rateUnder 5 percent
    Recordable incidentsZero
    Invoice accuracy99 percent or more
    Timesheet approval on time100 percent
    Cost per man-hour = Total labour invoice ÷ Total man-hours worked
    🧪
    Practical Example: The total labour invoice is $9,450,000 for 150,000 man-hours. Cost per man-hour = 9,450,000 ÷ 150,000 = $63. The client's estimate was $61, so the review explains the 2-dollar gap through extra overtime on emergent work.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Invoicing hours that are not on approved timesheets
    • Missing the scorecard review because the job is busy
    • Not explaining cost differences against the client's estimate

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A labour invoice is $4,560,000 for 76,000 man-hours. What is the cost per man-hour?

    Show answer
    4,560,000 ÷ 76,000 = $60.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    ⚖️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: change orders, disputes and long-term accounts

    At expert level you protect margin and the relationship at the same time. Every change in scope, headcount, rates or duration should be captured in a written change order before work starts, or as fast as the contract allows during an emergency.

    Resolve disputes with data: timesheets, approved requests, daily reports and the rate card. Use each turnaround to build a long-term account, such as a framework agreement across several plants, with agreed rates and pools.

    SituationExpert response
    Client asks for 30 more welders at nightConfirm in writing with rate and duration
    Schedule extends 5 daysIssue change order for extra days and per diems
    Dispute on overtime hoursReconcile timesheets against gate records
    Client cuts rates mid-jobRefer to signed rate card and change rules
    Strong performancePropose a framework agreement
    Change order value = Extra man-hours × Bill rate + Extra fixed costs
    🧪
    Practical Example: The client asks for 10 extra boilermakers for 6 days of 12 hours at a bill rate of $66. Extra man-hours = 10 × 6 × 12 = 720. Per diems are $90 × 10 × 6 = $5,400. Change order value = 720 × 66 + 5,400 = 47,520 + 5,400 = $52,920.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Starting extra work with no written approval
    • Arguing without timesheets and records
    • Treating every turnaround as a one-off sale

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The client adds 8 riggers for 5 days of 12 hours at $55 an hour with no other costs. What is the change order value?

    Show answer
    8 × 5 × 12 = 480 hours. 480 × 55 = $26,400.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud, safety and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Turnaround staffing handles sensitive personal data for hundreds of workers in a few weeks: passports, visas, medical and drug test results, background checks, bank details and home addresses. It also controls who can enter a hazardous site. One weak link can cause a data breach, a payroll fraud or an unsafe person on site.

    Security here means protecting people and the client: keeping data private, controlling who can see and change it, preventing fraud in timesheets and payments, keeping unqualified or unchecked people off site and following labour, privacy and safety law. Confirm the exact legal duties with your privacy, safety and legal teams.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Leak of worker ID or medical dataIdentity theft and legal claimsEncrypt files, use secure portals and limit access
    Fake certificatesUnsafe work and failed weldsVerify tickets with the issuer
    Ghost workers on timesheetsPayroll fraudMatch timesheets with gate swipe records
    Changed bank detailsWages paid to fraudstersTwo-step approval for bank changes
    Badge sharingUnchecked people on sitePhoto badges and gate checks

    🛡️ Security for: High-intensity timeline staffing

    • Store candidate pools and CVs in the applicant tracking system, not on personal phones.
    • Use opt-in for bulk job messages and honour opt-out requests quickly.
    • Never share candidate medical or background results with recruiters who do not need them.

    🛡️ Security for: TAR lifecycle comprehension

    • Share schedules and histograms only with people who need them, because they reveal client commercial plans.
    • Do not post shutdown dates publicly before the client allows it.
    • Keep lessons-learned data anonymised when worker names are not needed.

    🛡️ Security for: Mobilization ramp-up planning

    • Share passports and visas only through secure portals with travel and immigration vendors.
    • Issue badges only after induction and checks are recorded.
    • Collect and deactivate badges at release and record it.

    🛡️ Security for: Client interface

    • Send rate cards and invoices only to named client contacts.
    • Require two approvals for any change to worker bank details or rates.
    • Keep change orders and timesheets in a locked register that cannot be edited after approval.

    🧪 Measuring security

    Gate match rate = Timesheet entries matching gate records ÷ Timesheet entries checked × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: Finance checks 500 timesheet entries and 494 match the gate swipe records. Gate match rate = 494 ÷ 500 × 100 = 98.8 percent. The 6 mismatches are investigated and 2 are found to be hours claimed by workers who had already left site.

    Security checklist

    • Worker data is encrypted and shared only through approved channels.
    • Certificates are verified with the issuer before work starts.
    • Timesheets are matched with gate records every week.
    • Bank detail changes need two approvals.
    • Badges are issued after induction and collected at release.
    • Staff are trained on privacy and fraud each year.

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: High-intensity timeline staffing

    How do you calculate headcount for a turnaround craft?

    Show model answer
    Divide planned man-hours by the productive hours each worker gives: hours per shift times shift days times a productivity factor. Then add a reserve for no-shows and early leavers.

    Why does a sourcing funnel matter?

    Show model answer
    Many candidates drop out at each stage, so you must contact far more people than you need. The funnel tells you how many to contact and where the losses happen.

    Two days before start you are 20 welders short. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Check the reserve and pools, call partner agencies, offer travelling crews or a completion bonus, and tell the client at once with the impact and the plan. Fill critical path work first.

    Your fill rate is 96 percent overall. Is that good?

    Show model answer
    Only if the weak crafts are not critical. I would check fill rate by craft and shift, because a 70 percent fill on coded welders can stop the job even with a high total.

    You need 30 workers. Pass rates multiply to 0.25. How many do you contact?

    Show model answer
    30 ÷ 0.25 = 120 candidates.

    💬 Interview Questions: TAR lifecycle comprehension

    What are the main phases of a turnaround?

    Show model answer
    Scope and planning, scope freeze, pre-shutdown work, shutdown and decontamination, execution, start-up and demobilization. Each phase has a different craft mix.

    What is emergent work?

    Show model answer
    New work found when equipment is opened, such as extra corrosion repairs. It often adds 10 to 25 percent to man-hours, so you keep a pool for it.

    The SPI is 0.88 on day 8. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Find which areas and crafts are behind, check whether the cause is labour, permits, scaffolds or materials, and propose options to the client such as extra crews on the critical path.

    Why does staffing care about scope freeze?

    Show model answer
    The final labour estimate depends on frozen scope. New scope after the freeze changes headcount and sourcing at short notice, so it needs a formal change.

    A unit makes 30,000 barrels a day at a $7 margin. What does a 3-day overrun cost?

    Show model answer
    30,000 × 7 = $210,000 a day. 3 × 210,000 = $630,000.

    💬 Interview Questions: Mobilization ramp-up planning

    What does gate-ready mean?

    Show model answer
    The worker has passed all checks, is inducted, badged, has PPE and can start work. Arrival on site is not enough.

    How do you set the latest start date for mobilization?

    Show model answer
    Take the first shift date and subtract the longest lead time item, often a background check or visa. Anyone who has not started by then will be late.

    Induction cannot cope with arrivals. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Add sessions and trainers, level arrivals across more days, pre-load paperwork online and bring the long-lead crafts first.

    How do you plan ramp-down?

    Show model answer
    Release crafts as the planner confirms work complete, keep emergent cover, stagger releases, collect tools and badges and plan remobilization for good crews.

    50 fitters wait 3 hours at a loaded rate of $65. What is the idle cost?

    Show model answer
    50 × 3 × 65 = $9,750.

    💬 Interview Questions: Client interface

    What should a daily client report contain?

    Show model answer
    Headcount against plan by craft and shift, arrivals and releases, no-shows, safety events, risks for the next 48 hours and decisions needed.

    What is in a staffing markup?

    Show model answer
    Payroll taxes, insurance, recruitment and mobilization costs, overheads and the agency margin. It must be checked against real costs before bidding.

    The client asks a site supervisor for 15 extra workers. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Thank them, confirm the need, then raise a written change order with rates and duration for the contracts team to approve before workers start.

    The client disputes overtime hours. How do you respond?

    Show model answer
    Reconcile timesheets with gate records and supervisor approvals, share the evidence and correct any real errors quickly.

    Pay is $50 an hour and markup is 35 percent. What is the bill rate?

    Show model answer
    50 × 1.35 = $67.50 an hour.
