    Course Overview

    Turnaround Cost & Timesheet Auditor: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a Turnaround Cost and Timesheet Auditor makes sure every hour billed on a shutdown was really worked, correctly paid and correctly charged. It covers four skills: daily turnaround timesheet reconciliation, overtime audits, standby rate validation and high-volume invoice generation.

    The role matters because labour is often the largest cost of a turnaround. With thousands of workers on 12-hour shifts, small errors add up fast. A 1 percent error on a $20 million labour bill is $200,000. The auditor who reconciles daily, checks overtime and standby rules and produces clean invoices protects both the client and the contractor, and prevents disputes that drag on for months.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples in the main lessons. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures are illustrative and use US dollars. Overtime law, contract terms and tax rules differ by country and contract, so always confirm current rules with your commercial, payroll and legal teams.

    Who this course is for

    • Cost controllers and commercial staff on turnarounds
    • Payroll and billing teams at industrial staffing agencies
    • Client contract and cost engineers who check contractor invoices
    • Internal auditors who review shutdown costs

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What timesheet reconciliation is and why daily matters

    Timesheet reconciliation means comparing the hours on each worker's timesheet with independent records, mainly gate swipe data, and with the roster and supervisor approvals. Differences are investigated and fixed before the hours are paid or billed.

    Daily reconciliation matters because errors are easier to fix while memories are fresh. If you wait until the end of the turnaround, you face thousands of mismatches, missing approvals and arguments that nobody can settle.

    TermMeaning
    TimesheetRecord of hours a worker claims
    Gate recordBadge swipe in and out of site
    On-site timeTime between gate in and gate out
    Approved hoursHours signed off by a supervisor
    VarianceDifference between timesheet and gate hours
    ToleranceAccepted difference, such as 30 minutes for walking and changing
    Timesheet variance = Timesheet hours − Gate hours
    🧪
    Practical Example: A worker's timesheet shows 12.5 hours. Gate records show 11.25 hours on site. Variance = 12.5 − 11.25 = 1.25 hours. The tolerance is 0.5 hours, so the line is flagged for the supervisor to explain.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reconciling only at the end of the turnaround
    • Accepting timesheets without supervisor approval
    • Having no agreed tolerance

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A timesheet shows 13 hours and gate data shows 12.25. What is the variance?

    Show answer
    13 − 12.25 = 0.75 hours.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Data sources, tolerances and matching rules

    You need three data sources: timesheets, gate records and the roster. Each has gaps. Gate records miss workers who tailgate or use a different gate. Rosters show plans, not reality. Timesheets can be filled in late from memory.

    Agree matching rules with the client: the tolerance, how breaks are treated, which gate events count and who can approve differences. Write them down so every auditor applies the same rules.

    SourceShowsCommon gap
    TimesheetClaimed hours and job codesLate entry, rounding up
    Gate recordTime on siteMissed swipes, tailgating
    RosterPlanned shiftNot updated for swaps
    Supervisor approvalHours agreedBulk approvals without checks
    Job codeWhere hours are chargedWrong code
    Match rate = Timesheet lines within tolerance ÷ Timesheet lines checked × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: On day 9 there were 1,640 timesheet lines. 1,598 were within the 0.5-hour tolerance of gate records. Match rate = 1,598 ÷ 1,640 × 100 = 97.4 percent. The 42 flagged lines go to supervisors.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Trusting one source alone
    • Different auditors using different tolerances
    • Ignoring job codes when checking hours

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    2,400 lines checked and 2,352 within tolerance. What is the match rate?

    Show answer
    2,352 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 98 percent.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running daily reconciliation step by step

    Each morning, export the previous day's timesheets, gate records and roster. Match them by worker ID and date. Flag lines outside tolerance, missing timesheets, workers on site with no timesheet and timesheets with no gate record.

    Send flagged lines to supervisors with a 24-hour deadline. Hold unexplained lines from billing. Record every decision so the audit trail is complete.

    StepOutput
    1. Export timesheets, gate data and rosterDaily data set
    2. Match by worker ID and dateMatched lines
    3. Flag exceptionsException list
    4. Send to supervisorsExplanations or corrections
    5. Hold unexplained linesClean billing set
    6. Record decisionsAudit trail
    Value of held hours = Held hours × Bill rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: On day 12, 64 hours are held because supervisors have not explained them. The average bill rate is $72. Value held = 64 × 72 = $4,608, which will not be billed until the lines are cleared.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Billing lines that are still unexplained
    • Matching by name instead of worker ID
    • No deadline for supervisor answers

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    40 hours are held at a bill rate of $68. What is the value held?

    Show answer
    40 × 68 = $2,720.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Reconciliation KPIs: match rate, open exceptions and approval timeliness

    Track the daily match rate, the number and age of open exceptions, the share of timesheets approved on time and the hours rejected after review. A falling match rate is an early warning of a process or fraud problem.

    Report by crew, supervisor and contractor. If one supervisor's crews have many exceptions, the supervisor may be approving timesheets without checking.

    KPIFormulaTypical target
    Daily match rateLines within tolerance ÷ Lines checkedOver 98 percent
    Open exceptionsCount and age in daysNone older than 2 days
    On-time approvalTimesheets approved within 24 hours ÷ TimesheetsOver 95 percent
    Rejected hoursHours removed after reviewTracked by crew
    Missing timesheetsWorkers on site with no timesheetZero
    On-time approval rate = Timesheets approved within 24 hours ÷ Timesheets submitted × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: In week 2, 9,800 timesheets were submitted and 9,212 were approved within 24 hours. Rate = 9,212 ÷ 9,800 × 100 = 94 percent, just under the 95 percent target. Three supervisors account for most late approvals and are reminded.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Letting exceptions age for weeks
    • Not reporting by supervisor
    • Treating missing timesheets as zero hours without checking

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    6,000 timesheets and 5,820 approved on time. What is the rate?

    Show answer
    5,820 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = 97 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: fraud patterns, data analytics and segregation of duties

    At expert level you look for patterns: identical hours for a whole crew every day, hours claimed after a worker's release date, two timesheets for one worker on the same day, and hours on days the gate shows no entry. Simple analytics on the whole data set find these faster than sampling.

    You also design controls. The person who enters timesheets should not approve them, and the person who approves them should not bill them. Changes after approval need a reason and a second approval.

    PatternWhat it may meanControl
    Same hours for all of a crewBulk entry without checkingSpot checks against gate data
    Hours after release dateGhost hoursMatch against release dates
    Duplicate timesheetsDouble billingDuplicate detection
    No gate entryWorker not on siteHold and investigate
    Changes after approvalPossible manipulationSecond approval and audit trail
    Duplicate rate = Duplicate timesheet lines ÷ Total timesheet lines × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: An analytics check of 48,000 lines finds 36 duplicates where the same worker and date appear twice. Duplicate rate = 36 ÷ 48,000 × 100 = 0.075 percent. The duplicates are removed before invoicing and the entry system is changed to block them.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Sampling only, with no full data checks
    • One person entering, approving and billing
    • Allowing edits after approval with no record

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    30,000 lines and 21 duplicates. What is the duplicate rate?

    Show answer
    21 ÷ 30,000 × 100 = 0.07 percent.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What an overtime audit is and why it matters

    An overtime audit checks that overtime hours were authorised, worked and calculated correctly. On a turnaround with 12-hour shifts, overtime can be 40 percent or more of all hours, so mistakes in overtime rules have a large effect on cost.

    Overtime rules come from law, union agreements and the contract. They may set overtime after a number of hours in a day, after a number of hours in a week, on rest days or on public holidays, often at time and a half or double time.

    TermMeaning
    Straight timeNormal hours at the base rate
    OvertimeHours above a threshold at a higher rate
    Time and a half1.5 times the base rate
    Double time2 times the base rate
    Daily thresholdOvertime after a set number of hours a day
    Weekly thresholdOvertime after a set number of hours a week
    Overtime share = Overtime hours ÷ Total hours × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: In week 3 the turnaround worked 96,000 hours, of which 41,000 were overtime. Overtime share = 41,000 ÷ 96,000 × 100 = 42.7 percent. The budget assumed 40 percent, so the cost forecast is updated.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Applying the wrong threshold for the site
    • Not checking that overtime was authorised
    • Treating all overtime at the same multiplier

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    60,000 hours were worked and 24,000 were overtime. What is the overtime share?

    Show answer
    24,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = 40 percent.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Daily, weekly and premium overtime rules

    A weekly rule such as overtime after 40 hours pays a worker on seven 12-hour shifts 40 straight and 44 overtime hours. A daily rule such as overtime after 8 hours a day pays 8 straight and 4 overtime hours each day. Some contracts combine both and say which applies when they overlap. Some add double time on the seventh day or holidays.

    Write each contract's rules in a table and apply them in a calculator. Check a sample by hand every week.

    RuleExample
    Weekly thresholdOvertime after 40 hours a week at 1.5 times
    Daily thresholdOvertime after 8 or 10 hours a day at 1.5 times
    Seventh dayAll hours at 2 times on the seventh consecutive day
    Public holidayDouble time or holiday premium
    No pyramidingThe same hour is not paid two premiums
    Weekly pay (weekly rule) = 40 × Rate + (Hours − 40) × Rate × 1.5
    🧪
    Practical Example: A rigger works 84 hours in a week at $30 an hour with overtime after 40 hours at 1.5 times. Pay = 40 × 30 + 44 × 30 × 1.5 = 1,200 + 1,980 = $3,180.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying the same hour two premiums when the contract forbids it
    • Using a daily rule where the contract uses a weekly rule
    • Missing seventh-day or holiday rules

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A worker works 72 hours at $28 with overtime after 40 hours at 1.5 times. What is the weekly pay?

    Show answer
    40 × 28 + 32 × 28 × 1.5 = 1,120 + 1,344 = $2,464.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running an overtime audit step by step

    Take a week of approved timesheets. Recalculate straight time and overtime for every worker using the contract rules. Compare with what payroll paid and what was billed. Check that overtime above the planned shift was authorised by a named supervisor.

    List every difference with its value. Separate errors in pay from errors in billing, because they need different fixes.

    StepOutput
    1. Get contract overtime rulesRules table
    2. Recalculate hours by ruleExpected straight and overtime
    3. Compare with payrollPay differences
    4. Compare with invoicesBilling differences
    5. Check authorisationUnauthorised overtime list
    6. Report and correctCorrections and root causes
    Overtime billing error = Billed overtime value − Correct overtime value
    🧪
    Practical Example: For a crew, billed overtime is 1,320 hours at $90, which is $118,800. Recalculation shows 1,260 overtime hours, which is $113,400. Billing error = 118,800 − 113,400 = $5,400 overbilled.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Checking only payroll and not billing
    • Not checking authorisation for extra hours
    • Reporting differences without values

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Billed overtime is $80,000 and the correct value is $76,500. What is the error?

    Show answer
    80,000 − 76,500 = $3,500 overbilled.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Overtime KPIs: share, cost, authorisation and errors

    Track overtime share against budget, overtime cost, the share of overtime hours with authorisation and audit errors found. Overtime above budget is not always wrong, but it must be explained.

    Break overtime down by craft, area and reason, such as emergent work, schedule recovery or absence cover. This shows the client where the money went.

    KPIFormulaTypical target
    Overtime shareOvertime hours ÷ Total hoursWithin budget
    Overtime costOvertime hours × Overtime rateWithin forecast
    Authorised overtimeAuthorised overtime hours ÷ Overtime hours100 percent
    Audit error rateLines with overtime errors ÷ Lines auditedUnder 1 percent
    Overtime by reasonHours by causeExplained
    Overtime premium cost = Overtime hours × Base rate × (Multiplier − 1)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The turnaround worked 41,000 overtime hours at an average base bill rate of $60 and a 1.5 multiplier. Premium cost = 41,000 × 60 × 0.5 = $1,230,000. This is the extra cost above paying the same hours at straight time.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reporting overtime without reasons
    • Not separating premium cost from total cost
    • Ignoring unauthorised overtime

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    20,000 overtime hours at a base rate of $50 and a 1.5 multiplier. What is the premium cost?

    Show answer
    20,000 × 50 × 0.5 = $500,000.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: overtime control, fatigue links and contract disputes

    At expert level you help the client control overtime before it happens: approval rules for holdovers, daily overtime reports and links to fatigue limits. High overtime often means crews are too small or work is behind.

    When disputes arise, you settle them with evidence: the contract clause, approved timesheets, gate records and authorisations. Keep calm, precise and fair to both sides.

    AreaExpert practice
    ApprovalNamed approval for overtime beyond the shift
    Daily reportOvertime by crew and reason
    Fatigue linkOvertime checked against hour limits
    DisputesClause, timesheets, gate data, approvals
    ForecastOvertime trend in the cost forecast
    Overtime forecast = Overtime hours to date + Remaining days × Average daily overtime hours
    🧪
    Practical Example: By day 18, overtime is 52,000 hours. The average daily overtime is 2,900 hours and 10 days remain. Forecast = 52,000 + 10 × 2,900 = 81,000 overtime hours.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Approving overtime that breaks fatigue limits
    • Settling disputes by opinion instead of evidence
    • Leaving overtime out of the cost forecast

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Overtime to date is 30,000 hours, daily average 1,800 and 8 days remain. What is the forecast?

    Show answer
    30,000 + 8 × 1,800 = 44,400 hours.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What standby rates are and why they need validation

    Standby is time when workers or equipment are available and ready but cannot work for reasons outside the contractor's control, such as a permit delay, a gas alarm, bad weather or a client instruction to stop. Many contracts pay standby at a lower rate than working time, or at the full rate for a short time and less after that.

    Standby needs validation because it is easy to claim and hard to prove later. The auditor checks that the cause was really outside the contractor's control, that the time was recorded when it happened and that the rate and limits in the contract were applied.

    TermMeaning
    StandbyReady to work but unable to due to an outside cause
    Waiting timeShort delay, often unpaid up to a limit
    Stand-downInstruction to stop work for a period
    Standby rateRate paid for standby, often a share of the full rate
    Delay logRecord of each delay, cause and time
    Contractor-caused delayDelay the contractor must bear
    Standby cost = Workers × Standby hours × Standby rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: A gas alarm stops 40 welders for 3 hours. The contract pays standby at 75 percent of the $76 bill rate, which is $57. Standby cost = 40 × 3 × 57 = $6,840.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying standby for delays caused by the contractor
    • Accepting standby claims with no delay log
    • Using the full rate when the contract sets a lower one

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    25 fitters stand by for 2 hours at a standby rate of $54. What is the cost?

    Show answer
    25 × 2 × 54 = $2,700.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Contract standby clauses and evidence

    Read the standby clause carefully. It may say which causes qualify, a free waiting period before standby starts, the rate as a percentage, daily caps, how weather is treated and what notice the contractor must give. Some contracts expect workers to be moved to other work where possible before standby is paid.

    Evidence must be made at the time: a delay log signed by the client's area lead, permit records, gas alarm logs, weather data and the instruction to stop.

    Clause itemExample
    Qualifying causesPermit delays, client stop, gas alarm, severe weather
    Free waiting timeFirst 30 minutes per event not paid
    Standby rate75 percent of the bill rate
    Daily capNo more than 4 standby hours per worker per day
    MitigationContractor must redeploy workers where possible
    NoticeDelay reported to the client within 2 hours
    Payable standby hours = Event hours − Free waiting time (not below zero, within the cap)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A permit delay lasts 2.5 hours. The contract gives 0.5 hours free and a 4-hour daily cap. Payable standby = 2.5 − 0.5 = 2 hours per worker.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Ignoring the free waiting time
    • Paying above the daily cap
    • Not checking whether workers could have been redeployed

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A stop lasts 1.75 hours with 0.5 hours free. How many payable hours?

    Show answer
    1.75 − 0.5 = 1.25 hours.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Validating standby claims step by step

    For each claim, find the delay log entry, check the cause against the contract and confirm it was signed by the client's representative. Check the workers on the claim against timesheets and gate records for that day.

    Apply the free time, rate and cap. Check whether some workers were redeployed. Approve, reduce or reject the claim, and record why.

    StepOutput
    1. Find the delay log entryEvidence of event
    2. Check cause against contractQualifying or not
    3. Check signatures and noticeValid record
    4. Check workers on siteMatched to gate data
    5. Apply free time, rate and capPayable value
    6. Approve, reduce or rejectDecision with reason
    Validated standby value = Workers validated × Payable hours × Standby rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: A claim lists 50 riggers for 3 hours of standby after a client stop. Gate data shows 46 riggers on site. Free time is 0.5 hours. The standby rate is $42. Validated value = 46 × 2.5 × 42 = $4,830, against a claim of 50 × 3 × 42 = $6,300.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Approving claims without a signed delay log
    • Not checking which workers were on site
    • Approving without recording the reason

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A claim lists 30 workers for 2 hours. 28 were on site, free time is 0.5 hours and the rate is $50. What is the validated value?

    Show answer
    28 × 1.5 × 50 = $2,100.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Standby KPIs: share of hours, causes and claim outcomes

    Track standby hours as a share of total hours, standby by cause, claims approved, reduced and rejected, and the time to validate. A high standby share points to planning problems such as permit delays.

    Report standby by cause to the client. Permit delays and late materials are often the client's side to fix; contractor-caused delays are not paid.

    KPIFormulaTypical use
    Standby shareStandby hours ÷ Total hoursPlanning health
    Standby by causeHours by causeFocus improvements
    Claim reduction(Claimed − Validated) ÷ ClaimedClaim quality
    Validation timeDays from claim to decisionUnder 5 days
    Rejected claimsRejected ÷ ClaimsTracked
    Claim reduction rate = (Claimed value − Validated value) ÷ Claimed value × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Standby claims in week 2 total $84,000. Validated value is $71,400. Reduction = (84,000 − 71,400) ÷ 84,000 × 100 = 15 percent. Most reductions came from workers listed who were not on site.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Not tracking standby by cause
    • Letting claims wait weeks for decisions
    • Not feeding causes back to planners

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Claims total $50,000 and validated value is $43,000. What is the reduction rate?

    Show answer
    (50,000 − 43,000) ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 14 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: equipment standby, weather and fair settlement

    At expert level you validate equipment standby as well as labour. Cranes, generators and specialist tools may have standby rates in the rental or contract terms. Check whether the equipment was really idle and ready, not broken down, which is usually the supplier's risk.

    Weather standby needs clear rules: wind limits for lifts, lightning rules and heat stress stops. Use official or site weather records. Aim for fair settlement: pay what the contract allows, reject what it does not, and explain both.

    AreaExpert practice
    Equipment standbyCheck ready condition and rental terms
    BreakdownsUsually not payable as standby
    WeatherSite weather records and stated limits
    MitigationEvidence that workers were redeployed where possible
    SettlementClear reasons for each decision
    Crane standby cost = Standby hours × Crane standby rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: High wind stops a crane for 6 hours. The rental terms pay standby at $150 an hour after the first hour. Cost = (6 − 1) × 150 = $750.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying standby for broken equipment
    • Using weather apps instead of site records
    • Rejecting fair claims to save money

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A crane is on weather standby for 5 hours, with the first hour free and a $160 standby rate. What is the cost?

    Show answer
    (5 − 1) × 160 = $640.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What high-volume invoice generation is and why accuracy matters

    High-volume invoice generation means building invoices from tens of thousands of approved timesheet lines, rate cards, allowances, standby and change orders, often weekly. Each line must be traceable to approved data.

    Accuracy matters because clients check invoices closely. One error can cause the whole invoice to be rejected and delay payment. Clean, fast invoices improve cash flow and trust.

    TermMeaning
    Invoice lineOne charge, such as a worker's hours for a day or week

    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Invoice structure: labour, allowances, standby and change orders

    A turnaround invoice usually has sections: labour by craft and rate type, per diems and allowances, mobilization fees, standby, change orders, expenses and credits. Each section has its own source data and approval.

    Agree the format with the client before the first invoice. Use the same structure, codes and backup every week so the client can check quickly.

    SectionSourceApproval
    LabourApproved timesheets and rate cardSupervisor and client
    Per diemsDays worked and contract rateContract terms
    MobilizationMobilization recordsContract terms
    StandbyValidated claimsClient representative
    Change ordersSigned change ordersClient contracts team
    CreditsCorrections from auditsAuditor
    Invoice total = Labour + Allowances + Standby + Change orders + Expenses − Credits
    🧪
    Practical Example: A weekly invoice has labour $2,088,000, per diems $252,000, standby $18,400, change orders $64,000, expenses $9,600 and credits $5,400. Total = 2,088,000 + 252,000 + 18,400 + 64,000 + 9,600 − 5,400 = $2,426,600 before tax and retention.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Mixing change order work into normal labour lines
    • Forgetting credits from audits
    • Changing format every week

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Labour $900,000, allowances $80,000, standby $5,000, change orders $20,000 and credits $3,000. What is the total?

    Show answer
    900,000 + 80,000 + 5,000 + 20,000 − 3,000 = $1,002,000.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Building the invoice step by step

    Close the period: lock approved timesheets and reconciled data. Apply the current rate card by worker, craft and shift. Add allowances, validated standby and approved change orders. Apply credits. Run checks: totals against hours, rates against the rate card and lines against approvals.

    Produce the invoice and a backup file that ties every line to its source. Get internal approval, then send to the client by the agreed date.

    StepOutput
    1. Lock the periodFinal approved data
    2. Apply rate cardPriced labour lines
    3. Add allowances, standby and change ordersFull charge list
    4. Apply creditsNet charges
    5. Run checksChecked invoice
    6. Approve and send with backupIssued invoice
    Days to invoice = Invoice issue date − Period end date
    🧪
    Practical Example: The billing week ended on Sunday 14 September and the invoice was issued on Thursday 18 September. Days to invoice = 4 days. The contract target is 5 days, so it is on time.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Invoicing before the period is locked
    • No automated checks on rates
    • Backup that cannot be tied to invoice lines

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The period ended on 6 October and the invoice was issued on 13 October. How many days to invoice?

    Show answer
    7 days.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Invoice KPIs: accuracy, rejection, days to invoice and cash

    Track invoice accuracy from client checks, the share of invoices rejected or disputed, days to invoice and days to payment. Errors cause rejections, and rejections delay cash.

    Learn from every query. Group them by cause, such as wrong rate, missing approval or duplicate lines, and fix the cause in the process.

    KPIFormulaTypical target
    Invoice line accuracyCorrect lines ÷ Lines checkedOver 99.5 percent
    Rejection rateInvoices rejected ÷ Invoices issuedNear zero
    Days to invoiceIssue date − Period endWithin contract target
    Days to paymentPayment date − Issue dateWithin payment terms
    Disputed valueValue disputed ÷ Value invoicedUnder 1 percent
    Disputed value share = Value disputed ÷ Value invoiced × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Over the turnaround, $18,400,000 was invoiced and the client disputed $147,200. Disputed share = 147,200 ÷ 18,400,000 × 100 = 0.8 percent.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Not grouping queries by cause
    • Ignoring small disputes that add up
    • Not tracking days to payment

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    $12,000,000 invoiced and $96,000 disputed. What is the disputed share?

    Show answer
    96,000 ÷ 12,000,000 × 100 = 0.8 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: cost reporting, forecasts and billing integrity

    At expert level the invoice data feeds the cost report. Compare actual cost with budget by craft and area, and forecast the final cost with the remaining days. Early, honest forecasts help the client decide.

    You also protect billing integrity: segregation of duties, locked periods, rate card version control and audit trails. Never alter approved data to make an invoice look better.

    AreaExpert practice
    Cost reportActual vs budget by craft and area
    ForecastCost to date plus remaining cost
    Rate card controlOne approved version in use
    Locked periodsNo changes without a credit or debit note
    Audit trailEvery change recorded with a reason
    Forecast final cost = Cost to date + Remaining days × Average daily cost
    🧪
    Practical Example: By day 20, labour cost is $14,200,000. The average daily cost over the last 5 days is $690,000 and 8 days remain. Forecast = 14,200,000 + 8 × 690,000 = $19,720,000. The budget is $19,000,000, so a forecast overrun of $720,000 is reported.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Forecasting from the original plan instead of actual trends
    • Editing locked periods directly
    • Using different rate card versions in one invoice

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Cost to date is $8,000,000, daily cost is $400,000 and 6 days remain. What is the forecast?

    Show answer
    8,000,000 + 6 × 400,000 = $10,400,000.

    How to test and prove the quality of each skill's work.

    ✅ Testing & Quality: Daily timesheet reconciliation

    Reconciliation is tested by re-performing a sample from raw data. An auditor who did not do the daily matching re-checks lines.

    CheckHow to test itPass standard

    ToleranceCheck tolerance usedAs agreed
    Held linesCheck none were billedAll held
    DecisionsCheck reasons on cleared linesAll recorded
    Re-performance match = Lines with the same result on re-check ÷ Lines re-checked × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: An auditor re-matches 80 lines from raw data and gets the same result on 79. One line used the wrong date because of a night shift crossing midnight. Match = 79 ÷ 80 × 100 = 98.75 percent, so the matching rule for night shifts is fixed.

    ✅ Testing & Quality: Overtime audits

    🧪

    🏗️ Real-World Project: Reconciling a day of 2,000 timesheet lines

    Skill: Daily timesheet reconciliation

    On day 10 there are 2,000 timesheet lines. 1,950 are within the 0.5-hour tolerance. One worker's timesheet shows 13 hours against 11.5 hours of gate time. 48 hours are held at an average bill rate of $74. An analytics check over 40,000 lines found 28 duplicates. In week 2, 10,000 timesheets were submitted and 9,600 approved within 24 hours.

    Lines2,000
    Within tolerance1,950
    Example worker13 vs 11.5 hours
    Held hours48 at $74
    Duplicates28 of 40,000
    On-time approval9,600 of 10,000

    Your tasks

    1. Calculate the match rate.
    2. Calculate the example variance and decide if it is flagged.
    3. Calculate the value of held hours.
    4. Calculate the duplicate rate and on-time approval rate.

    Check your work

    Show worked solution

On-time approval = 9,600 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = 96 percent. 5) Example: block duplicate worker and date entries in the timesheet system.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud, safety and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Cost and timesheet audit handles large volumes of sensitive data: worker IDs, hours, gate records, pay rates, bank details, contract rates and invoices worth millions. This data is a target for fraud, and errors can cost both client and contractor heavily.

    Security here means protecting personal and commercial data, keeping duties separate, locking approved data, preventing ghost hours, duplicate billing and rate manipulation, and keeping a full audit trail. Confirm the exact legal duties with your privacy, finance and legal teams.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Ghost hoursPaying for work not doneGate match and release date checks
    Duplicate billingOverchargingDuplicate detection
    Rate manipulationOvercharging or underpayingRate card version control
    Edits after approvalHidden changesLocked periods and audit trail
    Data leak of rates or payCommercial and privacy harmAccess controls

    🛡️ Security for: Daily timesheet reconciliation

    • Give timesheet entry, approval and billing to different people.
    • Restrict gate data access to auditors and security.
    • Keep an audit trail of every change to approved timesheets.

    🛡️ Security for: Overtime audits

    • Lock the overtime rules table and control changes.
    • Keep payroll and billing data access separate where possible.
    • Report any pressure to approve unsafe or false overtime.

    🛡️ Security for: Standby rate validation

    • Keep delay logs signed and stored securely.
    • Restrict who can approve standby claims.
    • Record every standby decision with evidence.

    🛡️ Security for: High-volume invoice generation

    • Use one approved rate card version and control changes.
    • Send invoices only to named client contacts.
    • Make changes to locked periods only through credit or debit notes.

    🧪 Measuring security

    Segregation compliance = Timesheet lines entered, approved and billed by different people ÷ Lines checked × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: An internal auditor checks 500 lines and finds 494 where entry, approval and billing were done by different people. In 6 lines, one supervisor entered and approved their own crew's hours. Compliance = 494 ÷ 500 × 100 = 98.8 percent, so the system is changed to block self-approval.

    Security checklist

    • Entry, approval and billing are done by different people.
    • Approved periods are locked with an audit trail.
    • Analytics run weekly for duplicates and ghost hours.
    • Only one rate card version is used.
    • Standby decisions are supported by evidence.
    • Staff are trained on privacy and fraud each year.

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Daily timesheet reconciliation

    Why reconcile timesheets daily?

    Show model answer
    Errors are easier to fix while memories and records are fresh, and the backlog stays small. At close-out it becomes impossible to resolve thousands of mismatches.

    What sources do you use for reconciliation?

    Show model answer
    Timesheets, gate records, the roster and supervisor approvals, matched by worker ID and date.

    A supervisor approves identical hours for the whole crew every day. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Spot-check the crew against gate data, discuss the findings with the supervisor and require line-by-line approval.

    What is segregation of duties here?

    Show model answer
    Different people enter, approve and bill timesheets, so no one person can create and charge false hours.

    A timesheet shows 12.75 hours and the gate shows 11.5. The tolerance is 0.5. Is it flagged?

    Show model answer
    Variance = 1.25 hours, above 0.5, so yes.

    💬 Interview Questions: Overtime audits

    What is the difference between daily and weekly overtime rules?

    Show model answer
    A daily rule pays overtime after a set number of hours each day. A weekly rule pays overtime after a set number of hours in the week. The contract decides which applies.

    What is pyramiding?

    Show model answer
    Paying the same hour two premiums, such as daily and weekly overtime. Many contracts forbid it.

    Overtime is 10 percent above budget. What do you report?

    Show model answer
    The overtime by reason, craft and area, the cost effect, the forecast and options such as adding crews to reduce overtime.

    How do you check overtime was authorised?

    Show model answer
    Compare overtime above the planned shift with named supervisor approvals and reasons.

    84 hours at $40 with overtime after 40 at 1.5. What is the pay?

    Show model answer
    40 × 40 + 44 × 40 × 1.5 = 1,600 + 2,640 = $4,240.

    💬 Interview Questions: Standby rate validation

    What is standby?

    Show model answer
    Time when workers are ready but cannot work for a reason outside the contractor's control, paid as the contract allows.

    What evidence do you need for a standby claim?

    Show model answer
    A delay log made at the time and signed by the client's representative, records of the cause and gate data showing workers on site.

    A claim lists 50 workers but only 44 were on site. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Validate only the 44 on site, apply the contract terms and record the reason for the reduction.

    Is equipment breakdown standby?

    Show model answer
    Usually not. Breakdowns are normally the supplier's or contractor's risk, but I check the contract terms.

    30 workers, 2 payable hours, standby rate $55. What is the cost?

    Show model answer
    30 × 2 × 55 = $3,300.

    💬 Interview Questions: High-volume invoice generation

    What makes a good turnaround invoice?

    Show model answer
    It is built from locked, approved data, uses the current rate card, follows the agreed format and has backup for every line.

    Why lock the period before invoicing?

    Show model answer
    So the data cannot change after pricing. Later changes go through credit or debit notes with an audit trail.

    The client rejects an invoice for wrong rates. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Find the lines and cause, correct them with a credit or new invoice, and fix the rate card version in the billing system.

    How do you forecast final labour cost?

    Show model answer
    Cost to date plus remaining days times the recent average daily cost, adjusted for known changes such as ramp-down.

    12,000 straight hours at $60 and 8,000 overtime hours at $90. What is the labour value?

    Show model answer
    720,000 + 720,000 = $1,440,000.

    📚 Course Summary: what you covered

    Daily timesheet reconciliation

    • Match timesheets, gate data and the roster every day by worker ID.
    • Use a written tolerance and hold unexplained lines.
    • Track match rate, open exceptions and approvals.
    • Run analytics for duplicates and ghost hours and keep duties separate.

    Overtime audits

    • Write each contract's overtime rules and recalculate weekly.
    • Compare payroll and billing separately.
    • Check authorisation and report overtime by reason.
    • Forecast overtime and link it to fatigue limits.

    Standby rate validation

    • Read the standby clause: causes, free time, rate and cap.
    • Require signed delay logs and match workers to gate data.
    • Record a reason for every decision.
    • Report standby by cause and validate equipment standby fairly.

    High-volume invoice generation

    • Lock the period and use one current rate card.
    • Build invoices with labour, allowances, standby, change orders and credits.
    • Run checks and send backup for every line on time.
    • Feed cost reports and forecast the final cost.

    🧮 Formula sheet

    MeasureFormula
    Timesheet varianceTimesheet hours − Gate hours
    Match rateTimesheet lines within tolerance ÷ Timesheet lines checked × 100
    Value of held hoursHeld hours × Bill rate
    On-time approval rateTimesheets approved within 24 hours ÷ Timesheets submitted × 100
    Duplicate rateDuplicate timesheet lines ÷ Total timesheet lines × 100
    Overtime shareOvertime hours ÷ Total hours × 100
    Weekly pay (weekly rule)40 × Rate + (Hours − 40) × Rate × 1.5
    Overtime billing errorBilled overtime value − Correct overtime value
    Overtime premium costOvertime hours × Base rate × (Multiplier − 1)
    Overtime forecastOvertime hours to date + Remaining days × Average daily overtime hours
    Standby costWorkers × Standby hours × Standby rate
    Payable standby hoursEvent hours − Free waiting time (not below zero, within the cap)
    Validated standby valueWorkers validated × Payable hours × Standby rate
    Claim reduction rate(Claimed value − Validated value) ÷ Claimed value × 100
    Crane standby costStandby hours × Crane standby rate
    Labour invoice valueSum of (Hours × Rate) for each line
    Invoice totalLabour + Allowances + Standby + Change orders + Expenses − Credits
    Days to invoiceInvoice issue date − Period end date
    Disputed value shareValue disputed ÷ Value invoiced × 100
    Forecast final costCost to date + Remaining days × Average daily cost
