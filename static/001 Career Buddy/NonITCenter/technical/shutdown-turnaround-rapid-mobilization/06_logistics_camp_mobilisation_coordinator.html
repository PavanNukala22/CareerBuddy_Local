    Course Overview

    Logistics & Camp Mobilisation Coordinator: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a Logistics and Camp Mobilisation Coordinator houses, moves, feeds and badges a large temporary workforce for a shutdown. It covers four skills: emergency modular housing coordination, shift-busing logistics, industrial catering scaled for more than 1,000 workers and running badging desks.

    The role matters because a turnaround workforce can double the population of a small town overnight. If beds, buses, meals or badges fail, workers arrive late, tired or hungry, shifts start short and safety slips. Done well, every worker sleeps, eats, travels and enters site smoothly, and the client never has to think about it.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples in the main lessons. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures are illustrative and use US dollars. Building, food safety, transport and access control rules differ by country and site, so always confirm current requirements with the client and local authorities.

    Who this course is for

    • Logistics and facilities coordinators supporting shutdowns
    • Camp managers and accommodation providers for industrial sites
    • Staffing agency mobilization teams
    • Site services and security staff at refineries and plants

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Explain the main options for housing a temporary workforce.
    2. Calculate bed demand from peak headcount, shifts and occupancy rules.
    3. Plan modular camp set-up, utilities and housekeeping.
    4. Design shift bus routes and calculate buses, trips and timings.
    5. Calculate bus utilisation and on-time performance.
    6. Plan catering for day and night shifts for more than 1,000 workers.
    7. Calculate meals per day, serving capacity and food cost.
    8. Apply food safety controls and handle dietary needs.
    9. Set up badging desks and calculate desk throughput and queue times.
    10. Link badges to induction, checks and release.
    11. Apply the main data protection, access control and fraud controls used in camp and site logistics.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What emergency modular housing is and why it matters

    Emergency modular housing means setting up temporary accommodation fast, usually with prefabricated units such as sleeper modules, ablution blocks, laundry and recreation units. It is used when local hotels and rentals cannot house the peak workforce, or when the site is remote.

    It matters because rest is a safety control. Workers on 12-hour shifts need a clean, quiet bed close to site. Poor housing leads to fatigue, early leavers and complaints that reach the client. A good coordinator plans beds against the histogram and keeps the camp safe and clean.

    TermMeaning
    Modular campTemporary accommodation built from prefabricated units
    Sleeper moduleUnit with bedrooms
    Ablution blockShowers and toilets
    Hot beddingSame bed used by day and night shift workers
    OccupancyBeds in use ÷ Beds available
    Mobilization lead timeTime to deliver and set up units
    Beds needed = Peak workers needing housing × (1 + Buffer percent)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The peak workforce is 1,400 and 70 percent need housing. That is 1,400 × 0.7 = 980 workers. With a 5 percent buffer, beds needed = 980 × 1.05 = 1,029 beds.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning beds for average headcount instead of peak
    • Forgetting that local hotels fill up during big shutdowns
    • Ignoring set-up lead times for modular units

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Peak is 1,000 workers, 60 percent need housing and the buffer is 5 percent. How many beds?

    Show answer
    1,000 × 0.6 = 600. 600 × 1.05 = 630 beds.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Housing options, mix and occupancy rules

    Most shutdowns use a mix: local hotels, apartments, existing camps and new modular units. Hotels are fast but limited and costly at peak. Modular camps take weeks to set up but give control and capacity. Some clients allow hot bedding, where a day-shift worker and a night-shift worker share a room at different times. Others do not, for hygiene and rest reasons.

    Check client and local rules on room sharing, beds per room, fire safety, distance to site and inspections before choosing the mix.

    OptionStrengthLimit
    Local hotelsFast and readyLimited rooms and price rises at peak
    Apartments and rentalsGood for long staysScattered, harder to manage
    Existing campsReady infrastructureMay be full or far
    Modular campLarge capacity, controlLead time, permits, utilities
    Hot beddingFewer beds neededHygiene and rest concerns
    Beds with hot bedding = Workers housed ÷ 2 (when day and night shifts are equal)
    🧪
    Practical Example: 800 workers need housing, split evenly between day and night shifts. Without hot bedding, 800 beds are needed. With hot bedding, 800 ÷ 2 = 400 beds, plus a buffer for overlaps at shift change and rest days.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using hot bedding without client approval
    • Counting hotel rooms that are not confirmed
    • Choosing housing far from site without checking travel time

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    600 workers need housing, split evenly across shifts. How many beds with hot bedding before a buffer?

    Show answer
    600 ÷ 2 = 300 beds.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Setting up a modular camp step by step

    Start from the bed curve, which is the histogram converted to housed workers by day. Choose a site with access, drainage and utilities. Get permits, order units, plan power, water, sewage and internet, then schedule delivery and installation so the camp is ready before the ramp-up.

    Plan housekeeping, security, laundry, waste and maintenance. Inspect the camp before the first arrivals and fix defects.

    StepOutput
    1. Build the bed curveBeds needed by day
    2. Choose and permit the siteApproved location
    3. Order unitsSleeper, ablution, laundry, recreation
    4. Plan utilitiesPower, water, sewage, internet
    5. Install and inspectReady camp
    6. Run housekeeping and securityClean, safe camp
    Ablution units needed = Workers housed ÷ Workers per ablution unit
    🧪
    Practical Example: A camp houses 960 workers and the standard is one ablution unit for 24 workers. Units needed = 960 ÷ 24 = 40 ablution units.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Ordering units before the site is permitted
    • Forgetting sewage and water capacity
    • Opening the camp without an inspection

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A camp houses 720 workers with one ablution unit per 20 workers. How many units are needed?

    Show answer
    720 ÷ 20 = 36 units.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Housing KPIs: occupancy, cost per bed night and complaints

    Track occupancy, cost per bed night, room turnaround after check-out, maintenance requests and complaints. Occupancy that is too low wastes money. Occupancy near 100 percent leaves no room for late arrivals or room faults.

    Report housing results daily during ramp-up and weekly during steady state. Use complaints and early leaver reasons to find problems quickly.

    KPIFormulaTypical target
    OccupancyBeds in use ÷ Beds available85 to 95 percent at peak
    Cost per bed nightHousing cost ÷ Bed nightsWithin budget
    Room turnaroundHours from check-out to readyUnder 4 hours
    Maintenance close timeHours to fix a requestUnder 24 hours
    Complaint rateComplaints ÷ Workers housedFalling over time
    Cost per bed night = Total housing cost ÷ Total bed nights
    🧪
    Practical Example: The camp cost $1,134,000 for 1,000 beds over 28 nights, all used. Bed nights = 1,000 × 28 = 28,000. Cost per bed night = 1,134,000 ÷ 28,000 = $40.50.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Running at 100 percent occupancy with no spare rooms
    • Not tracking complaints and their causes
    • Comparing camp and hotel costs without counting transport

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Housing cost $560,000 for 20,000 bed nights. What is the cost per bed night?

    Show answer
    560,000 ÷ 20,000 = $28.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: surge housing, welfare and camp risk

    At expert level you plan for surges, such as emergent work adding 200 workers in a week. Hold options on extra hotel blocks, spare modules or a second camp site. Agree in advance who pays for surge capacity.

    You also own welfare and risk: fire safety, security, alcohol rules, noise for day sleepers, mental health support, illness outbreaks and fair room allocation. A well-run camp reduces early leavers and protects the client's reputation.

    RiskControl
    Sudden surge in workersPre-agreed hotel blocks and spare modules
    Fire in sleeper unitsAlarms, drills, fire watch and inspections
    Noise disturbing day sleepersQuiet zones and shift-based room blocks
    Illness outbreakCleaning plan, isolation rooms and medical support
    Conflict and misconductCamp rules, security and fair processes
    Surge capacity = Optioned beds ÷ Expected surge workers needing housing
    🧪
    Practical Example: Emergent work may add 250 workers, and 80 percent will need housing, which is 200 beds. You hold options on 150 hotel rooms and 80 spare module beds. Surge capacity = 230 ÷ 200 = 1.15.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Mixing day and night sleepers in the same blocks
    • Having no plan for illness outbreaks
    • Agreeing surge housing with no cost agreement

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A surge of 300 workers, 70 percent needing housing, and options on 240 beds. What is the surge capacity?

    Show answer
    Need = 210 beds. 240 ÷ 210 = 1.14.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What shift-busing logistics is and why it matters

    Shift-busing logistics means moving workers between accommodation, park-and-ride lots and the site at each shift change. A 1,000-person shutdown with day and night shifts may need dozens of bus trips every morning and evening, all arriving in a narrow window before toolbox talks and permits.

    It matters because late buses mean late shifts, and every minute of a crew waiting costs money. Many sites also limit private cars for safety and traffic reasons. Good busing reduces fatigue, accidents and parking chaos.

    TermMeaning
    RoutePath from pick-up points to the site gate
    TripOne bus journey on a route
    Cycle timeTime for a bus to complete a trip and return
    Load factorSeats used ÷ Seats available
    Park-and-rideLot where workers park and take a bus
    Shift change windowTime when workers must arrive and leave
    Buses needed = Workers to move ÷ (Seats per bus × Trips per bus in the window)
    🧪
    Practical Example: 900 workers must reach the gate between 05:00 and 06:00. Buses seat 50 and each can make 2 trips in that hour. Buses needed = 900 ÷ (50 × 2) = 9 buses.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning buses for average numbers instead of peak shift
    • Ignoring traffic and gate queue time
    • Forgetting night shift returns at the same time as day shift arrivals

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    600 workers must move in one hour. Buses seat 40 and each makes 3 trips. How many buses?

    Show answer
    600 ÷ (40 × 3) = 5 buses.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Routes, cycle times and pick-up points

    Design routes from each camp, hotel cluster and park-and-ride lot. Calculate the cycle time: loading, driving to site, unloading, returning and a buffer. The cycle time decides how many trips each bus can make in the shift window.

    Keep pick-up points few, safe and well lit. Publish timetables and use the same bus for the same crew where possible so crews arrive together.

    ItemExample
    Loading time8 minutes
    Drive to gate18 minutes
    Gate and unloading6 minutes
    Return drive15 minutes
    Buffer3 minutes
    Cycle time50 minutes
    Trips per bus in window = Window minutes ÷ Cycle time minutes (round down)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The window is 90 minutes and the cycle time is 40 minutes. Trips per bus = 90 ÷ 40 = 2.25, so each bus makes 2 full trips.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Leaving out loading and gate time from cycle time
    • Too many small pick-up points that slow every route
    • Not publishing timetables to workers

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The window is 120 minutes and the cycle time is 50 minutes. How many full trips can a bus make?

    Show answer
    120 ÷ 50 = 2.4, so 2 full trips.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Planning the bus operation step by step

    Start from the roster: how many workers per shift live at each location. Build routes and cycle times, then calculate buses per route and total buses for the busiest window. Book buses and drivers with a reserve.

    Agree driver rules, vehicle checks, seat belts, speed limits and drop-off areas with site safety. Track every trip with a dispatcher and adjust as headcount changes.

    StepOutput
    1. Workers per location per shiftDemand table
    2. Build routes and cycle timesRoute plan
    3. Calculate buses per routeFleet size
    4. Book buses, drivers and reserveContracted fleet
    5. Agree safety rulesApproved transport plan
    6. Dispatch and trackLive bus board
    Total fleet = Sum of buses per route + Reserve buses
    🧪
    Practical Example: Route A needs 4 buses, route B 3 and route C 2, a total of 9. With a 10 percent reserve rounded up, add 1. Total fleet = 9 + 1 = 10 buses.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Booking no reserve buses
    • Not agreeing driver hours and safety rules
    • Not updating routes as the roster changes

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Routes need 5, 4 and 3 buses with one reserve. What is the total fleet?

    Show answer
    5 + 4 + 3 + 1 = 13 buses.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Bus KPIs: on-time arrival, load factor and cost per seat

    Track on-time arrival at the gate, load factor, missed pick-ups, incidents and cost per worker trip. A high load factor saves money but can leave workers behind. Late arrivals by route show where routes or timings need fixing.

    Report bus results to the client every day during ramp-up. Use GPS tracking where possible to confirm times.

    KPIFormulaTypical target
    On-time arrivalTrips arriving on time ÷ TripsOver 95 percent
    Load factorSeats used ÷ Seats available75 to 90 percent
    Missed pick-upsWorkers left behindZero
    Cost per worker tripBus cost ÷ Worker tripsWithin budget
    IncidentsVehicle incidentsZero
    Load factor = Seats used ÷ Seats available × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: In the morning window 20 bus trips with 50 seats carried 860 workers. Seats available = 20 × 50 = 1,000. Load factor = 860 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 86 percent.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Running at 100 percent load so late workers have no seat
    • Not tracking which routes are late
    • Ignoring vehicle incidents as contractor problems

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    15 trips with 40 seats carried 510 workers. What is the load factor?

    Show answer
    Seats = 600. 510 ÷ 600 × 100 = 85 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: traffic, fatigue, emergencies and driver control

    At expert level you manage transport risk. Coordinate with police and the client on traffic plans for peak shift change. Ban workers from driving long distances after night shift where possible and provide buses instead. Keep a plan for emergencies, such as a site evacuation that needs buses at short notice.

    Control drivers and vehicles: licences checked, vehicle inspections, driver hours limited and no phone use. Keep passenger lists so you know who is on each bus.

    RiskControl
    Traffic jams at shift changeStaggered start times and traffic plan
    Fatigued workers drivingBuses after night shift
    Site evacuationEmergency bus plan and muster points
    Unlicensed or tired driversLicence checks and driver hour limits
    Unknown passengersPassenger lists or badge scans on board
    Cost per worker trip = Total bus cost ÷ Worker trips
    🧪
    Practical Example: Busing cost $186,000 over 30 days. Each day 1,000 workers make 2 trips each. Worker trips = 1,000 × 2 × 30 = 60,000. Cost per worker trip = 186,000 ÷ 60,000 = $3.10.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • No emergency bus plan
    • Letting contractors manage driver hours with no checks
    • No record of who is on each bus

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Busing cost $120,000 for 48,000 worker trips. What is the cost per worker trip?

    Show answer
    120,000 ÷ 48,000 = $2.50.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What industrial catering is and why scale matters

    Industrial catering provides breakfast, lunch, dinner and night meals for shift workers, often from a camp kitchen, a site canteen and packed meal stations. At 1,000 or more workers, catering becomes a production operation with large kitchens, cold chains, serving lines and strict food safety controls.

    It matters because hungry or ill workers are unsafe and unproductive. A food poisoning outbreak can take dozens of workers off a shutdown in a day. Good catering keeps meal times short, food safe and nutrition right for heavy physical work.

    TermMeaning

    Serving lineCounter where meals are served

    Cold chainKeeping chilled food at safe temperatures

    Meals per day = Workers × Meals per worker per day
    🧪
    Practical Example: 1,300 workers each get 3 meals a day, plus 20 percent take an extra night snack. Meals = 1,300 × 3 + 1,300 × 0.2 = 3,900 + 260 = 4,160 meals a day.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning meals for day shift only
    • Ignoring dietary and religious needs
    • Treating catering as a small add-on service

    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Meal plans for day and night shifts

    Day and night shifts eat at different times, so the kitchen runs almost all day. Typical plans give a breakfast before shift, a hot or packed lunch during shift and dinner after shift. Night shift needs the same, shifted by 12 hours.

    Meal breaks are short, so serving speed matters. Packed meals are often used inside the plant fence to avoid long walks. Hydration stations are vital in hot weather.

    MealDay shiftNight shift
    Breakfast04:30 to 05:30 in camp16:30 to 17:30 in camp
    Mid-shift meal11:00 to 13:00 on site23:00 to 01:00 on site
    Dinner18:30 to 20:00 in camp06:30 to 08:00 in camp
    Snacks and waterThrough the shiftThrough the shift
    Packed mealsFor work areas far from canteenFor work areas far from canteen
    Packed meals = Workers on shift × Share in remote work areas
    🧪
    Practical Example: 700 workers are on day shift and 35 percent work in areas far from the canteen. Packed meals = 700 × 0.35 = 245 packed lunches a day shift.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Running one kitchen schedule for both shifts
    • No hydration plan in hot weather
    • Making workers walk too far for meals during short breaks

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    600 workers on night shift and 40 percent are in remote areas. How many packed meals?

    Show answer

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Planning catering capacity step by step

    Start from headcount by shift and day. Calculate meals by meal period, then serving capacity: number of serving lines, workers served per line per minute and the length of the meal window. Size seating so workers can eat within the break.

    Contract a caterer with large-scale experience, agree menus, dietary options, food safety controls, hygiene inspections and delivery for packed meals. Test the serving lines before peak.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Not testing serving speed before peak
    • Too few seats for the break length
    • No agreed menu for dietary needs

    Use daily headcount from the roster to order food, and adjust as releases begin. Run short satisfaction surveys and act on complaints quickly.

    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: food safety, outbreaks and supply resilience

    At expert level you treat food safety as a major site risk. Require a hazard analysis system such as HACCP, temperature logs, trained food handlers, regular inspections and samples kept from each batch where rules require. Have an outbreak plan with medical support, isolation and fast investigation.

    Build supply resilience. Large shutdowns can strain local suppliers. Agree backup suppliers, keep safe stock levels and plan for road closures or weather.

    RiskControl
    Food poisoningHACCP, temperature logs, trained staff
    Allergy reactionClear labels and allergen controls
    Supply shortageBackup suppliers and safe stock
    Water supply failureBottled water reserve
    OutbreakMedical plan, isolation, investigation
    Temperature log compliance = Checks within safe range ÷ Checks recorded × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The kitchen recorded 840 fridge and hot-holding checks in a week. 832 were within the safe range. Compliance = 832 ÷ 840 × 100 = 99 percent. The 8 out-of-range checks were on one fridge, which was repaired and its food discarded.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • No temperature logs or allergen labels
    • One supplier for everything
    • No plan for an illness outbreak

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    600 checks were recorded and 594 were within range. What is the compliance?

    Show answer
    594 ÷ 600 × 100 = 99 percent.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What a badging desk does and why it matters

    A badging desk issues site access badges to workers. It checks identity, confirms that induction and required checks are complete, takes a photo, prints the badge and activates access in the security system. At release, the badge is collected and deactivated.

    It matters because the badge is the key to a hazardous site. A wrong badge lets unchecked people in. A slow desk leaves hundreds of workers queueing while shifts start short. A badge system that is not updated at release leaves former workers with access.

    TermMeaning
    Site badgeCard or pass that allows site entry
    Access control systemSoftware that grants and logs entry
    Induction recordProof that safety training is complete
    ActivationTurning on access for a badge
    DeactivationTurning off access at release
    ThroughputWorkers badged per hour
    Desk throughput = Workers badged ÷ Desk hours
    🧪
    Practical Example: Four desks badged 480 workers in 6 hours. Desk hours = 4 × 6 = 24. Throughput = 480 ÷ 24 = 20 workers per desk hour.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Printing badges before induction is complete
    • Not deactivating badges at release
    • Having too few desks for peak arrival days

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Camp and site logistics handles personal data for thousands of people: names, IDs, photos, room numbers, travel and bus lists, dietary and medical needs and badge access logs. It also controls who can enter a hazardous site and where workers sleep. A data leak can expose workers to risk. A badge failure can let unchecked people onto site.

    Security here means protecting personal data, controlling access to camps and site, preventing fraud in bookings, meals and transport, and keeping people safe. Confirm the exact legal duties with your privacy, security and safety teams.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Room list leakWorker safety and privacyAccess-limited booking system
    Badge without checksUnchecked people on siteSystem link to tracker
    Ghost meals or bed nightsBilling fraudMatch bills to headcount and badge data
    Unknown bus passengersSecurity and emergency riskPassenger lists or badge scans
    Medical or dietary data leakHealth data breachNeed-to-know access only

    🛡️ Security for: Emergency modular housing coordination

    • Keep room lists in an access-limited booking system.
    • Check identity at camp check-in.
    • Match camp invoices with bed night records.

    🛡️ Security for: Shift-busing logistics

    • Keep passenger lists or badge scans for each trip.
    • Check driver licences and keep records secure.
    • Match bus invoices with dispatcher logs and GPS data.

    🛡️ Security for: Industrial catering for 1,000+ workers

    • Share dietary and allergy data only with catering staff who need it.
    • Match meal invoices with headcount and meal counts.
    • Control kitchen access to authorised staff.

    🛡️ Security for: Badging desk operations

    • Activate badges only when all checks are on file.
    • Require security approval for any manual override.
    • Deactivate badges on release day and audit active badges.

    🧪 Measuring security

    Access record match = Active site badges matching the live roster ÷ Active site badges × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: At the end of week 3, 1,420 badges are active and 1,409 match the live roster. Match = 1,409 ÷ 1,420 × 100 = 99.2 percent. The 11 unmatched badges belong to released workers and are deactivated at once.

    Security checklist

    • Room and bus lists are access-limited.
    • Badges are activated only after all checks.
    • Manual overrides need security approval.
    • Badges are deactivated on release day.
    • Invoices are matched with headcount, bed nights, meals and trips.
    • Staff are trained on privacy and fraud each year.
