    Course Overview

    Turnaround Demobilization Specialist: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a Turnaround Demobilization Specialist closes a shutdown cleanly: clearing the site, recovering tools, settling pay and returning hired equipment fast. It covers four skills: rapid site clearance, tool return and sign-off verification, payroll settlement sign-offs and fast equipment hand-back logistics.

    The role matters because the end of a turnaround is where money and control leak away. Scaffolds and waste left behind delay restart. Missing tools cost thousands. Wrong final pay causes disputes and claims. Hired cranes, generators and containers keep charging rent every day they are not returned. A good demobilization saves real money and leaves the client ready to run.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples in the main lessons. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures are illustrative and use US dollars. Final pay, deductions, waste and transport rules differ by country, so always confirm current rules with your payroll, legal and environmental teams.

    Who this course is for

    • Turnaround coordinators who manage the end of an outage
    • Staffing agency mobilization and payroll teams
    • Site services, stores and logistics staff
    • Contractor managers who close out work packages

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Explain what demobilization covers and why it needs a plan.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What rapid site clearance is and why it matters

    Site clearance is the work of removing everything the turnaround brought in: scaffolds, temporary lighting, hoses, materials, waste, containers, tents and barriers. Each area must be left clean and safe so operations can accept it back and start up.

    It matters because clearance is often on the path to restart. A single scaffold left on a line, or waste near a hot surface, can delay start-up or cause a fire. Clearance needs its own crews, plan and sign-offs, not leftover workers at the end of shifts.

    TermMeaning
    Scaffold strikeTaking down scaffolds
    HousekeepingCleaning and tidying an area
    Waste streamType of waste, such as metal, general or hazardous
    Area hand-backOperations accepting an area as clear
    Punch listList of small items still to finish
    Walk-downJoint inspection of an area
    Clearance progress = Areas handed back ÷ Total areas × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The turnaround has 36 work areas. By the end of day 26, 27 have been handed back. Progress = 27 ÷ 36 × 100 = 75 percent, with 9 areas left before the planned start-up on day 28.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Leaving clearance to the last day
    • Releasing scaffolders before strike is done
    • No joint walk-down with operations

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    There are 40 areas and 34 are handed back. What is the progress?

    Show answer
    34 ÷ 40 × 100 = 85 percent.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Clearance scope: scaffolds, waste and temporary facilities

    List everything to be removed in each area. Scaffolds are often the largest item. Waste must be sorted into streams: scrap metal, general waste, insulation, contaminated or hazardous waste and recyclables. Temporary facilities include lighting towers, welfare cabins, tents, fencing and power cables.

    Each stream has its own handling and disposal rules. Hazardous waste needs licensed carriers and records. Never mix streams to save time.

    ItemHow it is cleared
    ScaffoldsStrike by scaffolders, count and load out
    Scrap metalSkips for recycling
    General wasteLicensed waste contractor
    Hazardous wasteLicensed carrier with records
    Temporary power and lightingIsolated by electricians and removed
    Welfare cabins and tentsEmptied, cleaned and collected
    Scaffold strike hours = Scaffold tonnes × Strike hours per tonne
    🧪
    Practical Example: The site has 180 tonnes of scaffold to strike at 6 hours per tonne. Strike hours = 180 × 6 = 1,080 scaffolder-hours.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Mixing hazardous waste with general waste
    • Forgetting temporary power that must be isolated
    • Not counting scaffold tonnes before planning crews

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    There are 120 tonnes of scaffold at 5 hours per tonne. How many strike hours?

    Show answer
    120 × 5 = 600 scaffolder-hours.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Planning clearance step by step

    Start clearance planning before execution ends. Use the schedule to see when each area finishes, then plan strike and clean-up to follow area by area. Book scaffold, waste and transport crews and skips in advance.

    For each area, run a joint walk-down with operations and safety. Record punch items and close them before hand-back. Track progress daily on an area board.

    StepOutput
    1. List clearance scope by areaScope list
    2. Link to area finish datesClearance sequence
    3. Calculate crewsCrew plan by day
    4. Book skips and carriersWaste plan
    5. Walk down each areaPunch list
    6. Close punch items and hand backSigned hand-back
    Clearance crew size = Clearance hours ÷ (Hours per shift × Shifts available × Productivity factor)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Strike and clean-up need 1,080 hours over 6 shifts of 12 hours at 0.75 productivity. Crew = 1,080 ÷ (12 × 6 × 0.75) = 1,080 ÷ 54 = 20 workers.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning crews without counting scope
    • Waste skips arriving after areas are ready
    • Handing back areas with open punch items

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Clearance needs 720 hours over 5 shifts of 12 hours at 0.8 productivity. How many workers?

    Show answer
    12 × 5 × 0.8 = 48. 720 ÷ 48 = 15 workers.

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Clearance KPIs: hand-back rate, punch items and waste records

    Track areas handed back against plan, open punch items, waste removed by stream and safety events during strike. Strike work has its own risks, such as falling objects and manual handling.

    Report daily in the last week of the turnaround. Escalate any area that will not be clear in time for its start-up step.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Tracking only the total and not the plan date
    • Loads leaving site without waste records
    • Ignoring strike safety because work is ending

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: restart readiness, environmental compliance and lessons

    At expert level clearance is tied to restart readiness. Work with operations on the start-up sequence so the first systems to start are cleared first. Make sure no blinds, scaffolds or temporary items remain on systems being commissioned.

    You also protect the client from environmental and legal risk. Keep waste transfer notes and hazardous waste records. Record lessons, such as which areas were slow and why, for the next turnaround.

    AreaExpert practice
    Start-up sequenceClear first-start systems first
    Temporary items registerEvery temporary item logged in and out
    Waste recordsTransfer notes and hazardous waste records filed
    Environmental checksNo spills, drains clear
    Lessons learnedSlow areas and causes recorded
    Temporary item recovery = Temporary items removed ÷ Temporary items logged × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The register shows 640 temporary items logged in, such as lights, hoses and barriers. 632 have been removed. Recovery = 632 ÷ 640 × 100 = 98.75 percent. The 8 remaining items are located in a final walk-down before start-up.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Clearing areas in a random order
    • No register of temporary items
    • Missing hazardous waste records

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What tool return and sign-off is and why it matters

    During a turnaround, workers are issued tools, PPE, radios, gas monitors, harnesses and badges. At release, each item must be returned, checked and signed off. The sign-off is part of the worker's release checklist.

    It matters because lost tools are expensive and some items are safety-critical. Gas monitors, harnesses and calibrated torque wrenches must be checked before reuse. A clear process stops losses, protects calibration records and avoids arguments with workers.

    TermMeaning
    Tool issueRecording a tool given to a worker or crew
    Tool returnRecording the tool coming back
    Release checklistItems a worker must clear before leaving
    Calibrated toolTool with a calibration certificate
    QuarantineHolding a returned tool for inspection
    Write-offRecording a tool as lost or scrapped
    Tool recovery rate = Tools returned ÷ Tools issued × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The tool crib issued 2,400 tools and 2,352 came back. Recovery = 2,352 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 98 percent. 48 tools are missing.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Issuing tools with no record
    • Letting workers leave before tool sign-off
    • Returning safety equipment to stock without checks

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    1,500 tools issued and 1,485 returned. What is the recovery rate?

    Show answer
    1,485 ÷ 1,500 × 100 = 99 percent.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Tool registers, crews and safety-critical items

    Tools can be issued to a person or to a crew. Personal issue makes responsibility clear. Crew issue is faster but needs a named crew lead. Use barcodes or RFID tags where possible so returns are quick and accurate.

    Safety-critical items need extra steps on return: gas monitors need a bump test and data download, harnesses need inspection, calibrated tools need calibration status checked and lifting gear needs inspection before reuse.

    ItemReturn step
    Hand toolsScan, count, check condition
    Power toolsScan, check cables and guards
    Gas monitorsBump test and data download
    Harnesses and lanyardsVisual inspection, quarantine if damaged
    Torque wrenchesCheck calibration status
    RadiosScan, battery check
    Missing tool cost = Missing tools × Average replacement cost
    🧪
    Practical Example: 48 tools are missing with an average replacement cost of $185. Missing tool cost = 48 × 185 = $8,880.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Crew issue with no named crew lead
    • Not bump-testing gas monitors on return
    • Putting damaged harnesses back into stock

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    30 tools are missing at an average of $220. What is the cost?

    Show answer
    30 × 220 = $6,600.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running tool returns step by step

    Plan returns with the release schedule. The day before release, send each crew lead a list of tools issued to the crew. On release day, workers return items at the crib, where staff scan, check and sign off each item.

    Items that are missing are recorded and followed up the same day. Safety-critical items go to inspection or quarantine. Only when all items are cleared does the worker's release checklist show tool sign-off.

    StepOutput
    1. Pull tool lists per worker and crewReturn lists
    2. Send lists to crew leadsPrepared crews
    3. Scan and check returnsReturned items
    4. Inspect safety-critical itemsInspected or quarantined
    5. Record missing itemsMissing item log
    6. Sign off release checklistTool clearance
    Crib throughput = Workers cleared ÷ Crib staff hours
    🧪
    Practical Example: On a release day, 3 crib staff work 8 hours and clear 240 workers. Staff hours = 24. Throughput = 240 ÷ 24 = 10 workers per staff hour.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • No return lists sent to crew leads
    • Signing off workers with missing items without a record
    • Too few crib staff on big release days

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    2 crib staff work 10 hours and clear 160 workers. What is the throughput?

    Show answer
    Staff hours = 20. 160 ÷ 20 = 8 workers per staff hour.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Tool KPIs: recovery, loss cost and inspection results

    Track recovery rate, missing tool cost, quarantine rate, release delays caused by tool returns and recovery by crew. A crew with a poor recovery rate may need a stronger crew lead or personal issue.

    Agree with the client and workers in advance how lost tools are handled. Any deduction from pay for lost tools must follow the law and the worker's contract, and some places do not allow it.

    Quarantine rateItems quarantined ÷ Items returnedTracked
    Release delayWorkers delayed by tool issuesLow

    Quarantine rate = Items quarantined ÷ Items returned × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 380 harnesses returned, 19 were quarantined for damage. Quarantine rate = 19 ÷ 380 × 100 = 5 percent. Most damage was on one crew's lanyards, so their work at height is reviewed.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Deducting lost tool costs from pay without checking the law
    • Not tracking recovery by crew
    • Ignoring patterns in quarantined items

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    250 gas monitors returned and 10 quarantined. What is the quarantine rate?

    Show answer
    10 ÷ 250 × 100 = 4 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🔐 Advanced Concepts: Expert: asset tagging, calibration chains and theft prevention

    At expert level you use asset tagging such as barcodes or RFID for every tool, with issue and return linked to badges. Calibration records follow each tool so the client knows which tools were used on which joints and whether they were in calibration.

    You also prevent theft. Control crib access, check vehicles leaving site where allowed, review high-value items daily and investigate patterns. Treat workers fairly: most losses are mistakes, not theft.

    RiskControl
    High-value tool lossDaily count and tagged issue
    Calibration gapsCalibration status checked at issue and return
    TheftCrib access control and investigation of patterns
    Unfair blameClear records and fair process
    Lost data on gas monitorsData download at return
    High-value item variance = Expected high-value items − Counted high-value items
    🧪
    Practical Example: The crib expects 60 calibrated torque wrenches in stock at the end of day. The count finds 57. Variance = 60 − 57 = 3, which triggers a same-day search using issue records.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Counting high-value items only at the end of the turnaround
    • No calibration link between tools and work
    • Treating every loss as theft

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What payroll settlement is and why sign-offs matter

    Payroll settlement at demobilization means calculating and paying each worker's final pay: hours since the last pay run, overtime, night premiums, per diems, completion bonuses, travel and any lawful deductions. The release checklist feeds into it: hours approved, tools returned, badge collected.

    Sign-offs matter because final pay is where errors and disputes appear. A missing timesheet, a wrong premium or an unlawful deduction can lead to complaints, claims and loss of trust. Many places have strict deadlines for paying final wages.

    TermMeaning
    Final payLast payment to a released worker
    Approved hoursHours signed off by a supervisor
    Per diemDaily living allowance
    Completion bonusBonus paid for finishing the job
    DeductionAmount taken from pay, only where lawful
    Final pay deadlineLegal or contract time to pay
    Gross final pay = Straight hours × Rate + Overtime hours × Rate × 1.5 + Allowances + Bonus
    🧪
    Practical Example: A fitter's final period has 40 straight hours and 32 overtime hours at $34 an hour, 6 days of per diem at $85 and a $500 completion bonus. Gross = 40 × 34 + 32 × 34 × 1.5 + 6 × 85 + 500 = 1,360 + 1,632 + 510 + 500 = $4,002.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying from the roster instead of approved timesheets
    • Forgetting per diems or completion bonuses
    • Missing legal final pay deadlines

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    40 straight hours and 20 overtime hours at $30, plus 5 days per diem at $80. What is the gross?

    Show answer

    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Sign-off chain: timesheets, release checklist and approvals

    Final pay should follow a sign-off chain. The supervisor approves the last timesheets. The release checklist confirms tools, PPE and badge returned. Payroll calculates pay. A second person checks the calculation. The payroll manager approves the payment.

    Completion bonuses usually depend on the worker staying until their planned release date and meeting conditions set out in writing. Check the conditions before paying or refusing a bonus.

    Sign-off completeness = Workers with all sign-offs ÷ Workers released × 100
    🧪

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying without timesheet approval
    • Refusing bonuses without checking written conditions
    • One person calculating and approving pay alone

    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Settling final pay step by step

    Start with the release schedule. For each release wave, collect approved timesheets, release checklists and bonus decisions. Calculate pay with the rate card and allowances. Apply only deductions that are lawful and agreed in writing.

    Run a second check on every calculation. Approve and pay within the legal and contract deadline. Send each worker a clear payslip, and set up a fast route for questions.

    StepOutput
    1. Collect approved timesheetsHours to pay
    2. Collect release checklistsClearance status
    3. Decide bonus eligibilityBonus list
    4. Calculate payDraft final pay
    5. Second check and approvalApproved pay
    6. Pay and send payslipsPaid workers
    Net pay = Gross pay − Tax and statutory deductions − Other lawful deductions
    🧪
    Practical Example: A worker's gross final pay is $4,002. Tax and statutory deductions are $880. There is a lawful, agreed deduction of $120 for an unreturned radio, allowed under the worker's signed agreement and local law. Net pay = 4,002 − 880 − 120 = $3,002.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Applying deductions that are not lawful or agreed
    • No second check
    • Unclear payslips that cause queries

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Gross is $3,200, tax is $640 and there are no other deductions. What is the net pay?

    Show answer
    3,200 − 640 = $2,560.

    Find the causes of errors: missing timesheets, wrong rates, missed premiums or late bonus decisions. Fix the process, not just the payslip.

    • Fixing payslips without fixing the cause

    At expert level you know the final pay rules for every location: deadlines, what can be deducted, overtime rules and how bonuses must be treated. Get legal advice for new locations.

    You also check for fraud. Compare timesheets with gate records, check for workers paid after their release date and verify any late bank detail changes with a second person. Keep an audit trail of every sign-off and change.

    • Assuming the same final pay rules apply everywhere
    • Accepting late bank detail changes without checks
    • No audit trail of approvals

    📘 Fundamentals: What equipment hand-back is and why speed matters

    A turnaround hires large amounts of equipment: cranes, man-lifts, generators, compressors, lighting towers, welfare cabins, containers, scaffolding and specialist tools. Equipment hand-back means off-hiring each item, cleaning it, checking condition and returning it to the supplier.

    Speed matters because rental runs until the supplier accepts the item back, often by the day or week. Equipment left idle on site after its work is done is pure cost. Damage found at return can also lead to charges.

    • Not getting an off-hire number
    • Returning equipment without a condition report

    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Rental registers, off-hire rules and supplier terms

    Keep a rental register for every item: supplier, item, on-hire date, rate, area, user, expected off-hire date and off-hire number. Read supplier terms: notice periods, minimum hire periods, collection charges, cleaning and fuel rules.

    Off-hire is usually confirmed by the supplier with a reference number. Without it, the supplier may keep charging. Collection can take days after off-hire, so check whether charges stop at off-hire or at collection.

    Register fieldWhy it matters
    Supplier and itemWho to contact
    On-hire date and rateCost to date
    Area and userWho decides when it is finished
    Expected off-hirePlan for hand-back
    Off-hire numberProof that charges stop
    Condition at returnDefence against damage claims
    Rental cost to date = Days on hire × Daily rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: A man-lift went on hire on day 2 at $180 a day. On day 26 it has been on hire for 24 days. Cost to date = 24 × 180 = $4,320.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • No rental register
    • Ignoring minimum hire periods and collection terms
    • Not knowing who decides that the item is finished

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A lighting tower at $60 a day has been on hire for 30 days. What is the cost to date?

    Show answer
    30 × 60 = $1,800.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running hand-back step by step

    Plan hand-back from the schedule. For each item, ask the area lead the day its work ends. Off-hire the same day, get the off-hire number and book collection. Before collection, clean, refuel where required, photograph and complete the condition report.

    Track the register daily. Escalate any item still on hire after its expected off-hire date.

    StepOutput
    1. Link items to work end datesOff-hire plan
    2. Confirm work is finishedArea lead approval
    3. Off-hire with supplierOff-hire number
    4. Clean, refuel and photographReady for return
    5. Condition reportSigned record
    6. Collection and register updateItem closed
    Rental saving = Days saved × Daily rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: Planned off-hire was the end of the turnaround on day 30. By off-hiring 12 lighting towers on day 22 when area work ended, 8 days were saved per tower at $60 a day. Saving = 12 × 8 × 60 = $5,760.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Waiting for the end of the turnaround to off-hire everything
    • Returning dirty or empty equipment against terms
    • No photos or condition report

    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Hand-back KPIs: idle days, on-time off-hire and damage

    Track idle days, items off-hired on time, damage charges and disputed invoices. Review the rental invoice against the register and off-hire numbers before paying.

    Idle days by area show where area leads are slow to release equipment. Damage charges by item type show where better care or checks are needed.

    • Paying rental invoices without checking off-hire numbers
    • Not tracking idle days by area
    • Ignoring repeated damage charges

    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: invoice reconciliation, damage disputes and supplier performance

    At expert level you reconcile every rental invoice line with the register: dates, rates, off-hire numbers and collection dates. Challenge charges after off-hire with the off-hire number as evidence.

    Use photos and condition reports to defend against unfair damage charges, and accept fair ones. Score suppliers on availability, condition at delivery, collection speed and invoice accuracy, and use the scores in the next turnaround.

    AreaExpert practice
    Invoice checkEvery line matched to register and off-hire number
    Charges after off-hireChallenged with evidence
    Damage disputesPhotos and condition reports
    Supplier scoringAvailability, condition, speed, invoice accuracy
    Next turnaroundPreferred suppliers and agreed terms
    Overbilling found = Invoiced amount − Correct amount from register
    🧪
    Practical Example: A supplier invoices $48,600 for cranes. The register and off-hire numbers support $45,400. Overbilling found = 48,600 − 45,400 = $3,200, which is disputed with the off-hire evidence.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying invoices before reconciliation
    • No evidence for damage disputes
    • Using the same poor supplier every time

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    An invoice is $22,500 and the register supports $21,300. How much overbilling was found?

    Show answer
    22,500 − 21,300 = $1,200.

    Testing & Quality

    How to test and prove the quality of each skill's work.

    ✅ Testing & Quality: Rapid site clearance

    Site clearance is tested by a joint walk-down and record checks. Operations and safety, not the clearance crew, sign off each area.

    CheckHow to test itPass standard
    Area conditionWalk down 10 areasNo scaffolds, waste or temporary items left
    Waste recordsCheck 20 loadsAll with records
    Temporary itemsCompare register with fieldAll removed or accounted for
    Punch itemsCheck closed itemsAll closed before hand-back
    Walk-down pass rate = Areas passing first walk-down ÷ Areas walked down × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: Operations walk down 20 areas and 17 pass first time. Three have leftover hoses and a barrier. Pass rate = 17 ÷ 20 × 100 = 85 percent, so crews now do a self-check walk-down before calling operations.

    ✅ Testing & Quality: Tool return and sign-off verification

    Tool return is tested by counting stock and comparing with records. Stores or audit, not the crib staff, do the count.

    CheckHow to test itPass standard

    Release sign-offsCheck 30 released workersAll have tool sign-off
    Safety inspectionsCheck 20 returned harnesses and monitorsAll inspected
    Missing logCheck follow-upAll missing items actioned
    Register accuracy = Items matching the register ÷ Items counted × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: Stores count 200 items and 196 match the register. Four were returned but not scanned. Accuracy = 196 ÷ 200 × 100 = 98 percent, so a second scanner is added to the crib on release days.

    ✅ Testing & Quality: Payroll settlement sign-offs

    Payroll settlement is tested by re-calculating a sample of final payslips. A second payroll person or auditor does the sample.

    CheckHow to test itPass standard

    DeductionsCheck deductions against agreementsAll lawful and agreed

    🧪
    Worked example: An auditor recalculates 40 final payslips and 39 match. One missed a $500 completion bonus. Match = 39 ÷ 40 × 100 = 97.5 percent. The bonus is paid and the bonus list is now checked against every payslip.

    ✅ Testing & Quality: Fast equipment hand-back logistics

    Equipment hand-back is tested by checking the register, off-hire numbers and invoices. Finance or procurement samples the records.

    CheckHow to test itPass standard

    🧪

    🏗️ Real-World Project: Clearing a 40-area turnaround

    Skill: Rapid site clearance

    The turnaround has 40 areas. There are 150 tonnes of scaffold at 6 hours per tonne and 360 hours of clean-up. Clearance has 6 shifts of 12 hours at 0.8 productivity. By day 26, 30 areas were planned to be handed back and 27 were. The register shows 720 temporary items, of which 712 have been removed.

    ItemData
    Areas40
    Scaffold150 tonnes at 6 hours a tonne
    Clean-up360 hours
    Shifts6 of 12 hours
    Productivity0.8
    Temporary items712 of 720 removed

    Your tasks

    1. Calculate total clearance hours.
    2. Calculate the clearance crew size.
    3. Calculate hand-back against plan.
    4. Calculate temporary item recovery.
    5. Suggest one way to protect start-up.

    Check your work

    Show worked solution
    1) Strike = 150 × 6 = 900 hours. Total = 900 + 360 = 1,260 hours. 2) Hours per worker = 12 × 6 × 0.8 = 57.6. Crew = 1,260 ÷ 57.6 = 21.9, so 22 workers. 3) Hand-back = 27 ÷ 30 × 100 = 90 percent. 4) Recovery = 712 ÷ 720 × 100 = 98.9 percent. 5) Example: clear first-start systems first, matching the operations start-up sequence.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud, safety and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Demobilization handles sensitive personal and financial data: final pay, bank details, tax data, deductions, release records and tool issue history. It also controls valuable assets and hired equipment worth millions. The end of a turnaround is a high-risk time for fraud and data loss because teams are tired and people are leaving.

    Security here means protecting personal and pay data, preventing payroll and invoice fraud, controlling tools and assets, keeping waste and environmental records and closing access. Confirm the exact legal duties with your privacy, payroll, legal and environmental teams.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Bank detail change fraudFinal pay stolenTwo-person verification
    Ghost hours in final payPayroll fraudGate and timesheet match
    Tool theftAsset lossTagged issue and daily counts
    Rental overbillingCost fraudInvoice reconciliation
    Waste records missingLegal and environmental riskRecords for every load

    🛡️ Security for: Rapid site clearance

    • Keep waste transfer and hazardous waste records filed and secure.
    • Control access to site areas during clearance.
    • Keep the temporary items register accurate and controlled.

    🛡️ Security for: Tool return and sign-off verification

    • Link tool issue and return to badges.
    • Control crib access to authorised staff.
    • Investigate patterns of loss fairly and with evidence.

    🛡️ Security for: Payroll settlement sign-offs

    • Verify any bank detail change at release with a second person.
    • Restrict access to final pay and tax data to payroll staff.
    • Keep an audit trail of every sign-off and change.

    🛡️ Security for: Fast equipment hand-back logistics

    • Send off-hire requests only through named contacts and record numbers.
    • Reconcile rental invoices before payment.
    • Keep condition photos and reports in a controlled system.

    🧪 Measuring security

    Bank change verification rate = Bank detail changes verified by a second person ÷ Bank detail changes × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: During demobilization, 46 workers asked to change bank details. 45 were verified by a second person through a known phone number before payment, and 1 was not. Verification rate = 45 ÷ 46 × 100 = 97.8 percent. The unverified change is held until it is checked.

    Security checklist

    • Bank detail changes are verified by a second person.
    • Final pay hours are matched to gate records.
    • Tools are tagged and counted daily.
    • Rental invoices are reconciled before payment.
    • Waste records are filed for every load.
    • Staff are trained on privacy and fraud each year.
