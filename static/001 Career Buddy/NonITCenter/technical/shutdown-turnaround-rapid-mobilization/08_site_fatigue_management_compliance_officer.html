    Course Overview

    Site Fatigue Management & Compliance Officer: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a Site Fatigue Management and Compliance Officer keeps shutdown workers within safe working hours and rested enough to work safely. It covers three skills: monitoring shift hour rules for 12-hour and longer shifts, checking rest-break compliance and tracking safety risks caused by fatigue.

    The role matters because shutdowns combine long shifts, night work, many days in a row and heavy, hazardous tasks. Fatigue slows reactions, weakens judgement and raises the chance of errors, injuries and process incidents. Industry guidance, such as API RP 755 for refining and petrochemical sites, and many national laws set limits on hours and rest. The officer turns these rules into daily checks that people follow.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples in the main lessons. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures and limits in this course are illustrative examples. Working time laws, industry guidance and site rules differ, so always confirm the limits that apply to your site with the client's safety, HR and legal teams.

    Who this course is for

    • Safety advisors and coordinators supporting shutdowns
    • HR and compliance staff who monitor working hours
    • Turnaround planners and supervisors who build rosters
    • Staffing agency compliance leads

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What shift hour regulation is and why it matters

    Shift hour regulation means the limits on how long people can work: hours per shift, hours per week or rolling period, number of consecutive shifts and minimum days off. Limits come from law, industry guidance, the client's fatigue policy and union agreements. On a shutdown with 12-hour shifts, workers can reach these limits fast.

    It matters because hours are the easiest fatigue risk to measure and control. Long hours and many consecutive shifts build up sleep debt. Monitoring hours every day lets you spot breaches before they happen and plan relief workers.

    TermMeaning
    Shift lengthHours from start to end of a shift
    HoldoverStaying beyond the scheduled end of shift
    Rolling periodA moving window, such as any 7 days
    Consecutive shiftsShifts worked without a day off
    ExceedanceWork beyond a limit
    ExceptionApproved, controlled exceedance
    Hours in period = Sum of shift lengths in the period
    🧪
    Practical Example: A pipefitter worked 12, 12, 13.5, 12, 12, 14 and 12 hours over 7 days. Hours = 12 + 12 + 13.5 + 12 + 12 + 14 + 12 = 87.5 hours. If the site limit is 84 hours in 7 days, the worker has exceeded it by 3.5 hours.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Counting only scheduled hours and ignoring holdovers
    • Checking weekly totals only on Mondays instead of a rolling window
    • Forgetting travel and handover time where the policy counts it

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Shifts were 12, 12.5, 12, 13, 12, 12 and 12 hours. What is the 7-day total?

    Show answer
    12 + 12.5 + 12 + 13 + 12 + 12 + 12 = 85.5 hours.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Common hour limits and how to read them

    Hour rules usually cover four things: maximum hours in one shift, maximum hours in a rolling period such as 7 days, maximum consecutive shifts, and required time off after a run of shifts. Some rules differ for normal operations and for outages. Some laws, such as the European Working Time Directive, also set average weekly limits over longer periods.

    Write the site's rules in a simple table and use exactly those numbers in your checks. Never mix rules from different sites or countries.

    Rule typeExample limit for an outage
    Hours per shiftNo more than 14 hours including holdover
    Hours in any 7 daysNo more than 84 hours
    Consecutive 12-hour shiftsNo more than 14
    Time off after a runAt least 48 hours
    Day to night switchRest before first night shift
    Hours remaining in window = Limit − Hours already worked in the window
    🧪
    Practical Example: The 7-day limit is 84 hours. A welder has worked 72 hours in the last 6 days. Hours remaining = 84 − 72 = 12, so they can work one more 12-hour shift today but no holdover.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using another site's limits
    • Treating example limits as legal limits
    • Not separating normal operation and outage rules

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The limit is 84 hours in 7 days and a fitter has worked 75. How many hours remain?

    Show answer
    84 − 75 = 9 hours.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Monitoring hours step by step

    Get hours from the source that is closest to reality, usually gate swipes or electronic timesheets, not the roster alone. Load them into a tracker or fatigue tool that calculates daily, rolling and consecutive totals for every worker.

    Each day before the shift, list workers who are near a limit and share it with supervisors. After each shift, check for breaches, record the cause and act. Report trends weekly.

    StepOutput
    1. Write the site rules tableAgreed limits
    2. Collect hours from gate or timesheetsActual hours
    3. Calculate rolling and consecutive totalsTotals per worker
    4. Flag workers near limitsPre-shift warning list
    5. Check breaches after shiftBreach log
    6. Report weeklyTrends and actions
    Near-limit flag = Hours in window + Next shift length > Limit
    🧪
    Practical Example: The limit is 84 hours in 7 days. A rigger has 73 hours and the next shift is 12 hours. 73 + 12 = 85, which is above 84, so the rigger is flagged and the supervisor plans a shorter shift or a rest day.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using the roster instead of actual hours
    • Flagging after the breach instead of before
    • Not telling supervisors who is near a limit

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    The limit is 84 hours. A worker has 70 hours and the next shift is 12. Are they flagged?

    Show answer
    70 + 12 = 82, which is not above 84, so they are not flagged.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Hours KPIs: exceedance rate, holdovers and overtime

    Track the share of worker-weeks that exceed limits, the number of holdovers, average weekly hours and the number of approved exceptions. Rising holdovers are often an early warning that crews are too small or work is behind.

    Break KPIs down by craft, crew and shift. A single crew with many breaches often points to a supervisor or planning problem.

    KPIFormulaTypical target
    Exceedance rateWorker-weeks over limit ÷ Worker-weeksZero without approved exception
    Holdover rateShifts with holdover ÷ Shifts workedUnder 5 percent
    Average weekly hoursTotal hours ÷ Worker-weeksWithin plan
    Exceptions approvedCount per weekLow and justified
    Consecutive shift breachesCount per weekZero
    Exceedance rate = Worker-weeks over the limit ÷ Worker-weeks checked × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: In week 2 there were 640 worker-weeks and 16 exceeded the 84-hour limit. Exceedance rate = 16 ÷ 640 × 100 = 2.5 percent. Twelve were welders on one critical area, so the planner adds a relief welding crew.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reporting averages that hide individual breaches
    • Ignoring holdovers because they are short
    • Not breaking data down by crew

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    800 worker-weeks and 12 exceeded the limit. What is the exceedance rate?

    Show answer
    12 ÷ 800 × 100 = 1.5 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: exceptions, critical work and system design

    At expert level you run a strict exception process. Sometimes critical work, such as an emergency repair, needs a worker beyond the limit. The exception must be approved in advance by a named manager, with a fatigue risk assessment, extra controls such as a buddy or extra breaks, and recorded in a log.

    You also design the system so exceptions are rare: enough relief workers, realistic schedules, rosters that respect limits by design, and automatic alerts from the timekeeping system.

    AreaExpert practice
    Exception approvalNamed manager approves before the work
    Risk assessmentFatigue factors and extra controls recorded
    Extra controlsBuddy checks, extra breaks, no safety-critical tasks
    Exception logEvery exception recorded and reviewed
    PreventionRelief crews and automatic alerts
    Exception rate = Approved exceptions ÷ Worker-shifts × 1,000
    🧪
    Practical Example: In a 4-week outage there were 18,000 worker-shifts and 27 approved exceptions. Exception rate = 27 ÷ 18,000 × 1,000 = 1.5 per 1,000 worker-shifts. The review finds most were on one compressor, so more relief mechanics are booked for the next outage.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Approving exceptions after the work is done
    • No extra controls for workers beyond limits
    • Treating exceptions as normal practice

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    24,000 worker-shifts and 36 exceptions. What is the exception rate per 1,000 shifts?

    Show answer
    36 ÷ 24,000 × 1,000 = 1.5.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What rest-break compliance is and why it matters

    Rest-break compliance covers two things: breaks during a shift, such as meal and rest breaks, and rest between shifts, such as a minimum number of hours off before the next start. Many laws and policies also require a weekly rest period.

    It matters because breaks restore attention during a long shift, and rest between shifts allows sleep. A worker who finishes at 06:30, travels for an hour and starts again at 17:30 has only a few hours for sleep. Rest rules protect against this.

    TermMeaning
    In-shift breakBreak during the shift for food and rest
    Daily restHours off between the end of one shift and start of the next
    Weekly restA longer rest period, such as 24 or 48 hours
    Quick returnShort gap between shifts
    Commute timeTravel time that reduces real rest
    Sleep opportunityTime available for sleep
    Rest between shifts = Next shift start time − Previous shift end time
    🧪
    Practical Example: A worker ends a shift at 19:00 and starts the next at 06:00. Rest = 11 hours. With 1 hour of travel each way and 2 hours for meals and hygiene, the sleep opportunity is about 11 − 2 − 2 = 7 hours.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Counting rest from the scheduled end instead of the actual end
    • Ignoring commute time when judging rest
    • Allowing breaks to be skipped to finish jobs

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A shift ends at 20:30 and the next starts at 07:00. How much rest is that?

    Show answer
    20:30 to 07:00 is 10.5 hours.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Break rules, minimum rest and weekly rest

    Rules often require a break after a set number of hours worked, minimum daily rest between shifts and a weekly rest period. For example, the European Working Time Directive sets a minimum of 11 consecutive hours of daily rest, a break when the working day is longer than 6 hours and a weekly rest period. Site rules for 12-hour shifts often add more breaks.

    Write the rules for your site, then check them for every worker. Breaks must be real: a worker who eats while watching a hole is not on a break.

    Rule typeExample
    In-shift breaks30 minutes after 5 hours, plus short rest breaks
    Daily restAt least 11 hours between shifts
    Weekly restAt least 24 hours in each 7 days, or 48 hours after a long run
    Day-night switchExtra rest before the first night
    Hot work and heat stressMore frequent breaks in heat
    Break compliance = Shifts with all required breaks taken ÷ Shifts checked × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Supervisors checked 400 shifts and 372 had all required breaks recorded. Compliance = 372 ÷ 400 × 100 = 93 percent. Most missing breaks were on night shift in one unit, so a relief person is added to cover watch roles during breaks.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Recording breaks that were not taken
    • Not providing cover so watch roles can take breaks
    • Using one rule set for normal and hot conditions

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    250 shifts checked and 235 had all breaks. What is break compliance?

    Show answer
    235 ÷ 250 × 100 = 94 percent.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Checking rest compliance step by step

    Use actual start and end times from gate or timesheet data. Calculate rest between every pair of shifts for every worker, check weekly rest, and compare in-shift breaks with records from supervisors or the timekeeping system.

    Flag breaches the same day. For each breach, find the cause, such as holdover, a call-in or a roster error, and fix it. Tell the worker's supervisor and record the action.

    StepOutput
    1. Write break and rest rulesAgreed rules
    2. Get actual timesStart and end per shift
    3. Calculate rest between shiftsRest per worker
    4. Check weekly restWeekly rest status
    5. Check in-shift breaksBreak compliance
    6. Flag and fix breachesBreach log with actions
    Rest breach = Minimum rest − Actual rest (when actual rest is less than minimum)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The minimum rest is 11 hours. A worker ended at 21:30 after a holdover and started again at 06:00. Actual rest = 8.5 hours. Breach = 11 − 8.5 = 2.5 hours short.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Calculating rest from the roster instead of actual times
    • Missing call-ins on days off
    • Logging breaches with no cause or action

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Minimum rest is 11 hours. A worker ended at 22:00 and started at 07:00. How short is the rest?

    Show answer
    Actual rest = 9 hours. 11 − 9 = 2 hours short.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Rest KPIs: rest breaches, call-ins and weekly rest

    Track the share of shift pairs with too little rest, the number of call-ins on rest days, the share of workers with proper weekly rest and break compliance. Call-ins on rest days are a common hidden cause of fatigue.

    Report by craft and crew. If one area keeps calling workers in, it may need a larger crew or better planning.

    KPIFormulaTypical target
    Rest breach rateShift pairs under minimum rest ÷ Shift pairs checkedZero without exception
    Call-in rateCall-ins on rest days ÷ Rest daysLow and justified
    Weekly rest complianceWorkers with weekly rest ÷ Workers checked100 percent
    Break complianceShifts with all breaks ÷ Shifts checkedOver 98 percent
    Breach close timeHours from breach found to actionUnder 24 hours
    Rest breach rate = Shift pairs under minimum rest ÷ Shift pairs checked × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The tracker checked 5,600 shift pairs in a week and found 42 under the 11-hour minimum. Rate = 42 ÷ 5,600 × 100 = 0.75 percent. 30 were caused by holdovers on one unit, so holdovers there now need approval.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Not counting call-ins as rest breaches
    • Treating break records as proof without spot checks
    • Closing breaches with no action

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    4,000 shift pairs checked and 30 breaches. What is the rest breach rate?

    Show answer
    30 ÷ 4,000 × 100 = 0.75 percent.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: designing rest into rosters and logistics

    At expert level you design rest into the plan instead of policing it afterwards. Rosters include rest days and switch days. Bus times allow workers to get home quickly. Camps are quiet for day sleepers. Meal times fit the breaks.

    You also protect break cover. Fire watches, hole watches and attendants cannot leave their post, so relief people must be rostered. Heat stress plans add extra breaks and water in hot weather.

    AreaExpert practice
    RostersRest days and switch days built in
    TransportFast buses after shifts
    CampsQuiet hours and shift-based blocks
    Watch rolesRelief cover for breaks
    Heat stressExtra breaks, water and shade
    Sleep opportunity = Rest between shifts − Commute − Meals and hygiene time
    🧪
    Practical Example: Rest between shifts is 12 hours, the commute is 45 minutes each way and meals and hygiene take 2 hours. Sleep opportunity = 12 − 1.5 − 2 = 8.5 hours.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Ignoring commute time when planning rest
    • No relief for watch roles
    • Noisy camps for day sleepers

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    Rest is 11 hours, commute 1 hour each way and meals 2 hours. What is the sleep opportunity?

    Show answer
    11 − 2 − 2 = 7 hours.

    Level 1 · Foundations · Fundamentals

    📘 Fundamentals: What fatigue risk is and how it causes incidents

    Fatigue is a state of reduced mental and physical ability caused by too little sleep, long periods awake, night work and heavy workload. It slows reactions, narrows attention, weakens memory and judgement and can cause micro-sleeps.

    Fatigue-induced incidents include slips, trips and falls, dropped objects, vehicle incidents, wrong valve operation, missed steps in procedures and permit errors. Fatigue is often a hidden cause, so investigations must ask about hours, rest and sleep.

    TermMeaning
    Sleep debtBuild-up of missed sleep over days
    Time awakeHours since the person last slept
    Circadian lowBody clock low point, usually early morning
    Micro-sleepBrief, unintended sleep of a few seconds
    Fatigue indicatorSign such as yawning, slow reactions or errors
    Fatigue-related incidentIncident where fatigue was a factor
    Fatigue-related share = Incidents with fatigue as a factor ÷ Incidents investigated × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The shutdown investigated 40 incidents and near misses. In 9, fatigue was found to be a contributing factor. Share = 9 ÷ 40 × 100 = 22.5 percent.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Not asking about hours and sleep in investigations
    • Treating fatigue as a personal weakness
    • Ignoring the early morning low point on night shift

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    50 incidents were investigated and 8 involved fatigue. What is the share?

    Show answer
    8 ÷ 50 × 100 = 16 percent.
    Level 1 · Foundations · Core Concepts

    🧭 Core Concepts: Fatigue factors and warning signs

    Fatigue risk grows with shift length, number of consecutive shifts, night work, short rest, long commutes, heavy or monotonous work and heat. It is also higher in the early morning hours on night shift.

    Train supervisors and workers to spot warning signs and to speak up without blame. Signs include long blinks, yawning, slow responses, irritability, forgetting steps and drifting attention.

    FactorWhy it raises risk
    Long shiftsMore time awake and less recovery
    Many consecutive shiftsSleep debt builds up
    Night shiftsWork against the body clock
    Short restLess sleep opportunity
    Long commuteLess rest and driving risk
    Heat and heavy workPhysical exhaustion
    Simple fatigue score = Sum of factor points
    🧪
    Practical Example: A site uses a simple score: 2 points for a shift over 12 hours, 2 for more than 10 consecutive shifts, 2 for nights, 2 for rest under 11 hours and 1 for a commute over 1 hour. A night welder on shift 12 with 13-hour shifts, 10 hours rest and a 30-minute commute scores 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 0 = 8. The site rule is to review anyone scoring 6 or more.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using a score as a replacement for judgement
    • No training for supervisors on signs
    • A blame culture that stops workers reporting fatigue

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    A worker on day shift has 12-hour shifts, 8 consecutive shifts, rest of 11.5 hours and a 90-minute commute. Using the same points, what is the score?

    Show answer
    Shift not over 12: 0. Consecutive not over 10: 0. Day shift: 0. Rest not under 11: 0. Commute over 1 hour: 1. Score = 1.
    Level 2 · Core concepts · Intermediate Concepts

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Tracking fatigue risk step by step

    Combine hours and rest data with roster data to score each worker's fatigue risk each day. Share the high-risk list with supervisors before the shift. Supervisors adjust tasks, add checks or give rest.

    Add fatigue questions to incident and near miss investigations. Record fatigue reports from workers. Review all of this weekly with the client's safety team.

    StepOutput
    1. Define fatigue factors and scoresScoring method
    2. Score workers dailyRisk list
    3. Share with supervisorsPre-shift actions
    4. Collect fatigue reportsReport log
    5. Add fatigue to investigationsFatigue findings
    6. Weekly reviewTrends and actions
    High-risk share = Workers scoring at or above the review level ÷ Workers scored × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: On day 15, 1,050 workers were scored and 84 were at or above the review level. High-risk share = 84 ÷ 1,050 × 100 = 8 percent. Most were night welders on long runs, so 20 relief welders are added.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Scoring workers but not telling supervisors
    • Taking no action on high scores
    • Not recording worker fatigue reports

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    900 workers scored and 63 at or above the review level. What is the high-risk share?

    Show answer
    63 ÷ 900 × 100 = 7 percent.
    Level 3 · Intermediate · Intermediate Concepts

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Fatigue KPIs: incident rates, reports and controls

    Use rates so that results can be compared between weeks and sites. Incident rates per 200,000 hours or per 100,000 hours are common. Compare fatigue-related incidents on day and night shifts and across hours into the shift.

    Also track leading indicators: fatigue reports, high-risk workers, controls applied and supervisor checks. Leading indicators show risk before incidents happen.

    KPIFormulaTypical use
    Incident rateIncidents × 200,000 ÷ Hours workedCompare periods and sites
    Fatigue-related shareFatigue incidents ÷ IncidentsTrend over time
    Fatigue reportsReports per weekHigher can mean better reporting
    High-risk shareHigh-risk workers ÷ Workers scoredFalling over time
    Controls appliedHigh-risk workers with controls ÷ High-risk workers100 percent
    Incident rate = Incidents × 200,000 ÷ Hours worked
    🧪
    Practical Example: A shutdown recorded 6 recordable incidents in 480,000 hours. Rate = 6 × 200,000 ÷ 480,000 = 2.5 per 200,000 hours. On night shift alone, 4 incidents in 220,000 hours gives 4 × 200,000 ÷ 220,000 = 3.6, which is higher than day shift.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Comparing raw counts instead of rates
    • Seeing more fatigue reports as bad news only
    • Not checking that high-risk workers got controls

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    5 incidents in 400,000 hours. What is the rate per 200,000 hours?

    Show answer
    5 × 200,000 ÷ 400,000 = 2.5.
    Level 4 · Expert · Advanced Concepts

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: fatigue risk management systems and just culture

    At expert level you help the client run a fatigue risk management system: policy, hour and rest limits, risk scoring, training, reporting, investigation, controls and regular review. Industry guidance such as API RP 755 describes such systems for refining and petrochemical sites.

    A just culture is vital. Workers must be able to say they are too tired to work safely without being punished. The system must also be fair: the same rules for agency and client staff, and no pressure to hide hours.

    AreaExpert practice
    PolicyWritten limits, roles and exceptions
    TrainingWorkers and supervisors trained on fatigue
    ReportingEasy, no-blame fatigue reporting
    ControlsTask changes, breaks, rest, buddy checks
    ReviewWeekly during outages, after each outage
    Control coverage = High-risk workers with recorded controls ÷ High-risk workers × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: In week 3, 120 workers were high-risk and 114 had recorded controls such as task changes or extra breaks. Coverage = 114 ÷ 120 × 100 = 95 percent. Six had no record, so supervisors on those crews are coached.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Punishing workers who report fatigue
    • Different rules for agency and client staff
    • A policy that nobody is trained on

    ✍️ Hands-on Practice

    80 high-risk workers and 78 had controls. What is the control coverage?

    Show answer
    78 ÷ 80 × 100 = 97.5 percent.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: Shift hour regulation monitoring

    The site rules table, gate swipe or timesheet exports, a fatigue tracking tool or spreadsheet, a pre-shift warning list and an exception log. The routine is to calculate totals daily, warn before breaches and log every exception.

    🏗️ Real-World Project: Checking a welding crew's hours

    Skill: Shift hour regulation monitoring

    The site limits are 14 hours per shift, 84 hours in any 7 days and 14 consecutive 12-hour shifts. A welder's last 7 shifts were 12, 12, 13, 12, 14.5, 12 and 12 hours. Another welder has 74 hours in the last 6 days and the next shift is 12 hours. In week 3, 30 of 900 worker-weeks exceeded the limit. There were 21,000 worker-shifts and 42 approved exceptions.

    ItemData
    Limits14 hours per shift, 84 in 7 days, 14 consecutive
    Welder A shifts12, 12, 13, 12, 14.5, 12, 12
    Welder B74 hours, next shift 12
    Exceedances30 of 900 worker-weeks
    Worker-shifts21,000
    Exceptions42

    Your tasks

    1. Calculate welder A's 7-day total and any breaches.
    2. Decide whether welder B is flagged.
    3. Calculate the exceedance rate.
    4. Calculate the exception rate per 1,000 worker-shifts.
    5. Suggest one prevention step.

    Check your work

    Show worked solution
    1) Total = 12 + 12 + 13 + 12 + 14.5 + 12 + 12 = 87.5 hours, which is 3.5 hours over 84. The 14.5-hour shift also breaks the 14-hour shift limit. 2) 74 + 12 = 86, above 84, so welder B is flagged. 3) Exceedance rate = 30 ÷ 900 × 100 = 3.3 percent. 4) Exception rate = 42 ÷ 21,000 × 1,000 = 2 per 1,000 worker-shifts. 5) Example: add relief welders on the critical area and make holdovers over 1 hour need supervisor approval.

    🏗️ Real-World Project: Auditing rest between shifts

    Skill: Rest-break compliance

    The minimum daily rest is 11 hours. Worker A ended at 19:30 and started at 06:00. Worker B ended at 21:00 after a holdover and started at 06:00. The commute is 1 hour each way and meals and hygiene take 2 hours. In a week, 6,000 shift pairs were checked and 48 were under the minimum. Supervisors recorded all breaks on 380 of 400 shifts.

    ItemData
    Minimum rest11 hours
    Worker A19:30 end, 06:00 start
    Worker B21:00 end, 06:00 start
    Commute and meals2 hours and 2 hours
    Shift pairs48 of 6,000 under minimum
    Breaks380 of 400 shifts

    Your tasks

    1. Calculate rest and sleep opportunity for worker A.
    2. Calculate rest and breach for worker B.
    3. Calculate the rest breach rate.
    4. Calculate break compliance.
    5. Suggest one fix for holdover breaches.

    Check your work

    Show worked solution
    1) Rest = 10.5 hours, which is a 0.5-hour breach. Sleep opportunity = 10.5 − 2 − 2 = 6.5 hours. 2) Rest = 9 hours. Breach = 11 − 9 = 2 hours. 3) Rate = 48 ÷ 6,000 × 100 = 0.8 percent. 4) Compliance = 380 ÷ 400 × 100 = 95 percent. 5) Example: when a worker holds over, the system automatically delays their next start to give the minimum rest, and the supervisor plans cover.

    🏗️ Real-World Project: Tracking fatigue risk on a night-heavy outage

    Skill: Fatigue-induced safety risk tracking

    The outage worked 600,000 hours with 9 recordable incidents. Night shift worked 280,000 hours with 6 of them. 12 of 45 investigated events had fatigue as a factor. On day 18, 1,100 workers were scored and 99 were high-risk. 93 of the 99 had recorded controls.

    ItemData
    Total hours600,000
    Total incidents9
    Night hours and incidents280,000 and 6
    Fatigue factor12 of 45 events
    High-risk workers99 of 1,100
    Controls recorded93 of 99

    Your tasks

    1. Calculate the overall incident rate per 200,000 hours.
    2. Calculate the night and day incident rates.
    3. Calculate the fatigue-related share.
    4. Calculate the high-risk share and control coverage.
    5. Suggest one action from the data.

    Check your work

    Show worked solution
    1) Overall = 9 × 200,000 ÷ 600,000 = 3.0. 2) Night = 6 × 200,000 ÷ 280,000 = 4.3. Day hours = 320,000 with 3 incidents, so day = 3 × 200,000 ÷ 320,000 = 1.9. 3) Share = 12 ÷ 45 × 100 = 26.7 percent. 4) High-risk = 99 ÷ 1,100 × 100 = 9 percent. Coverage = 93 ÷ 99 × 100 = 93.9 percent. 5) Example: night rates are more than twice day rates, so add relief crews to cut consecutive nights and schedule safety-critical tasks away from the early morning low point.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud, safety and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Fatigue management handles sensitive personal data: hours, gate times, rest patterns, fatigue scores, fatigue reports, incident details and sometimes health information. Misuse can harm workers, for example if fatigue reports are used to punish them. Edited timesheets can hide breaches.

    Security here means protecting personal and health data, keeping hour and rest records accurate and locked, preventing pressure to falsify records and using data fairly. Confirm the exact legal duties with your privacy, HR and legal teams.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Edited timesheetsHidden breachesLocked records with audit trail
    Fatigue reports used to punishUnder-reporting and harmJust culture policy and access limits
    Health data shared widelyPrivacy breachNeed-to-know access
    Pressure to work beyond limitsSafety riskException approval and whistleblowing channel
    Data used unfairlyDiscrimination riskSame rules for all groups

    🛡️ Security for: Shift hour regulation monitoring

    • Lock hour records after approval and keep an audit trail of changes.
    • Give only fatigue, payroll and supervisors the access they need.
    • Provide a confidential channel to report pressure to break hour limits.

    🛡️ Security for: Rest-break compliance

    • Keep break and rest records accurate and do not allow back-filling.
    • Store rest breach logs securely with causes and actions.
    • Share individual rest data only with the worker and supervisors who need it.

    🛡️ Security for: Fatigue-induced safety risk tracking

    • Keep fatigue reports confidential and use them for safety, not discipline.
    • Store any health information separately with strict access.
    • Use anonymised data for trend reports to the wider site.

    🧪 Measuring security

    Record change control = Hour record changes with an approved reason ÷ Hour record changes × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: In the outage, 260 hour records were changed after first entry. 252 had an approved reason in the audit trail and 8 did not. Change control = 252 ÷ 260 × 100 = 96.9 percent. The 8 changes are investigated and editing rights are reduced.

    Security checklist

    • Hour records are locked after approval with an audit trail.
    • Fatigue reports are confidential and not used for discipline.
    • Health data is stored separately with strict access.
    • Trend reports use anonymised data.
    • Workers can report pressure to break limits confidentially.
    • Staff are trained on privacy and fairness each year.

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Shift hour regulation monitoring

    Why do shutdowns carry high fatigue risk?

    Show model answer
    Long shifts, many consecutive days, night work, short rest and heavy, hazardous tasks all combine, and they build sleep debt quickly.

    Why use a rolling 7-day window instead of a calendar week?

    Show model answer
    A rolling window catches heavy runs that cross week boundaries, which a calendar week can hide.

    A supervisor wants a welder to stay 4 extra hours for a critical repair. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Check the worker's hours and rest, and if it would exceed a limit, follow the exception process: named approval in advance, a fatigue risk assessment and extra controls, or find a relief welder.

    Why use gate data rather than the roster?

    Show model answer
    The roster shows the plan. Gate data shows actual hours, including holdovers and call-ins.

    The limit is 84 hours and a worker has 76 with a 12-hour shift next. Are they flagged?

    Show model answer
    76 + 12 = 88, above 84, so yes.

    💬 Interview Questions: Rest-break compliance

    What is the difference between in-shift breaks and daily rest?

    Show model answer
    In-shift breaks are rests during the shift. Daily rest is the time off between the end of one shift and the start of the next.

    Why count commute time when judging rest?

    Show model answer
    Travel reduces the real time available for sleep. Legal rest can still leave too little sleep if the commute is long.

    Breaks are recorded but crews say they cannot take them. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Observe in the field, add relief cover for watch roles, and make sure records reflect real breaks.

    How do holdovers cause rest breaches?

    Show model answer
    A late finish shortens the gap before the next start unless the next start is moved, so holdovers need rest adjustment.

    A shift ends at 20:00 and the next starts at 06:30. How much rest?

    Show model answer
    10.5 hours.

    💬 Interview Questions: Fatigue-induced safety risk tracking

    What are common fatigue warning signs?

    Show model answer
    Yawning, long blinks, slow responses, irritability, forgetting steps, drifting attention and micro-sleeps.

    Why ask fatigue questions in every investigation?

    Show model answer
    Fatigue is often a hidden cause. Without questions on hours, rest and time awake, it is missed and not fixed.

    Fatigue reports have dropped to almost zero. Is that good?

    Show model answer
    Not necessarily. It may mean workers fear blame. I would check the reporting culture and talk to crews.

    How do you compare incidents between day and night shifts?

    Show model answer
    Use rates per hours worked, such as per 200,000 hours, because the shifts may have different hours.

    4 incidents in 320,000 hours. What is the rate per 200,000 hours?

    Show model answer
    4 × 200,000 ÷ 320,000 = 2.5.
