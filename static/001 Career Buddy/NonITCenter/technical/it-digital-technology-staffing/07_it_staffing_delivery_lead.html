    The Role & Delivery Basics

    Before you enforce an SLA or review a recruiter dashboard, understand what an IT Staffing Delivery Lead owns, how requirements travel through your team, and the words every delivery conversation uses.

    🧭 What does a delivery lead do?

    An IT Staffing Delivery Lead makes sure the promises your firm made to clients are actually kept. Sales wins the requirement; you and your team deliver the candidates, on time and at quality. You run the engine room of recruiting: the people, the process and the numbers.

    📜
    Enforce SLAs
    Make sure client service-level promises are met, and act early when they are at risk.
    ⏱️
    Track turnaround
    Measure how fast reqs are acknowledged, submitted, interviewed and filled.
    🎯
    Optimise ratios
    Improve how many submittals it takes to produce one hire.
    📊
    Track recruiters
    Measure and coach recruiter performance fairly.
    Example: a day in the role
    At 9 a.m. you check the SLA dashboard and see two P1 reqs about to breach. By 9:30 you have reassigned one to a recruiter with a ready pipeline. At 2 p.m. you coach a recruiter whose interview rate is low. At 4 p.m. you send the client a short status report with two risks flagged early.

    ⚖️ Delivery vs sales

    AreaSales or account managementDelivery
    FocusWinning clients and reqsFilling reqs well and on time
    Key question"What can we sell?""What can we deliver, and when?"
    Success looks likeNew business, rate, relationshipsFill rate, speed, quality, retention of clients
    Risk if weakFew reqsBroken promises and lost clients
    Example: when the two meet
    Sales promises a client first submittals within 4 business hours. You check capacity before agreeing: the team can do 8 hours for hard skills and 4 for common ones. You tell sales so the promise reflects reality, instead of discovering the gap after the client is unhappy.

    🔗 The delivery chain

    Every req flows through the same stages. Each stage has a number you can measure and improve.

    Monday 9:00: req arrives. 9:40: acknowledged. Tuesday 11:00: two candidates submitted. Thursday: both interviewed. Friday: offer to one. Next Wednesday: starts. Every step has a timestamp, which is how you later find where time was lost.

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Client side

    • Hiring managers: give feedback and make decisions.
    • Vendor or procurement managers: own the SLAs and scorecards.
    • MSP or VMS administrators: manage reqs and reports.

    🧑‍💼 Your side

    • Recruiters and sourcers: do the delivery work.
    • Account managers and directors: own the client relationship.
    • Finance and operations: margins, payroll, tools.
    Example: sharing bad news early
    A hiring manager has not given interview feedback for five days, and your SLA clock is still running. You tell the account manager the same day so they can raise it with the client. Early, factual updates protect the relationship; late surprises damage it.

    🗓️ Your weekly rhythm

    WhenActivity

    WeeklyReq aging review, recruiter 1:1s, pipeline review, client status report

    Delivery Operations Fundamentals

    The building blocks of running a delivery team: the funnel, how reqs age, how to prioritise, how much work a recruiter can handle, and the data you need to see clearly.

    🔻 The delivery funnel

    Think of a funnel: many candidates enter at the top and a few become hires. Each stage has a conversion rate, the share that moves to the next stage.

    StageCountConversion to next stage
    Submittals4050% become interviews
    Interviews2040% become offers
    Offers862.5% accepted
    Starts (hires)5-
    Example: reading a funnel
    From 40 submittals you get 5 hires, so the submittal-to-hire ratio is 5 ÷ 40 = 12.5%, or about 8 submittals per hire. If you want more hires, you can send more submittals or improve one of the conversions. Improving quality is usually cheaper than sending more.

    ⏳ Req lifecycle and aging

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Age bucketMeaningTypical action
    0 to 7 daysFreshNormal work
    8 to 14 daysMaturingCheck progress and feedback
    15 to 30 daysAgingReview strategy and rate
    30+ daysStaleDecide: re-scope, escalate, hold or close
    Example: acting on aging
    A req reaches day 16 with no interviews. You review it with the recruiter and find the rate is 20% below market. You tell the account manager, the client raises the rate, and two candidates are interviewed within a week. Aging reports exist to trigger exactly this conversation.

    🚦 Prioritising reqs

    PriorityMeaningExample SLA (illustrative)
    P1 (hot)Urgent, start date close, high valueFirst submittal within 8 business hours
    P2 (standard)Normal timelineWithin 24 business hours
    P3 (low)Flexible or hard-to-fill nicheWithin 48 business hours
    💡
    Prioritise using urgency, value (margin or strategic client) and fill difficulty. Tell the team why a req is P1, so they trust the system.
    Example: a priority call
    Two reqs arrive together. Req A starts in 5 days for a top-3 client. Req B starts in 6 weeks for a small client. You mark A as P1 and B as P2, move your best-pipelined recruiter to A, and explain the reasoning in the stand-up.

    🧮 Capacity basics

    Capacity is how much work a team can handle well. If recruiters carry too many reqs, speed and quality both fall.

    ItemExample (illustrative)
    Open reqs60
    Reqs one recruiter can handle well8
    Recruiters needed60 ÷ 8 = 7.5, so 8
    Recruiters available6, giving capacity for 48 reqs
    Gap60 − 48 = 12 reqs over capacity
    Example: acting on a capacity gap
    You are 12 reqs over capacity. Options: hire two recruiters, move 6 easy reqs to a sourcing partner, or negotiate longer SLAs on the lowest-priority reqs. You show the three options to your manager with the cost and risk of each, rather than just asking the team to work harder.

    🏗️ Team structure

    RoleDoes
    RecruiterOwns reqs, screens, submits, manages candidates
    SourcerFinds and engages candidates to feed the recruiters
    Recruiting coordinatorScheduling, paperwork, onboarding steps
    Delivery lead (you)Runs the process, numbers, SLAs and coaching
    Account managerOwns the client relationship
    Example: a pod model
    A pod of 4 recruiters, 2 sourcers and 1 coordinator serves three clients. Because sourcers keep pipelines full and the coordinator handles scheduling, recruiters spend more time on screening and submittals, and first-submittal TAT drops from 20 to 11 hours.

    🧰 Tools and data

    ToolUsed for
    ATSCandidate records and stages
    CRMClient and req information
    VMS or client portalReq intake, submittals, client reporting
    BI dashboardsTrends, SLA status, recruiter performance
    SpreadsheetsQuick analysis (but avoid as the master record)
    ⚠️
    Data quality decides everything. If recruiters forget to log a submittal or leave old statuses open, every metric is wrong. Teach and check data habits first.
    Example: a data-quality catch
    Your dashboard shows a recruiter with a 0% interview rate. You check and find she logs submittals in the client portal but not in the ATS. After fixing the habit, her true interview rate is 55%. Without checking the data, you would have coached the wrong problem.

    🟢 RAG status

    StatusMeaningAction
    GreenOn track within SLAKeep going
    AmberAt risk of breachAct now: reassign, help, inform the client
    RedBreached or certain to breachEscalate, recover, record the cause
    Example: amber saves a req
    At 80% of the SLA clock a P1 req turns amber. You see no submittals have gone out, call the recruiter and find a sourcing gap. You add a sourcer, two candidates go out before the clock expires, and the req never turns red.

    🪤 Common beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "More submittals always means better delivery."
    • "Only the recruiter's numbers matter."
    • "The dashboard is always right."

    ✅ Reality

    • Quality and ratios matter as much as volume.
    • Client behaviour, such as slow feedback, also affects results.
    • Dashboards depend on accurate data entry.
    Example: volume without quality
    A recruiter sends 30 submittals in a week and gets 3 interviews (10%). Another sends 12 and gets 7 (58%). The second is far more effective, and wastes less client time. Counting submittals alone would have rewarded the wrong behaviour.

    Module 03 · SLA Enforcement

    SLA Enforcement

    An SLA is a promise with a clock attached. Learn what SLAs measure, how the clock runs, how to see trouble coming, and how to handle a breach professionally.

    📜 What an SLA is

    A Service Level Agreement (SLA) is a written commitment about the level of service a client will receive, with measurable targets. In staffing, SLAs usually cover speed, quality and fill performance, and are often reviewed on a client scorecard.

    📑 Client SLA

    Written in the contract. Missing it can affect scorecards, volume, or, in some contracts, fees.

    🏠 Internal SLA

    Your own stricter targets, set so you stay safely inside the client SLA.

    Example: a buffer that protects you
    The client SLA says first submittal within 24 business hours. Your internal target is 16. When a req reaches 16 hours without a submittal, you act, with a full 8 hours of buffer before any breach happens.

    📏 Common SLA metrics

    MetricWhat it measuresExample target (illustrative)
    Time to acknowledgeReq received to recruiter confirmationWithin 2 business hours
    First submittal TATReq received to first candidate sent24 business hours
    Submittals per reqEnough choice for the clientAt least 3 qualified candidates
    Submittal-to-interview ratioQuality of what you sendAt least 40%
    Fill rateReqs filled ÷ reqs receivedAt least 50%
    Offer acceptanceAccepted offers ÷ offers madeAt least 70%
    Example: SLA compliance in numbers
    50 reqs arrived this month and 45 received a first submittal inside the SLA. Compliance = 45 ÷ 50 = 90%. If the contract requires 95%, you are 5 points short. The five late reqs are where you investigate the causes.

    🚦 SLA tiers by priority

    PriorityAcknowledgeFirst submittalWeekly check-in
    P11 business hour8 business hoursDaily
    P22 business hours24 business hoursTwice a week
    P34 business hours48 business hoursWeekly
    Example: tiers focus effort
    A team gets 10 new reqs on Monday: 2 are P1, 5 are P2 and 3 are P3. By lunchtime the P1s are assigned to the strongest recruiters, and the P3 reqs wait until the afternoon. Tiers stop urgent work from being buried under routine work.

    🕰️ Clock rules

    RuleWhy it matters
    Business hours vs calendar hoursWeekends and nights can be excluded from the clock
    Clock startThe moment the complete req is received, not a draft
    Clock stopFirst qualified submittal, as defined in the contract
    Pause conditionsClient has put the req on hold or is slow to clarify
    Time zonesUse the contract's agreed time zone
    Example: counting business hours
    With business hours 9 to 17, a req arrives Monday at 15:00 and the first submittal goes out Tuesday at 11:00. Monday counts 2 hours (15 to 17) and Tuesday counts 2 hours (9 to 11), so the SLA time is 4 business hours, though 20 calendar hours passed.

    🔔 Early warning system

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    TriggerWho actsAction
    50% of the clock used, no candidate readyRecruiterConfirm the plan; ask for help if needed
    80% used (amber)Delivery leadReassign, add a sourcer, or free up time
    SLA breached (red)Delivery lead and account managerInform the client, recover, record the cause
    Example: the 80% rule at work
    A P2 req has a 24-hour SLA. At hour 19 (about 80%) the dashboard turns amber with no submittal. You call the recruiter, learn the candidate she planned to send has withdrawn, and a sourcer finds two replacements by hour 22. The SLA is met by two hours.

    💥 Handling a breach

    1. Tell the client early, with facts and a recovery time.
    2. Fix the req: reassign, add resource, widen the search.
    3. Record the cause honestly: capacity, sourcing, client delay, unclear req.
    4. Fix the system, not just the one req.
    5. Follow up with the client after recovery.
    breach message exampleSubject: Update on Req 2041 (Java Developer): first submittal delayed

Hi Dana,

We missed our 24-hour target for Req 2041 by about 6 hours. The cause was a
gap in Java sourcing on our side. We now have two vetted candidates and
will send both by 3 p.m. today. We have added a sourcer to your reqs this
week to prevent a repeat. I will confirm once they are in your portal.
    Example: why honesty works
    The client replies, "Thanks for telling us before we had to ask. Please send them over." A short, factual, early message turned a breach into a trust-building moment. A client who finds out on their own tends to treat the same miss as a pattern.

    🤝 Shared accountability

    Some delays come from the client side. Good SLAs include client commitments as well.

    Client commitmentTypical target
    Clear, complete req detailsAt intake
    Interview schedulingWithin 3 business days of a submittal
    Interview feedbackWithin 2 business days of an interview
    Decision on offersWithin 3 business days
    Example: documenting a client delay
    A hiring manager takes 8 days to give feedback against a 2-day commitment, and two candidates take other jobs. You log the dates in your report, show them at the monthly review, and propose a feedback reminder. The data makes a hard conversation about client speed fair and factual.

    🪜 Escalation matrix

    LevelTriggerWhoWithin
    1Amber on a reqDelivery leadSame day
    2Breach on a P1Account manager and directorWithin 2 hours
    3Repeated breaches or complaintHead of delivery and client sponsorWithin 1 business day
    Example: following the matrix
    A P1 req breaches on Friday at 4 p.m. You follow Level 2: you call the account manager within the hour, agree the client message, and email by 5. The director is informed before the weekend, so nobody is surprised on Monday.

    📝 Renegotiating SLAs

    If a target is unrealistic for certain skills, use data to propose a better SLA, rather than silently missing it.

    EvidenceProposal
    Niche Go roles take a median of 40 business hours to first submittalA separate SLA for niche skills at 48 hours
    Client feedback averages 6 daysPause the SLA clock until feedback arrives
    High-volume common skills are always met in 12 hoursA tighter SLA for those reqs, in exchange for volume
    Example: data wins the discussion
    You show six months of data: common skills meet 12 hours, niche skills need 40. The client agrees to a two-tier SLA. Your compliance moves from 82% to 96% with no change in effort, and the client gets realistic promises.

    Turnaround Time (TAT) Tracking

    Speed wins reqs, and you cannot improve what you do not measure. Learn the family of TAT measures, how to count them correctly, and how to find and fix the slow step.

    ⏱️ The TAT family

    MeasureFromTo
    Time to acknowledgeReq receivedRecruiter confirms
    First submittal TATReq receivedFirst candidate sent
    Time to interviewFirst submittalFirst client interview
    Time to offerFirst submittalOffer made
    Time to fill (or start)Req receivedCandidate starts
    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: one req, five timestamps
    Req received Monday 9:00. Acknowledged 9:40 (40 minutes). First submittal Tuesday 11:00. First interview Thursday. Offer Friday. Start the following Wednesday. Each timestamp lets you measure one segment, and see exactly where time was lost.

    🕰️ Starting and stopping the clock

    QuestionGood rule
    When does the clock start?When the complete req reaches your team, with a recorded timestamp
    When does it stop for first submittal?When the first qualified candidate is submitted to the client
    Business or calendar hours?Follow the contract; measure both internally
    When can it pause?Only for agreed reasons, such as client hold or missing information, logged with dates
    Example: a pause done right
    A req arrives on Monday but misses the rate and location. You email the client for details at 10:00; the answer comes Tuesday 14:00. The clock is paused for that period, with both timestamps logged. Without a record, the pause looks like an excuse.

    📊 Average, median and percentiles

    An average can be pulled up by a few very slow reqs. The median is the middle value and shows the typical req. A percentile such as P90 shows how slow the slowest 10% are.

    ReqFirst submittal TAT (hours)
    A4
    B6
    C6
    D8
    E10
    F12
    G48

    Average = (4+6+6+8+10+12+48) ÷ 7 = 94 ÷ 7 ≈ 13.4 hours. Median (middle value) = 8 hours.

    Example: which number tells the truth?
    The average of 13.4 hours looks poor, but six of seven reqs were under 12 hours. The median of 8 hours shows the typical performance, and req G (48 hours) is the outlier to investigate. Report both, and look at the outlier separately.

    🗂️ Aging buckets

    BucketOpen reqsShare
    0 to 7 days2440%
    8 to 14 days1830%
    15 to 30 days1220%
    30+ days610%
    Total60100%
    Example: reading the buckets
    60 open reqs, and 6 (10%) are over 30 days old. You review those six this week: two are re-scoped with the client, one is put on hold, one is closed, and two get a new recruiter. Aging reports turn vague worry into a short action list.

    🔎 Finding bottlenecks

    SegmentMedian nowTargetVerdict
    Acknowledge1.0 hours2.0OK
    Req to first submittal20 hours16Slow
    Submittal to interview3 days3 daysOK
    Interview to offer9 days5 daysBottleneck
    Example: following the slow step
    The biggest gap is interview-to-offer (9 days against 5). That is mostly client decision time and your offer preparation. You build a pre-approved offer template and agree a 2-day decision window with the top clients, and the median falls to 6 days.

    🧩 Segmenting your TAT

    One overall number hides important differences. Split TAT by:

    Segment byWhy
    PriorityP1 should be faster than P3
    Skill typeNiche skills naturally take longer
    ClientSome clients provide clearer reqs or faster feedback
    Recruiter or podSpot coaching needs and good practices
    Example: a hidden problem
    Overall median first-submittal TAT is 12 hours, which looks fine. Split by skill, common roles take 6 hours and niche roles take 34 hours. The average hides a real weakness in niche sourcing that you can now address with a specialist sourcer.

    🚀 Improving TAT

    LeverHow it helps
    Talent pipelines and benchCandidates are ready before the req arrives
    Sourcing sprintsShort focused bursts for hard reqs
    Templates and standard profilesFaster, more consistent submittals
    Better intakeFewer clarification delays
    Clear ownership and prioritiesNo req waits in a queue unseen
    AutomationAlerts, auto-assignment, scheduling links
    ⚠️
    Speed must not destroy quality. If TAT improves but the submittal-to-interview ratio falls, you are sending weaker candidates faster. Track both together.
    Example: balanced improvement
    After building a pipeline of pre-vetted Java candidates, median first-submittal TAT falls from 20 to 9 hours, and the submittal-to-interview ratio rises from 45% to 52%. Both speed and quality improved because the candidates were ready and already screened.

    Every morning you open the live SLA board and sort by time left. You see three ambers, call two recruiters and reassign one req, all within 10 minutes. A good dashboard is not a report to admire; it is a trigger to act.

    Module 05 · Submittal-to-Hire Ratio Optimization

    Submittal-to-Hire Ratio Optimization

    The most powerful way to improve delivery is to waste fewer submittals. Learn the four funnel ratios, how to find the stage that leaks, and the fixes that move each one.

    🎯 Why ratios matter

    Each submittal costs recruiter time, client attention and candidate goodwill. A better ratio means more hires from the same effort, a happier client and a lower cost per hire.

    Example: same effort, more hires
    Team A sends 100 submittals and makes 8 hires (12.5 per hire). Team B sends 100 submittals and makes 14 hires (about 7 per hire). Team B is not working harder; it is working more precisely, and clients prefer it because fewer of their hours are wasted.

    🔢 The four ratios

    RatioFormulaWhat it tells you
    Submittal-to-interviewInterviews ÷ submittalsQuality of screening and fit
    Interview-to-offerOffers ÷ interviewsHow well candidates perform and match
    Offer acceptanceStarts ÷ offersPay, speed and candidate care
    Submittal-to-hireStarts ÷ submittalsOverall efficiency (the product of the three above)
    Read left to right.
    Example: calculating all four
    40 submittals, 20 interviews, 8 offers, 5 starts. Submittal-to-interview 20 ÷ 40 = 50%. Interview-to-offer 8 ÷ 20 = 40%. Offer acceptance 5 ÷ 8 = 62.5%. Submittal-to-hire 5 ÷ 40 = 12.5%, which is 0.50 × 0.40 × 0.625.

    🔻 Reading a funnel

    TeamSub→IntInt→OfferOffer→StartSub→Hire
    Team A50%40%62.5%12.5%
    Team B30%50%80%12.0%
    Team C60%40%62.5%15.0%
    Example: two teams, same total, different problems
    Team A and Team B have almost the same overall ratio, but different leaks. A loses people at acceptance (62.5%). B loses them at the submittal stage (30%). They need different fixes: A needs better offer handling, B needs better screening and intake.

    🩺 Diagnosing leaks

    Low ratioLikely causesFirst checks
    Submittal-to-interviewWeak screening, unclear req, wrong rate or locationRead rejected submittals and client reasons
    Interview-to-offerPoor interview preparation, mismatch of levelReview interview feedback themes
    Offer acceptanceLow pay, slow process, competing offers, weak candidate careAsk decliners why
    Example: reasons beat guesses
    You tag every rejection with a reason for one month: 40% "missing must-have skill", 25% "rate too high", 20% "location", 15% "other". The biggest fix is clearer intake and a must-have checklist before submitting, not more sourcing.

    🎯 Improving submittal quality

    1. Sharper intake: confirm must-haves, nice-to-haves, rate and location before searching.
    2. Must-have checklist: tick every must-have before a profile is sent.
    3. Pre-submittal call: confirm interest, rate, availability and authorization.
    4. Fewer, better submittals: send 3 strong candidates, not 8 uncertain ones.
    5. Calibrate with the client: share two sample profiles early and ask for feedback.
    Example: a checklist that lifted the ratio
    A team adds a five-point must-have checklist and a pre-submittal call. Within six weeks, submittal-to-interview rises from 38% to 55%, and the average submittals per req falls from 6 to 4, a saving of hours for both recruiters and clients.

    🎙️ Improving interviews to offers

    • Prepare candidates: share the interviewer, topics and company context.
    • Collect feedback fast: ask the client within 24 hours of each interview.
    • Learn from rejections: tag reasons and feed them back into screening.
    • Match level properly: do not submit seniors for mid-level reqs or the reverse.
    Example: preparation pays
    Recruiters start a 15-minute prep call before every interview: topics, interviewer style, the client's pain points. Interview-to-offer rises from 33% to 45% in two months. Candidates were already good; they were now showing it better.

    🤝 Improving acceptance

    Cause of declineFix
    Rate lower than expectedConfirm rate at the start; align before submitting
    Slow process; candidate took another offerShorten decision time; keep the candidate warm
    Competing offerAsk early about other processes; present value beyond rate
    Poor communicationWeekly update calls; one named contact
    Example: stopping a late decline
    A candidate was silent for three days after an offer. The recruiter called and learned she had a competing offer at a higher rate. Because the recruiter asked early about other processes in the first call, the client had time to improve the rate, and she accepted.

    ⚖️ Quality vs quantity

    🔴 Submittal dumping

    Sending many weak profiles to look busy. Low ratio, tired client, lower trust.

    🟢 Right-sized submittals

    Sending enough strong candidates for choice: usually 3 to 4 well-matched profiles per req.

    💡
    Set a minimum quality bar (must-have checklist passed) and a sensible maximum per req. Both protect the ratio and the client's time.
    Example: fixing a volume habit
    A recruiter submits 9 profiles per req, with 2 interviews. You coach her to send her best 4 with a short fit note. Her interviews per req rise to 2.2 from 4 submittals, and the client praises her profiles by name.

    💰 Cost per hire

    Item (illustrative)Value
    Recruiter hours per submittal (screening, calls, write-up)1.5 hours
    Submittals per hire8
    Hours per hire8 × 1.5 = 12 hours
    If ratio improves to 6 submittals per hire6 × 1.5 = 9 hours

    Example: what 25% means
    If the team makes 100 hires a year, saving 3 hours each frees 300 recruiter hours, enough time for about 25 extra hires at the old rate. Ratio improvement is the cheapest source of extra capacity.

    "We improved submittal-to-interview from 50% to 60% by adding a must-have checklist and pre-submittal calls. At 40 submittals a month, that adds about one hire a month, or 12 a year, with no extra headcount."

    Module 06 · Recruiter Performance Tracking

    Recruiter Performance Tracking

    Numbers should help people improve, not scare them. Learn to build a balanced scorecard, compare recruiters fairly, coach with evidence and stop metrics from being gamed.

    📊 What good tracking looks like

    🔴 Poor tracking

    • One number (submittals) decides everything
    • Compares recruiters with very different reqs
    • Used only to criticise
    • Data is entered inconsistently

    🟢 Good tracking

    • Several balanced measures
    • Adjusted for difficulty and mix
    • Used to coach and recognise
    • Data is reliable and visible to the recruiter
    Example: one number goes wrong
    A team is rewarded only for submittals per day. Recruiters send quickly and carelessly, interview rates fall from 50% to 28% and clients complain. When the scorecard adds quality and outcomes, behaviour changes within a month.

    ⚖️ The balanced scorecard

    DimensionExample measuresQuestion it answers
    SpeedTime to acknowledge, first-submittal TATDo they respond quickly?
    QualitySubmittal-to-interview, interview-to-offerAre their candidates good fits?
    OutcomesStarts, fill rate, marginDo reqs actually get filled?
    Process hygieneData accuracy, timely status updates, compliance stepsCan we trust their records?
    Candidate and client careFeedback, complaints, extension rateDo people want to work with them?
    Example: a four-part scorecard
    Weights: speed 25%, quality 30%, outcomes 30% and hygiene 15%. A recruiter strong on speed but weak on quality cannot score well overall, which tells her where to focus. The weights express what the business values.

    🔮 Activity vs outcome

    TypeExamplesUse
    Leading indicators (activity)Screening calls, submittals, interviews scheduledEarly warning; coaching topics
    Lagging indicators (outcomes)Starts, fill rate, revenue, marginFinal results; rewards
    💡
    Activity numbers tell you what to coach; outcome numbers tell you whether it worked. Using only activity rewards busyness; using only outcomes hides the cause of problems.
    Example: both views together
    A recruiter makes 25 screening calls a day (strong activity) but only 1 start a month (weak outcome). Reviewing her calls reveals she screens for the wrong skills. Fixing her intake lifts outcomes without any extra calls.

    🎚️ Fair benchmarking

    Not all reqs are equal. A niche Go role can take ten times the effort of a common support role. Compare recruiters like for like.

    💬 Coaching conversations

    1. Start with strengths backed by data.
    2. Pick one or two focus areas, not ten.
    3. Look at real examples together: rejected submittals, call recordings, notes.
    4. Agree an action with a date and a measure.
    5. Follow up at the next 1:1.
    1:1 script"Your first-submittal time has improved to a median of 11 hours. Nice work.
One thing I would like us to look at: your submittal-to-interview ratio is 32%
and the team average is 48%. Can we go through three rejected profiles
together and see what the client said? Then let's agree one change to try
for the next two weeks."
    Example: a coaching win
    Reviewing three rejected profiles together, the recruiter realises she skipped the must-have "AWS production experience" check. She adds it to her screening script. Two weeks later her ratio rises to 45%, and the improvement came from one concrete change, not general pressure.

    🎭 Spotting and stopping gaming

    Gaming behaviourWhy it happensFix
    Submittal dumpingRewarded for volumeMeasure ratios and cap per req
    Cherry-picking easy reqsWants quick startsAssign reqs by priority; use difficulty weights
    Stale statuses left openAvoids showing failureWeekly status audit; link to hygiene score
    Backdating timestampsAvoids SLA breachesSystem-generated timestamps; audit logs
    Hoarding reqsFear of losing creditShared team goals; recognise collaboration
    ⚠️
    When a measure becomes a target, people optimise it. Use several measures together and keep reading the data critically.
    Example: catching a pattern
    A recruiter's interview rate is a perfect 100%, which is suspicious. You check and find she logs only submittals that already have interviews scheduled. Her true rate is 40%. You explain why accurate logging matters and fix the process, rather than punishing her harshly for a first mistake.

    📉 Underperformance

    StepWhat to do
    1. Check the dataIs it accurate? Is the req mix fair?
    2. Find the causeSkill, training, workload, motivation, or personal circumstances?
    3. CoachClear expectations, support and a short plan
    4. Review progressCheck measures every 2 weeks
    5. Formal process if neededFollow company policy with HR, in writing and fairly
    Example: finding the real cause
    A recruiter's metrics drop for six weeks. In a private 1:1 you learn she is covering two colleagues' reqs after a resignation. The cause is workload, not skill. You rebalance the reqs and her numbers recover within a month.

    🏆 Recognition and retention

    • Praise specifically: say what they did and the result.
    • Share good practices: let top performers teach the team.
    • Give growth paths: senior recruiter, team lead, account roles.
    • Protect well-being: watch workload and burnout signals.
    • Reward collaboration as well as individual results.
    Example: sharing a win
    A recruiter's must-have checklist lifts her ratio from 38% to 55%. You ask her to present it for five minutes at the team meeting and give her credit. Three others adopt it, the team ratio rises, and she feels recognised, which helps keep her.

    Module 07 · Operating Rhythm & Escalations

    Operating Rhythm & Escalations

    Great delivery is a habit, not a heroic effort. Learn the meetings, reports and escalation routines that keep a team on track and keep clients informed.

    🥁 Why rhythm matters

    An operating rhythm is a regular set of meetings and checks. It makes problems visible early, spreads good practice and removes the need for last-minute rescues.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: rhythm vs firefighting
    Team X reviews aging reqs every Monday and rarely has a surprise breach. Team Y reviews only when a client complains, and is always rescuing. Both teams work hard; only one has a routine that finds trouble early.

    ☀️ The daily stand-up

    MinutesTopic
    0–3New reqs and priorities (P1s first)
    3–8Ambers and reds: what is blocked and who helps
    8–12Interviews, offers and starts today
    12–15Capacity: who has room, who is overloaded
    Example: a 15-minute stand-up that works
    The lead opens the SLA board: two ambers. One recruiter says she needs a sourcer for a Go req, another has capacity and takes a P1. In 15 minutes the day's risks are assigned, and nobody sits alone with a problem.

    📅 The weekly req review

    CheckDecision
    Aging over 15 daysRe-scope, adjust rate, add resource, or hold
    No submittals yetWhy? Is the req realistic?
    Submittals but no interviewsCheck quality and client feedback
    Interviews but no offersReview feedback and candidate fit
    Offers pendingCheck acceptance risk and speed
    Example: a decision in the review
    A req is 19 days old with five submittals and no interviews. The review reveals the client wants on-site work but the posted req says remote. You raise it with the account manager, the client confirms hybrid, and two candidates are interviewed in four days.

    🗓️ Monthly and quarterly reviews

    ReviewLooks atOutput

    Quarterly business review (QBR)Trends, client satisfaction, process changes, goalsPlan and targets

    Example: a trend caught in a QBR
    Over three months, first-submittal TAT on niche roles rises from 28 to 40 hours. At the QBR you link it to the loss of one specialist sourcer, and propose a hire. Looking at the trend, not just one month, shows the cause clearly.

    ⏸️ Handling holds and re-scopes

    SituationWhat to do
    Client puts the req on holdPause the SLA clock with dates logged; agree a review date
    Client changes requirementsConfirm in writing; restart or adjust the clock as agreed
    Req is unrealisticShare market data; propose a change in rate, skills or location
    Req is cancelledClose it properly; record the reason; re-use candidates elsewhere
    Example: an unrealistic req
    A client wants a Kubernetes expert with 10 years of experience at a mid-level rate. You show five similar postings and two market rate comparisons, and propose either a higher rate or a 6-year requirement. The client chooses the second, and the req fills in 12 days.

    🌊 Surge handling

    Sometimes 20 reqs arrive in one day, for example after winning a new client. A plan prevents chaos.

    StepAction
    1. TriageSort the reqs by priority and difficulty
    2. AssignMatch recruiters to reqs by skill and capacity
    3. Add resourceTemporary sourcers, partners, bench review
    4. Agree expectationsTell the client what to expect and when
    5. Monitor dailyCheck the SLA board twice a day during the surge
    Example: a surge plan
    A new client sends 20 reqs on a Monday. You triage into 4 P1, 10 P2 and 6 P3, assign the P1s to your four strongest recruiters, bring in two temporary sourcers, and tell the client the first submittals for P1s arrive within 8 hours. By Friday 17 of 20 have submittals out.

    📑 Reporting

    ReportAudienceFrequency
    Live SLA boardDelivery teamReal time
    Weekly client snapshotClient contactsWeekly
    Monthly scorecardClient and managementMonthly
    Recruiter scorecardsRecruiters and their leadMonthly
    Trend and capacity reportLeadershipQuarterly
    📝
    Report honestly. Never adjust timestamps, hide late reqs or change statuses to improve a number. Accurate reporting is the foundation of trust with clients and management.
    Example: honest reporting pays off
    You report 88% SLA compliance with a clear cause (three niche reqs) and a plan. The client's procurement team replies that they value the honesty and agree to a separate niche SLA. A falsely perfect 100% would have hidden the issue and invited a worse surprise later.

    🤖 Playbooks and automation

    • Playbooks: written steps for common situations (new req, amber req, breach, surge).
    • Automation: auto-assignment rules, SLA timers, alerts at 50% and 80%, scheduling links.
    • Templates: status updates, breach messages, offer prep checklists.
    • Knowledge sharing: short monthly sessions to share what worked.
    Example: automating the boring part
    An automated alert posts to the team channel when any req reaches 80% of its SLA clock. Manual dashboard checking drops by an hour a day, and amber reqs are acted on within 15 minutes instead of a couple of hours.

    Advanced: Forecasting, Root Causes & Judgement

    What separates a good delivery lead from a trusted one: planning capacity before it breaks, finding real root causes, resisting metric traps and handling difficult situations with calm, honest judgement.

    🔭 Capacity forecasting

    Predict how many recruiters you need, so you can hire, borrow or re-plan before SLAs slip.

    StepExample (illustrative)
    Expected new reqs next month40
    Reqs still open from this month35
    Reqs expected to fill or close30
    Open reqs next month40 + 35 − 30 = 45
    Reqs one recruiter handles well8
    Recruiters needed45 ÷ 8 = 5.6, so 6
    Recruiters available5, a gap of 1
    Example: acting before the breach
    Your forecast shows a gap of one recruiter next month. You request a temporary contractor now, so they are trained by the time volume peaks. Waiting until SLAs slip would cost a month of delays and a damaged scorecard.

    🌳 Root-cause analysis

    Do not stop at the first answer. Ask "why?" repeatedly until you reach a cause you can fix.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Surface causeRoot causeLasting fix
    Recruiter was slowShe had 14 reqs against a limit of 8Capacity limits and load balancing
    Client rejected candidatesIntake missed the must-have skillsIntake checklist
    Candidate declined offerRate was never confirmed up frontRate check at the first call
    Example: the second "why"
    First answer: "The recruiter did not send candidates fast enough." Second why: "Because she received the req two days late." Third why: "Because new reqs sat in a shared inbox." The fix is an auto-assignment rule, not coaching the recruiter on speed.

    🪤 Metric traps

    TrapWhat goes wrongSafer approach
    Averages hide outliersA few slow reqs skew the pictureReport median and P90 together
    Tiny samples3 reqs make a percentage meaninglessState the count; avoid big conclusions
    Single-metric focusOptimising one number damages anotherPair speed with quality
    Target gamingPeople optimise the number, not the goalUse several measures and audit data
    Correlation as causeTwo numbers move together by coincidenceLook for the mechanism before acting
    Example: a tiny sample
    A recruiter filled 2 of 2 reqs (100% fill rate) while another filled 8 of 12 (67%). Naively, the first looks better. With only 2 reqs the first figure says almost nothing, so you wait for more data before making any judgement.

    💎 Prioritising by value

    ReqWeekly marginDifficultyClient importanceDecision
    AHighCommon skillTop-3 clientP1, assign best recruiter
    BMediumHard nicheGrowing clientP2 with a sourcing sprint
    CLowCommonSmall clientP3, standard process
    💡
    Cost of delay: each day a high-margin req stays open costs real margin. A req worth $4,000 a week costs about $800 per working day of delay. Prioritise by that kind of arithmetic, but never ignore a strategic client's SLA.
    Example: margin-aware prioritisation
    Two reqs need a senior recruiter this afternoon. Req A is worth about $4,000 a week; Req C about $1,000. You give A first call and C second, and tell the C client the realistic time. The explanation matters as much as the order.

You send the log with dates to the client's vendor manager and say, "Here is where the time went. Could we pause the clock for the feedback delay and agree a reminder process?" They agree.

    Example: diagnosing a mixed picture
    SLA compliance is 97% but the submittal-to-hire ratio is only 6%. The team is hitting speed targets but sending weak candidates. You add a must-have checklist, speed dips to 94%, and the ratio rises to 11%: a better balance.

    ⚖️ Ethical delivery

    • Report accurately: never edit timestamps or hide late reqs.
    • Be fair in recruiter comparisons: adjust for difficulty, and avoid favourites.
    • Respect candidates: do not let speed targets lead to careless or misleading submittals.
    • Protect people: watch workload and well-being, and follow labour rules.
    • Be honest with clients about what can realistically be delivered.
    Example: the integrity test
    Under pressure, a manager suggests starting the SLA clock later to avoid a breach. You decline politely and offer an alternative: "Let's report it honestly, show the cause and the fix, and ask the client to review the SLA for niche roles." Honest numbers protect your reputation for the long term.

    📖 Full case study

    Situation: A fintech client's scorecard shows your SLA compliance at 82% (target 95%), submittal-to-interview at 34% (target 45%), and two complaints about slow feedback.

    1. Data check: you confirm timestamps and statuses are accurate. They are, with minor logging gaps fixed.
    2. Segment: common roles meet SLA at 97%; niche roles only at 55%. Interview feedback from the client takes 6 days on average.
    3. Root causes: a lack of niche sourcing capacity, unclear must-haves at intake, and slow client feedback.
    4. Actions: add a specialist sourcer; introduce a must-have checklist and pre-submittal calls; propose a two-tier SLA and a 2-day feedback commitment.
    5. Client meeting: you present the data and plan; the client agrees to the niche SLA and a feedback reminder.
    6. Result after 8 weeks: SLA compliance 94%, submittal-to-interview 47%, feedback time down to 3 days, no complaints.
    ✅
    Why it worked: data first, honest segmentation, root causes rather than blame, shared accountability with the client, and a plan with measures.
    Example: what you would say at the client review
    "Common roles meet SLA at 97%; niche roles do not. We are adding specialist sourcing and an intake checklist. We would like to agree a niche-role SLA and a 2-day feedback target so both of us can meet the commitments."
