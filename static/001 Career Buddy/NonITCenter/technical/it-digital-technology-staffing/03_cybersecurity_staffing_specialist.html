    The Role & Cyber Hiring Basics

    Before you source a SOC analyst or a penetration tester, understand what a Cybersecurity Staffing Specialist owns, why security hiring works differently from other IT hiring, and the words every conversation uses.

    🧭 What does this specialist do?

    A Cybersecurity Staffing Specialist finds, screens and places the people who defend organisations from cyber attacks: analysts who watch for threats, testers who ethically break into systems to find weaknesses, and engineers who build defences. You are not expected to hack anything. You are expected to tell real security skill from impressive-sounding claims, understand certifications, and handle security clearance verification correctly.

    🛡️
    Source
    Find SecOps and SOC analysts and penetration testers in a small, busy market.
    🎓
    Understand certs
    Know what CISSP, CEH, CISM and others really prove, and what they do not.
    🔐
    Verify clearance
    Check clearance claims through the proper channels, without asking for sensitive details.
    🤝
    Close
    Match people to roles, handle shifts and on-call, and win the offer.
    Example: one request, three different people
    A client says: "We need someone for security." That could mean a SOC analyst (watches alerts around the clock), a penetration tester (attacks systems with permission to find holes) or a security architect (designs defences). Your first job is to ask questions until the sentence becomes one specific profile.

    ⚖️ Why cyber hiring is different

    FactorGeneral IT hiringCybersecurity hiring
    Talent poolLargeSmall, especially for senior and specialised roles
    TrustNormal checksStaff hold privileged access, so deeper background checks
    CredentialsDegrees, some certsCertifications often required or heavily weighted
    Government workRareCommon, which brings clearances and citizenship rules
    Working patternOffice hoursShifts, on-call, incident response at any hour
    Proof of skillCode and projectsLabs, write-ups, reports, bug-bounty results
    Example: why one req takes months
    A defence contractor wants a SOC analyst who holds an active Secret clearance and a security certification, works night shifts, and lives near the site. Each condition shrinks the pool. Together they can turn a "common" role into a hard one, so set expectations early.

    🛤️ The hiring pipeline

    Every requirement ("req") passes through the same stages. In security, the stages that need extra care are intake (what are the real requirements: clearance, certs, shifts?) and verification (are the claims real?).

    SourcingSearch networks, communities and referrals.Long list
    ScreeningResume, call, scenario questions, hands-on evidence.Shortlist

    Monday: intake for a Tier 2 SOC analyst (rotating shifts, Secret clearance, Security+ or equivalent). Tuesday: you source 35 profiles. Wednesday: 8 calls. Thursday: 3 candidates pass scenario questions. Friday: the client's security officer confirms two clearances, and you submit both.

    Example: decoding a recruiter message
    "Client needs a SOC analyst with SIEM and EDR experience, Secret clearance, rotating shifts."
    Decoded: someone who monitors alerts, uses a log-collection platform (SIEM) and an endpoint protection tool (EDR), holds a government clearance, and can work day and night shifts.

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Client side

    • CISO / security director: owns the security programme and budget.
    • SOC manager or security lead: judges technical depth.
    • Facility Security Officer (FSO): verifies clearances for cleared contractors.
    • HR: pay, background checks, compliance.

    🧑‍💻 Candidate side

    • Analysts: watch, investigate and respond.
    • Testers: simulate attacks with permission.
    • Engineers and architects: build and design defences.
    • GRC staff: governance, risk and compliance.
    Example: asking the right person
    You are unsure whether a client needs an active clearance or just "clearable" candidates. Instead of guessing, you ask the hiring manager and the client's security officer. Their answer changes your whole search, and saves you from submitting people who cannot start.

    📡 Signal vs buzzwords

    Security resumes are full of tool names. Signal is specific evidence: what was detected, how it was investigated, what was fixed, what was learned.

    🔴 Low signal

    "Experienced in cybersecurity using SIEM, EDR, firewalls and threat intelligence."

    🟢 High signal

    "As a Tier 2 analyst I investigated about 40 escalated alerts a week. I tuned a noisy rule and cut false positives by 35%, and led response to a compromised account within 20 minutes."

    Example: the four follow-ups
    Resume: "Handled security incidents." Ask: What happened? (a phishing email stole credentials). What did you do? (reset the account, blocked the sender, searched for other victims). How much? (6 users affected). What would you change? (add multi-factor authentication earlier).

    Module 02 · Technology Literacy

    Cybersecurity Fundamentals

    A plain-English map of what security teams protect, how attacks unfold, and which job does which part, so every skill on a resume has a place to live in your head.

    🔺 The CIA triad

    Almost everything in security protects three goals, known as the CIA triad.

    🤫
    Confidentiality
    Only the right people can see the data.
    ✅
    Integrity
    Data and systems are not changed without permission.
    🟢
    Availability
    Systems and data are there when needed.
    Example: one bank, three attacks
    A hacker reads customer records: confidentiality lost. A hacker changes an account balance: integrity lost. Ransomware locks the bank's systems: availability lost. Recruiters who can name the goal at risk can follow what a team is protecting.

    🎯 How attacks unfold

    Most attacks follow a pattern. Defenders try to spot and stop the attacker at each step.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    StepPlain meaning
    ReconnaissanceGather information about the target
    Initial accessGet in, often through phishing or a weak password
    Foothold and moving sidewaysStay inside and reach other systems
    ImpactSteal data, lock systems, or disrupt services
    Example: a phishing story
    An employee clicks a fake invoice link and enters a password (initial access). The attacker logs in, finds a shared drive, and copies files. A SOC analyst sees unusual logins at 3 a.m. and stops it. The earlier the detection, the smaller the damage.

    🏰 Defence in depth

    No single tool is enough, so organisations stack several layers, like a castle with a moat, walls and guards.

    LayerExample controls
    PeopleTraining, phishing awareness
    IdentityStrong passwords, multi-factor authentication
    NetworkFirewalls, segmentation
    EndpointAntivirus, EDR, patching
    Application and dataSecure coding, encryption
    MonitoringLogging, SIEM, SOC
    Example: layers saving the day
    A stolen password alone does not give access, because multi-factor authentication asks for a second proof. If the attacker gets through anyway, endpoint protection flags odd behaviour, and the SOC investigates. Each layer is a second chance.

    🗂️ Security domains

    DomainFocusKeywords
    Security operationsMonitor, detect, respondSOC, SIEM, EDR, incident response
    Offensive securityFind weaknesses by simulating attacksPen testing, red team
    Cloud securityProtect cloud workloadsIAM, CSPM, AWS/Azure security
    Application securitySecure softwareSAST, DAST, OWASP
    Identity and accessWho can do whatIAM, SSO, MFA, PAM
    GRCGovernance, risk, complianceISO 27001, NIST, audits
    Threat intelligenceKnow the attackersIOCs, MITRE ATT&CK
    Example: match task to domain
    "Review how staff request and get access to payroll" → identity and access. "Check our web app for common flaws" → application security. "Prepare us for an ISO 27001 audit" → GRC.

    🗺️ Role landscape

    RoleMain jobKeywords
    SOC Analyst (Tier 1)Watches alerts, triagesSIEM, ticketing, playbooks
    SOC Analyst (Tier 2/3)Investigates and responds to complex incidentsEDR, forensics, threat hunting
    Incident ResponderLeads response when a breach happensContainment, forensics, reporting
    Detection EngineerBuilds the rules that raise alertsSIEM rules, ATT&CK, scripting
    Penetration TesterEthically attacks systems to find holesBurp Suite, Nmap, Metasploit, reports
    Security EngineerBuilds and maintains defencesFirewalls, cloud, automation
    Security ArchitectDesigns the overall security approachZero trust, frameworks, risk
    GRC AnalystPolicies, audits, complianceISO 27001, SOC 2, risk registers
    Example: pick the role
    "Someone to build and tune the rules that tell us about attacks" → Detection Engineer. "Someone to lead us when ransomware hits" → Incident Responder. "Someone to test our website before launch" → Penetration Tester.

    🎨 Red, blue and purple

    🔴 Red team

    Simulates real attackers, with permission, to test defences.

    🔵 Blue team

    Defends: monitors, detects, responds, hardens.

    🟣 Purple team

    Red and blue work together so every test improves detection.

    ⚪ White team

    Referees an exercise: sets rules and judges results.

    Example: an exercise
    The red team sends a simulated phishing email. The blue team's SIEM misses one step. In a purple session both teams review it and add a detection rule. Next time, the same technique triggers an alert in minutes.

    📐 Frameworks to know

    FrameworkWhat it isWhere you hear it
    MITRE ATT&CKA catalogue of attacker tactics and techniquesSOC, detection, threat intel
    NIST Cybersecurity FrameworkA structure for managing cyber riskGRC, leadership
    ISO 27001International standard for a security management systemAudits, compliance
    OWASP Top 10The most critical web application risksAppSec, pen testing
    Example: a name worth recognising
    A candidate says, "I mapped our detections to MITRE ATT&CK and found we had no coverage for credential theft." You do not need the details. You recognise that this is a structured, gap-finding approach, a good sign in a detection role.

    🪤 Beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "Hacker" means criminal, so avoid it.
    • "A certification proves they can do the job."
    • "Security is one job."

    ✅ Reality

    • Ethical hackers are authorised testers; intent and permission matter.
    • Certs show knowledge; ask for hands-on evidence.
    • Security has many specialities with different skills.
    Example: fixing a wrong search
    A recruiter searches only "cybersecurity engineer" for a night-shift monitoring role and gets architects who want day work. After the intake call they search "SOC analyst" OR "security analyst" OR "SecOps" with SIEM tools, and the shortlist fits.

    Module 03 · Sourcing SecOps & SOC Analysts

    SecOps & SOC Analysts

    The people who watch, investigate and respond around the clock. Learn how a SOC works, the tools behind it, and how to separate real analysts from tool-name collectors.

    🏢 Inside a SOC

    A Security Operations Center (SOC) is a team and a set of tools that continuously watch an organisation's systems for signs of attack, then investigate and respond. Think of an air-traffic control room: many screens, constant alerts, quick decisions.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: a night in the SOC
    At 2 a.m. the SIEM alerts: one user logged in from two countries in ten minutes. The analyst checks the logins, sees an impossible-travel pattern, disables the account, resets the password, and records what happened for the morning team.

    🪜 Analyst tiers

    TierMain workTypical experience
    Tier 1Watch alerts, confirm or dismiss, escalate0–2 years
    Tier 2Investigate escalated incidents, contain threats2–5 years
    Tier 3Deep forensics, threat hunting, improve detections5+ years
    SOC managerRuns the team, metrics, staffing, reporting7+ years
    📝
    Tier names and years differ by company. Judge by what the person actually handled: alert volume, incident types and how far they took an investigation.
    Example: same title, different scope
    Candidate A, "Senior SOC Analyst", mainly closes alerts using a runbook. Candidate B, "SOC Analyst II", investigates compromised accounts end to end and writes new detection rules. B may fit a Tier 2/3 req better despite the humbler title.

    🧰 The SOC toolkit

    NeedWhat it doesCommon tools
    SIEMCollects logs, correlates events, raises alertsSplunk, Microsoft Sentinel, IBM QRadar, Elastic Security
    EDR / XDRWatches endpoints, lets analysts isolate a machineCrowdStrike, Microsoft Defender, SentinelOne
    SOARAutomates repetitive response stepsCortex XSOAR, Splunk SOAR, Tines
    Network securityBlocks and inspects trafficFirewalls, IDS/IPS
    Threat intelligenceInformation on known attackers and indicatorsThreat feeds, MISP
    TicketingTracks cases and handoversServiceNow, Jira
    SplunkSentinelQRadarCrowdStrikeDefenderSentinelOneSOARMITRE ATT&CK
    Example: tools transfer
    A JD says Splunk and the candidate used Microsoft Sentinel for three years. Both are SIEMs: collect logs, write queries, build alerts. Ask: "What was the hardest query or alert you built?" Concepts transfer; brand names are only a starting point.

    🔎 Alert triage

    Triage means quickly deciding whether an alert is a real threat. A good analyst follows a repeatable routine.

    1. Validate: is the alert real or a false positive (fires but no real threat)?
    2. Enrich: add context: user, device, location, threat-intelligence lookups.
    3. Scope: who or what else is affected?
    4. Decide: close, escalate or contain.
    5. Document: write what was found and done.
    6. Improve: suggest tuning if the alert was noisy.
    Example: a triage in numbers
    An alert says "malware detected" on a laptop. The analyst confirms it is real, sees the same file on two other machines (scope), isolates all three with the EDR tool, and escalates to Tier 2. Total time: 12 minutes.

    🚨 Incident response lifecycle

    The classic NIST incident-handling lifecycle has four phases. Newer guidance maps the same ideas to a broader framework, but candidates still use these words.

    PhaseWhat happens
    1. PreparationPlans, tools, training, playbooks
    2. Detection and analysisSpot the incident, work out what it is
    3. Containment, eradication, recoveryStop the spread, remove the cause, restore systems
    4. Post-incident activityLessons learned and improvements
    Example: the lifecycle in a sentence
    "We contained the infected laptops, removed the malware, restored from clean backups, and afterwards added a rule so the same technique alerts earlier." That candidate has described containment, eradication, recovery and lessons learned in one breath.

    📊 Metrics & burnout

    MetricMeaning
    MTTDMean time to detect: how long before a threat is noticed
    MTTRMean time to respond or recover
    False-positive rateShare of alerts that were not real threats
    Alert volume per analystWorkload; too high means missed threats and burnout
    ⚠️
    Why this matters to you. SOC roles have high turnover because of shift work and alert fatigue. Ask candidates what they want from a role, and tell clients honestly about shift patterns.
    Example: a retention clue
    A SOC receives 1,200 alerts a day and 70% are false positives. Analysts are exhausted and leaving. A candidate who says "I tuned our top five noisy rules and automated enrichment, so false positives dropped from 70% to 40%" is solving exactly the client's real problem.

    ⚙️ SecOps beyond the analyst

    RoleFocusSignals to listen for
    Detection engineerBuilds and tunes alert rulesQuery languages, ATT&CK mapping, testing detections
    Threat hunterProactively searches for hidden threatsHypotheses, baselines, anomaly analysis
    SOAR / automation engineerAutomates repetitive responsePython, APIs, playbooks
    Incident responderLeads breach responseForensics, containment, reporting
    Example: hunting versus alert-watching
    A SOC analyst reacts when an alert fires. A threat hunter asks, "If an attacker were already inside, what would I see?" and searches the logs for that pattern even though no alert has fired.

    💬 Screening questions

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "Walk me through your last real incident."Detection, steps, tools, timeline, outcome, lesson"We handled many incidents."
    "An alert shows a login from a new country. What do you do?"Validate, check user and device, look for other activity, contain if needed, document"Block it."
    "How do you reduce false positives?"Tune thresholds, add context, whitelist carefully with review"Ignore noisy alerts."
    "How many alerts did you handle and how?"Numbers, shift pattern, escalation rulesCannot say
    "Are shifts and on-call acceptable?"Clear, honest answerEvasive
    Example: a strong answer
    "A user reported a strange email. I checked the headers, found the link led to a fake sign-in page, searched our mail logs and found 5 other recipients, removed the email from all inboxes, reset 2 compromised passwords and wrote it up. Took about 40 minutes." Specific steps, scope, numbers and outcome.

    🚩 Red flags

    • Lists ten security tools but cannot describe one incident.
    • No numbers: alert volume, response time, users affected.
    • Describes only watching dashboards, never investigating.
    • "Senior" title with only Tier 1 examples.
    • Unclear about shift availability, then surprised by it later.
    Example: probing a red flag
    Resume: "Expert in threat detection." You ask: "Give me one detection you wrote or tuned, and how you knew it worked." A vague answer means the claim is unproven; ask the SOC lead for a deeper interview before submitting.

    Module 04 · Sourcing Penetration Testers

    Penetration Testers

    Penetration testers are paid to break in, legally. Learn what they do, how to read their evidence, and how to separate genuine testers from people who only run scanning tools.

    🗝️ What penetration testing is

    A penetration test (pen test) is an authorised simulated attack on a system to find weaknesses before real attackers do. Think of hiring a locksmith to try to get into your building and report which doors were weak.

    🔍 Vulnerability scan

    An automated tool lists possible weaknesses. Quick, broad, often noisy.

    🗝️ Penetration test

    A skilled person tries to exploit weaknesses and shows real impact. Slower, deeper, creative.

    Example: scan versus test
    A scanner reports "outdated software found". A pen tester shows that this outdated software let them read customer records, and explains how to fix it. The second is far more useful, and is what you are hiring for.

    🎯 Types of tests

    TypeTargetKeywords
    Web applicationWebsites and web appsOWASP Top 10, Burp Suite, APIs
    Network / infrastructureServers, networks, Active DirectoryNmap, enumeration, privilege escalation
    CloudAWS, Azure, GCP setupsMisconfiguration, IAM
    MobileiOS and Android appsReverse engineering, API testing
    WirelessWi-Fi networksEncryption, rogue access points
    Social engineeringPeoplePhishing tests
    Red teamWhole organisation, stealthyLong engagements, objectives
    Example: matching tester to need
    A fintech launching a mobile banking app needs a mobile and API tester. A hospital worried about its internal network needs network / Active Directory testing. Different specialities, so one resume does not fit both.

    🛤️ How a test runs

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    StepPlain meaning
    Scope and permissionWritten agreement on what may be tested, when and how
    Recon and scanningLearn about the target and find possible entry points
    ExploitationProve a weakness is real
    ReportExplain findings, risk and fixes clearly
    RetestCheck the fixes worked
    💡
    The report is the product the client buys. A great tester who writes poor reports is a risky hire for consulting roles.
    Example: a finding, in words
    "The customer portal let any logged-in user view another user's invoices by changing a number in the address. Risk: high. Fix: check ownership on the server. Retested: fixed." Notice: clear issue, impact, fix and retest, with no jargon.

    🧰 Tools & standards

    ItemUsed for
    Kali LinuxA Linux system bundled with security tools
    NmapDiscovering devices and open services on a network
    Burp SuiteTesting web applications
    MetasploitA framework for testing known weaknesses
    WiresharkInspecting network traffic
    BloodHoundMapping attack paths in Active Directory
    OWASP Top 10, PTESStandards and methodologies for web risks and test structure
    ⚠️
    Tools do not make a tester. Anyone can install Kali Linux. The skill is in understanding what the results mean and chaining findings together.
    Example: tool list versus understanding
    Candidate A lists 20 tools. Candidate B says, "The scan showed an open admin page; I found it reused a default password, which gave me access to the database, which exposed customer emails." B understands impact. A has not shown it.

    ⚖️ Authorisation and ethics

    • Testing without written permission is illegal, however good the intent.
    • Rules of engagement define scope, allowed methods, timing and who to call if something breaks.
    • Testers must protect any data they see and report issues responsibly.
    • Never ask a candidate to "show me a live hack"; ask them to describe past authorised work instead.
    Example: a good boundary
    A candidate describes a finding but says, "I can't name the client or share the report because of confidentiality." That is a good sign. You ask for the method, impact and fix instead, and do not push for confidential details.

    🏅 Evidence of skill

    EvidenceWhat it showsCaution
    OSCP and similar practical certsCan complete hands-on challenges under time pressureLab skill is not the same as client work
    Public CVEs credited to themFound real vulnerabilities in real productsCheck the name and the product
    Bug bounty profile (HackerOne, Bugcrowd)Ongoing real-world findingsQuality varies; ask about their best find
    CTF and practice platforms (Hack The Box, TryHackMe)Passion and practiceA game, not a client engagement
    Conference talks, blogs, write-upsDepth and communicationCheck authorship
    Example: reading a profile
    A candidate's bug-bounty page shows 14 accepted reports, including two rated high severity, and a blog write-up explaining one of them clearly. That is strong, verifiable, public evidence, and a great conversation starter.

    💬 Screening questions

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "Describe your most interesting past engagement."Scope, approach, finding, impact, fix, personal role"I tested lots of systems."
    "How do you decide what to test first?"Risk, business impact, attack surface"Run the scanner."
    "What do you put in a report?"Summary for executives, details, risk rating, fix steps, evidence"A list of tool output."
    "How do you explain a finding to a non-technical manager?"Plain language, business impactHeavy jargon
    "What are your boundaries on scope?"Written permission, rules of engagement"I test whatever I find."
    Example: a strong answer
    "On a retail web app I found an insecure direct object reference in the order API. I could view other customers' orders. I rated it high, wrote a one-page summary for the CTO and detailed steps for developers, and retested after the fix."

    🚩 Red flags & worked example

    • Only scanner output, no manual findings.
    • Cannot describe a report or how they communicate findings.
    • Claims about illegal or unauthorised activity.
    • Senior claim with only practice-platform evidence.

    🟢 Candidate A

    "Six years of web and API testing. 30 reports delivered. Found a business-logic flaw that let users apply a discount twice. Two CVEs credited. I write the executive summary myself."

    Verdict: hands-on, reportable, verifiable. Advance.

    🔴 Candidate B

    "Experienced pen tester using Nmap, Burp, Metasploit, Nessus, Kali, Wireshark and many more tools."

    Verdict: tools only. Probe for a real engagement.

    Example: how you would decide
    Candidate A names a real flaw (a discount applied twice), a number of reports and public CVEs, so you advance A and ask for a report sample. Candidate B lists seven tools and no engagement, so you ask: "Tell me about one test from start to finish." If B cannot, you do not submit.

    Module 05 · Understanding Certifications

    Security Certifications: CISSP, CEH, CISM & More

    Certifications are the shorthand of the security world. Learn what each one proves, which roles it fits, how to verify it, and how to use it as a signal without letting it replace evidence of real skill.

    🎓 What certifications prove

    A certification shows that someone studied a body of knowledge and passed an exam, sometimes with required experience. Some are management-focused, some hands-on. None guarantees performance.

    ✅ What a cert can show

    • Structured learning
    • Commitment to the field
    • A shared vocabulary
    • Meets a client or contract requirement

    ❌ What it cannot show

    • That they can investigate a real incident
    • Communication or teamwork
    • Judgement under pressure
    • That the credential is still active
    Example: cert plus proof
    Candidate A: CISSP, and says "I led our ISO 27001 audit and cut open risks by 40%." Candidate B: CISSP and nothing else. The cert is the same, but only A has shown the knowledge in action.

    🗺️ The certification map

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    LevelExamplesTypical audience
    EntryCompTIA Security+, SSCPNewcomers, junior analysts
    TechnicalGIAC (GSEC, GCIH, GCIA), CEH, OSCP, PNPTAnalysts, responders, testers
    CloudCCSP, AWS Security Specialty, Azure AZ-500Cloud security engineers
    ManagementCISSP, CISM, CISAManagers, architects, auditors
    Example: reading a cert list
    A resume shows Security+, GCIH and CISM. You can read this as: solid basics, incident-handling skill, and a move toward management. That already tells you which kind of role to discuss.

    🏛️ CISSP, CISM and CISA

    CertIssuerFocusExperience requirement
    CISSPISC2Broad, management-oriented knowledge across eight security domainsSeveral years of paid experience in multiple domains; a route exists for those still gaining experience
    CISMISACASecurity management, governance, risk and incident managementSeveral years of security management experience
    CISAISACAAuditing and assurance of information systemsSeveral years of audit or control experience
    📝
    Exact experience rules and waivers change over time, so confirm current details on the issuer's own website before advising a client or candidate.
    Example: which one for which role
    A client hiring a Security Manager asks for CISM or CISSP. A client hiring an IT Auditor asks for CISA. Offering a CISA-only candidate for a technical SOC role would be a mismatch.

    🧪 CEH, OSCP and Security+

    CertIssuerWhat it mainly shows
    CEH (Certified Ethical Hacker)EC-CouncilKnowledge of attack techniques and tools; a separate practical version exists
    OSCPOffSecHands-on ability in a practical, timed exam
    Security+CompTIAEntry-level baseline knowledge; often listed in government and defence contract requirements (check current policy)
    Example: CEH versus OSCP
    For a senior penetration tester role, a candidate holding only the knowledge-based CEH deserves a deeper hands-on conversation. A candidate holding OSCP has already shown practical lab ability, though you still ask about real client work.

    🎯 Matching certs to roles

    RoleHelpful certifications
    Tier 1 SOC analystSecurity+, entry-level vendor or SIEM certs
    Tier 2/3 analyst, incident responderGCIH, GCIA, GCFA or equivalent practical experience
    Penetration testerOSCP, PNPT, GPEN, CREST certifications
    Cloud security engineerCCSP, AWS Security Specialty, AZ-500
    Security manager or architectCISSP, CISM
    GRC or auditorCISA, ISO 27001 Lead Implementer or Auditor
    Example: a fair requirement
    A JD says "CISSP required" for a hands-on penetration tester. Ask the manager if this is a true must-have. Often the better filter is OSCP or equivalent proven experience, and CISSP is a nice-to-have.

    ✅ Verifying credentials

    1. Ask for the credential ID or the digital badge link.
    2. Check it on the issuer's official verification page.
    3. Confirm the name matches the candidate's legal or known name.
    4. Check the status: active, expired or suspended.
    5. Note the date earned and compare it with the claimed timeline.
    Example: a quick check
    A candidate sends a badge link for GCIH. The page shows his name, the certification, and an expiry date two years away. You record the date in your notes and move on, a two-minute check that protects the client.

    ⏳ Expiry & maintenance

    Many security certifications must be maintained, typically with continuing-education credits (often called CPEs) over a multi-year cycle and sometimes an annual fee. If someone stops, the certification can lapse.

    StatusMeaningWhat you do
    ActiveIn good standingRecord the expiry date
    Expiring soonRenewal due within monthsAsk about renewal plans
    LapsedNo longer validAsk why; check the issuer's reinstatement route
    Example: an expiring cert
    A candidate's CISM expires in three months and the client contract requires an active one. You ask, "Are you on track for your continuing-education credits?" If yes, note it in the write-up. If no, you flag the risk before submitting.

    ⚖️ Using certs fairly

    • Treat certifications as one signal, not an automatic filter.
    • Many excellent practitioners are self-taught or lack a specific cert; rejecting them loses good people.
    • Never accept a cert claim without verification, and verify everyone the same way.
    • Be wary of "exam dumps": ask scenario questions that memorisation cannot answer.
    Example: widening the net
    A client insists on "CISSP only". Two strong candidates have 9 years of security architecture experience and CISM instead. You present both with evidence and ask whether CISM meets the intent. The client agrees, and the search ends a month sooner.

    Module 06 · Clearance Verification

    Security Clearance Verification

    Clearances open government and defence work, and they are one of the most misunderstood parts of cyber hiring. Learn the levels, the language, and the correct way to verify a claim without asking for sensitive information.

    📝
    This module uses the United States system as its main example. Other countries have their own systems, and rules change, so always follow your company's compliance guidance and your client's security officer.

    🔐 What a clearance is

    A security clearance is a government decision that a person is trustworthy enough to access classified information at a certain level. It is granted after an investigation into background, finances, conduct and loyalty. A clearance is not a certificate you can earn by studying.

    Example: why it matters in hiring
    A defence contractor wins a contract that requires every analyst to hold an active Secret clearance. A brilliant candidate without one cannot start on day one, however strong the skills. That is why clearance status is a must-check at intake.

    📶 Levels & access

    LevelMeaning (simplified)Typical investigation
    ConfidentialLowest level of classified accessTier 3
    SecretInformation that could cause serious damage if exposedTier 3
    Top Secret (TS)Information that could cause exceptionally grave damageTier 5
    TS/SCITop Secret plus access to Sensitive Compartmented InformationTier 5, plus program-specific approvals
    💡
    SCI is not a level above Top Secret. It is an additional access approval for specific compartments of sensitive information, granted on top of a TS clearance. Some roles also require a polygraph, commonly described as counterintelligence-scope or full-scope.
    Example: reading a requirement
    "TS/SCI with CI polygraph required." Decoded: a Top Secret clearance, SCI access, and a counterintelligence-scope polygraph. That is a narrow pool. Tell the client early how long this kind of search typically takes.

    🏗️ How clearances work

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    • You cannot self-sponsor. A government agency or a cleared company with a contract need must sponsor the process.
    • The process commonly takes months, which is why already-cleared talent is rare and costly.
    • Citizenship rules apply; clearances for classified information generally require citizenship.
    • Many clearances are now covered by continuous vetting: ongoing checks instead of only periodic reviews.
    Example: why you cannot just apply
    A strong analyst says, "I'll apply for a Secret clearance and start in two weeks." He cannot apply on his own. An employer with a contract must sponsor him, and the process can take months. Correct expectations prevent a broken promise to your client.

    🔎 Active, inactive, interim

    TermMeaningRecruiter action
    Active / currentGranted and in use with a sponsoring employerVerify through the proper channel
    Inactive / not currently heldGranted earlier, but the person is not currently in a cleared roleAsk when it was last active; reinstatement may be possible
    InterimTemporary access granted while the full investigation continuesConfirm the level and whether the client accepts interim
    ClearableNo clearance yet, but likely to pass with sponsorshipCheck the client will sponsor
    ReciprocityOne agency accepting another's clearanceAsk the security officer whether it applies
    📝
    As a general guide, a clearance can often be reinstated if the person has been out of cleared work for less than 24 months. Rules and exceptions apply, so the client's security officer decides.
    Example: an inactive clearance
    A candidate says, "I held a Secret clearance until 18 months ago when I changed to a private-sector job." That is inactive but possibly reinstatable. You record the dates and ask the client's security officer, rather than calling it "active".

    ✅ Verification process

    A recruiter cannot verify a clearance alone. Only authorised security personnel can check status in the government's personnel-security system.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    StepWhoWhat
    1. Collect basicsYouLevel, polygraph type if relevant, approximate dates, last employer type
    2. Record as "claimed"YouMark it unverified in your notes
    3. Verify statusFacility Security Officer (FSO) or authorised security managerCheck the official system
    4. ConfirmFSO to clientWritten confirmation of status
    Example: the clean workflow
    You note: "Claims active TS/SCI, last used June, full-scope polygraph (self-reported)." You submit him to the client as claimed, pending verification. The client's FSO confirms the status within days. Nothing sensitive ever passed through your hands.

    🚫 What you may and may not ask

    ✅ Fine to ask

    • "What clearance level do you hold, and is it active?"
    • "Roughly when was it granted or last active?"
    • "Do you hold a polygraph, and which type?"
    • "Are you open to sponsorship if needed?"

    🚫 Do not ask for

    • Contents of the security questionnaire (SF-86)
    • Details of the investigation or any findings
    • Names of classified programmes or projects
    • Any classified information at all
    ⚠️
    A genuine cleared professional will refuse to discuss classified work. Treat that refusal as a good sign, and never press for details.
    Example: a good boundary
    Candidate: "I can't discuss the programme I supported." You reply, "Understood. Just the level, the dates and the type of role, please." That respects the rules and gets you what you need for verification.

    🌱 Clearable candidates

    When active-cleared talent is scarce, clearable candidates can widen the pool if the client can sponsor and the timeline allows.

    OptionBenefitTrade-off
    Active-cleared onlyStarts quicklyVery small, expensive pool
    Interim acceptedFaster than full processDepends on client acceptance
    Clearable plus sponsorshipMuch larger pool, lower payMonths before full start; may work unclassified tasks meanwhile
    Example: giving the client options
    Client wants an active TS analyst in three weeks. You present: (1) one active-cleared candidate at a premium rate, (2) a strong clearable analyst who can begin on unclassified tasks while sponsorship proceeds. The client chooses the second to balance cost and timeline.

    🌍 Other countries

    CountryExamples of vetting levels
    United StatesConfidential, Secret, Top Secret, SCI access
    United KingdomBPSS (baseline checks), SC (Security Check), DV (Developed Vetting)
    OthersEach country has its own names, rules and recognition agreements
    Example: do not assume equivalence
    A candidate holds a UK SC clearance and applies for a US Secret role. Whether it is recognised depends on formal agreements and the client's security officer. You do not promise equivalence; you ask.

    🚩 Red flags & worked example

    • Cannot state the level, approximate dates or type of role.
    • Says "I'll get it quickly" or tries to self-sponsor.
    • Offers classified details to prove the claim.
    • Resume shows clearance level but no cleared employer or contract.

    🟢 Candidate A

    "Active TS/SCI through my current employer, last investigation completed three years ago. I can't discuss my work, but my FSO can confirm status."

    Verdict: clear, proper, consistent. Submit as claimed and verify via FSO.

    🔴 Candidate B

    "I have a top clearance. Let me tell you about the programme I worked on, it was really interesting."

    Verdict: offering classified details is a warning sign. Stop the conversation and verify through proper channels.

    Example: handling both candidates
    With Candidate A you record the claim, tell the client it is claimed, pending FSO verification, and submit. With Candidate B you say, "Please do not share programme details. Level, dates and role type are enough," and verify only through the security officer. Same rule, different behaviour from the candidate.

    Module 07 · Sourcing & Screening Workflows

    Sourcing & Screening Cyber Talent

    Repeatable steps that turn a vague requirement into a short, verified shortlist, with search strings, scorecards and scripts you can copy today.

    🗺️ End-to-end workflow

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    Each stage has an exit rule: a clear yes or no before the candidate moves on. This keeps quality high and saves the hiring manager's time.

    Example: exit rules in action
    Resume score under 12 out of 18 → stop. Screening call fails the incident walkthrough → stop. Certification fails the issuer's verification → pause and clarify. Clearance claim passes the FSO check → submit. Every decision can be explained to the client.

    📞 The intake call

    1. What will this person do in the first 90 days?
    2. Which tools must they know (SIEM, EDR, scanners)?
    3. Which certifications are true must-haves and which are nice-to-have?
    4. Is a clearance needed? Level, polygraph, active, interim or clearable?
    5. What are the shifts, on-call and onsite requirements?
    6. What is the budget and contract type (W2 or C2C)?
    7. What does the interview process look like and how fast do you decide?
    Example: turning a messy JD into a clear brief
    "Rockstar cyber ninja with CISSP, CEH, OSCP, Secret clearance, 10 years, SIEM, EDR, cloud, pen testing."
    Your brief: Tier 2 SOC analyst. Must: SIEM and EDR, 3+ years, active Secret, rotating shifts. Nice: CISSP or Security+. Not required: pen testing, OSCP.

    📍 Where to find security talent

    For a web-application tester you search a bug-bounty leaderboard and find a hunter with several accepted high-severity reports. You send a short, respectful message that names a specific finding, and you get a reply within a day.

    MethodGood forKeep in mind

    Example: a fair practical
    A SOC candidate gets a 30-minute exercise: a sample log file with a suspicious login pattern. They explain what happened and what they would do. It is short, safe, paid or time-limited, and the same for every candidate.

    🤝 Trust & background checks

    Security staff hold privileged access, so clients usually run background checks: identity, employment, education, criminal record (as lawful) and sometimes credit checks for sensitive roles. Rules differ by country and state, so follow legal guidance.

    • Tell candidates early what checks will happen and get consent.
    • Apply the same checks to everyone in the same role.
    • A past issue is not an automatic rejection; the client follows its policy and the law.
    Example: no surprises
    At the screening call you say: "This role includes an employment and identity check, and an enhanced check because of the data access. Is that fine?" The candidate agrees, and nobody is surprised halfway through the process.

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Verification, Judgement & Metrics

    What separates a good recruiter from a trusted security talent partner: checking claims fairly, handling hard situations calmly, and measuring your results.

    🕵️ Verifying claims

    ClaimHow to verify
    "CISSP certified"Credential ID checked on the issuer's official verification page; confirm status and name
    "Active Top Secret"Client's FSO or authorised security manager verifies in the official system; never ask for classified details
    "Senior penetration tester"Engagement walkthrough without client secrets: scope, finding, impact, fix, personal role
    "Led a SOC"Team size, alert volume, metrics, incidents handled, who they reported to
    "Found CVEs"Search the public advisory for the name and the product
    📝
    Be fair. A mismatch can be a name change, a typo or a lapsed renewal. Ask politely first, and use the same checks for everyone.
    Example: a polite verification
    The issuer's page shows no CISSP under the name on the resume. You write: "Could you share your credential ID or badge link? The name on the registry may differ from your resume." It turns out the candidate used a maiden name on the registry, and nothing was wrong.

    🎭 Hard scenarios

    ScenarioBest response
    Client wants active TS/SCI plus full-scope polygraph in three weeks at below-market payShow market scarcity and present options: raise budget, accept interim or clearable candidates with sponsorship, or extend timeline
    Candidate's certification expired last monthAsk about reinstatement status; tell the client honestly
    Candidate offers to describe classified work to prove clearanceStop politely; verify only through the security officer
    "Senior" pen tester has only practice-platform evidenceAsk for a real engagement walkthrough; consider a mid-level role
    SOC candidate rejects night shifts after offerClarify shift expectations at the screening call next time; revisit options with the client
    Example: a scarcity conversation
    Client wants an active TS/SCI analyst at $110k where comparable cleared roles post higher. You show current postings and offer: (1) a premium for one active-cleared candidate, (2) a clearable analyst with sponsorship and a three-month ramp, or (3) a longer timeline. The client picks option 2.

    🔥 Retention in a burnout market

    SOC and incident-response roles lose people to alert fatigue and shift work. A recruiter who understands this places better and loses fewer hires.

    🧹
    Alert tuning
    Fewer false positives means calmer analysts.
    🤖
    Automation
    SOAR handles repetitive enrichment so humans investigate.
    🔄
    Rotation and growth
    Move between triage, hunting and engineering.
    🎓
    Training budget
    Certification and lab support keeps people learning.
    Example: asking the client the right question
    Before sourcing you ask: "What is the shift pattern, how many alerts per analyst, and how do people grow here?" The honest answers let you pitch candidates who want exactly that, and avoid a hire who quits in three months.

    💰 Compensation signals

    📝
    Pay varies by country, city and time. No figures are quoted here; use your company's current market data.
    FactorEffect on pay
    Active clearance (especially TS/SCI, polygraph)Premium, because the pool is small
    Hands-on specialist skill (cloud, incident response, offensive)Premium over generalists
    Shift or on-call burdenOften paid as a shift differential or allowance

    Example: two roles, same title
    Both are "Security Analyst". Role A: day shift, no clearance. Role B: nights, active Secret clearance, on-call. Role B should reasonably pay more, and explaining why helps the client set the right band.

    📊 Metrics that matter

    MetricFormulaExample
    Screen-to-submittalSubmittals ÷ screening calls5 ÷ 20 = 25%
    Submittal-to-interviewInterviews ÷ submittals6 ÷ 10 = 60%
    Verification pass rateVerified ÷ claimed9 ÷ 10 = 90%
    Assessment drop-offDropped ÷ invited to practical4 ÷ 10 = 40%
    Time to fillStart date − req date74 days (cleared role)
    💡
    Read the numbers. A high assessment drop-off suggests the practical is too long or unclear. A low verification pass rate means your early screening is accepting claims too easily. A low submittal-to-interview ratio means poor fit.
    Example: diagnosing drop-off
    Out of 10 candidates invited to a 3-hour lab, 4 withdraw. You shorten it to 45 minutes, explain the purpose, and offer feedback. The next batch of 10 has only 1 withdrawal.

    ⚖️ Ethical sourcing

    • Use public, professional information and respect each platform's terms.
    • Follow data-protection laws and honour opt-out requests.
    • Do not contact people through hacked or leaked data, ever.
    • Never encourage candidates to test real systems without permission, even "as a quick demo".
    • Respect non-solicit agreements: check with legal first.
    Example: a clean habit
    You find a bug-bounty hunter's public profile, note the source ("public leaderboard, profile page"), message them professionally, and delete their details immediately when they ask. Nothing about the process would embarrass you if the candidate saw your notes.

    📖 Full case study

    Req: Tier 2 SOC Analyst for a defence contractor: active Secret clearance, SIEM and EDR, rotating shifts, onsite, start in 6 weeks.

    1. Intake: must-haves: Splunk or equivalent SIEM, EDR, 3+ years, active Secret. Nice: Security+ or CISSP. Interim not accepted.
    2. Source: 60 profiles from cleared communities and networks.
    3. Screen: 16 calls. 7 fail the incident walkthrough (tools only). 9 remain.
    4. Hands-on: 30-minute log exercise: 6 pass.
    5. Verify: certifications checked on issuer pages. Clearances recorded as "claimed" and sent to the client's FSO. 4 confirmed.
    6. Submit: 4 candidates with honest labels in week 2.
    7. Result: 3 interviews, 1 offer, accepted in week 4, started at week 6.
    ✅
    Why it worked: clear intake, evidence-based screening, a fair practical, proper verification channels, and honest labels in the write-up.
    Example: what you would say to the client
    "I have 4 candidates. Two have strong incident-response evidence and verified certifications. Clearance status for all four is confirmed by your FSO. I recommend Candidate 2 for depth and Candidate 4 as a strong alternative."
