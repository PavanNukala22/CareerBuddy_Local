    The Role & Tech Hiring Basics

    Before you screen a single engineer, understand what a Full-Stack & Cloud Tech Recruiter actually owns, how a requirement travels from client to candidate, and the vocabulary every hiring conversation uses.

    🧭 What does this recruiter do?

    A Full-Stack & Cloud Tech Recruiter finds, screens and presents software engineers who build the visible part of an app (screens, buttons), the hidden part (servers, business logic, databases) and the infrastructure that runs it (AWS, Azure, GCP). You are not expected to write code. You are expected to tell a real engineer from a keyword-stuffed resume, quickly and fairly.

    🔎
    Source
    Find people on LinkedIn, GitHub, job boards, referrals and your own talent database.
    🧪
    Screen
    Check skills, depth, communication, availability, rate and work authorization.
    📤
    Submit
    Send the hiring manager a short, evidence-based write-up, not just a resume.
    🤝
    Close
    Coordinate interviews, handle feedback, negotiate and get the offer accepted.
    💡
    Example. A client says: "We need a Senior Full-Stack Engineer: React, Node.js, AWS." Your job is to turn that sentence into a short list of 3–5 verified, interested, affordable candidates within the agreed turnaround time (TAT).
    Example: a day in the role
    Monday morning a client sends a req: Senior Full-Stack Engineer, React, Node.js, AWS. By lunch you have confirmed the must-haves. By evening you have 40 profiles. By Wednesday three verified candidates are submitted with short write-ups. That is the whole job in miniature.

    🛤️ The hiring pipeline

    Every requirement ("req") moves through the same stages. Most recruiters lose candidates at the screen and submittal stages, not at sourcing, so those are where you will spend most of this course.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    StageWhat happensTypical output
    IntakeMeet the hiring manager, clarify must-haves, budget, team, timeline.Written req summary
    SourcingSearch, message and re-engage candidates.Long list (30–60 profiles)
    ScreeningResume check, 15–20 minute call, light technical questions.Shortlist (3–5 people)
    SubmittalPresent candidates with evidence, rate and availability.Submission email or ATS note
    Interview & offerSchedule rounds, collect feedback, negotiate.Offer accepted or req closed
    Example: one req, start to finish
    Req #2041 (Senior Full-Stack) arrives Monday 9:00. Intake call at 11:00. By evening you have sourced 40 profiles. Tuesday: 8 recruiter calls. Wednesday noon: 4 candidates submitted with write-ups. TAT = 2 days, inside the client's 48-hour promise.

    📚 Key vocabulary

    You will hear these words every day. Learn them first and the rest of the course gets much easier.

    TermMeaningExample
    JD / ReqJob description / open requirement."Req #2041: Cloud Engineer, 6 months."
    ATSApplicant Tracking System where candidates and notes live.Greenhouse, Lever, Bullhorn.
    SubmittalSending a candidate to the client."I submitted 4 candidates today."
    TATTurnaround time from receiving a req to first submittal."Client expects TAT of 48 hours."
    W2 / C2C / 1099Employment models: employee / company-to-company / independent contractor."Open to C2C only."
    BenchConsultants employed by a staffing firm, waiting for a project."We have two Go engineers on bench."
    Notice periodTime before a candidate can start after resigning."30 days, negotiable to 15."
    StackThe set of technologies a product uses."React + Node.js + PostgreSQL on AWS."
    Example: decoding a real message
    "Client needs a Go engineer on C2C, notice under 30 days. I have one on bench at the right rate."
    Decoded: the client wants a Go developer, paid through his or her own company (C2C), who can start within a month (notice period), and your firm already employs one who is between projects (bench).

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Client side

    • Hiring manager: decides who joins the team.
    • Tech lead / interviewer: judges technical depth.
    • HR / procurement: approves rates, contracts and compliance.

    🧑‍💻 Candidate side

    • Active candidate: applying right now.
    • Passive candidate: employed, open only for the right role.
    • Referral: introduced by someone you trust.
    📝
    Tip. Build a friendly relationship with the tech lead, not only the hiring manager. A 10-minute weekly chat about what went wrong in the last interview improves your next submittals more than any tool.
    Example: a useful tech-lead chat
    The tech lead says: "The last candidate couldn't explain how they handle failed payments." You add "How do you handle failures and retries?" to every screening call. Your next three submittals each get an interview.

    📡 Signal, not syntax

    You do not need to know how to write a React hook. You need to hear whether the candidate does. Signal is evidence that someone has really done the work: specifics, numbers, trade-offs, and honest limits.

    🔴 Low signal

    "I worked on a cloud application using AWS and React with a team."

    🟢 High signal

    "I built the checkout UI in React and a Node.js API on AWS Lambda. We cut page load from 4s to 1.5s and handled 2,000 orders an hour on Black Friday."

    ✅
    Rule of thumb: ask what, how, how much, and what went wrong. Real engineers answer all four without hesitating.
    Example: the four questions in action
    Resume: "Worked with microservices."
    What? The order service. How? Spring Boot plus Kafka. How much? About 500 orders a minute. What went wrong? Duplicate messages, fixed with idempotency keys. That is a candidate who has actually done it.

    Module 02 · Technology Literacy

    Full-Stack Fundamentals

    A plain-English map of how a web application is built, so every skill on a resume has a place to live in your head.

    🍽️ What is "full-stack"?

    Think of an app as a restaurant. A full-stack engineer can work in every part of it.

    🪑
    Frontend = dining room
    What the customer sees and touches: pages, buttons, forms. Built with HTML, CSS, JavaScript, React.
    👨‍🍳
    Backend = kitchen
    Where orders are processed: business rules, APIs, security. Built with Node.js, Java, Go.
    🧺
    Database = pantry
    Where ingredients (data) are stored: PostgreSQL, MySQL, MongoDB.
    🏗️
    Cloud = the building
    Power, water and rent: servers and services on AWS, Azure or GCP.
    💡
    Example. On an e-commerce site, the product page you see is the frontend. Clicking "Add to cart" calls the backend, which saves the cart in the database, all running on cloud servers.
    Example: mapping a job ad to the restaurant
    A job ad says: build the checkout page in React, the order API in Node.js, store orders in PostgreSQL, run it on AWS. That is dining room (React), kitchen (Node.js), pantry (PostgreSQL) and building (AWS). A candidate who has worked on three or four of these is genuinely full-stack.

    🧱 The layers of a stack

    LayerJobResume keywords
    FrontendUser interface in the browserReact, Angular, Vue, TypeScript, HTML/CSS, Next.js
    BackendLogic, APIs, authenticationNode.js, Express, Spring Boot, Go, REST, GraphQL, gRPC
    DataStore and query informationPostgreSQL, MySQL, MongoDB, Redis, DynamoDB
    InfrastructureRun, scale and secure the appAWS, Azure, GCP, Docker, Kubernetes, Terraform
    DeliveryTest and ship code automaticallyCI/CD, GitHub Actions, Jenkins, unit tests
    Example: mapping a resume to layers
    React, Spring Boot, PostgreSQL, Docker, GitHub Actions covers Frontend, Backend, Data, Infrastructure and Delivery: a genuine full-stack profile. Compare with HTML, CSS, jQuery, which is frontend only, so it does not fit a full-stack req.

    🔁 How a request flows

    When a customer clicks Buy Now, this chain fires in under a second. Knowing it helps you ask smart questions like "Which part did you own?"

    Read left to right.
    example1. Customer clicks "Buy Now"           -> React sends POST /orders
2. API checks the user is logged in    -> Node.js / Spring Boot / Go
3. API saves the order                 -> PostgreSQL
4. API replies "order #8841 confirmed" -> React shows a success page
(all of this runs on servers in AWS / Azure / GCP)
    Example: one question that tests a full-stack claim
    A candidate says "I'm full-stack." You ask: "In the Buy Now flow, which steps did you build yourself?" A real full-stack engineer names the button and page, the API call and the order table. Someone who only built the button will quickly run out of detail.

    🧩 Popular stacks

    Companies rarely say "full-stack" alone. They name a stack. Recognising the pattern helps you match candidates fast.

    StackPartsTypical company
    MERNMongoDB, Express, React, Node.jsStartups, product teams
    Java + ReactSpring Boot, PostgreSQL/Oracle, React or AngularBanks, insurance, large enterprises
    Go microservicesGo, gRPC, Kubernetes, PostgreSQLFintech, infrastructure, high-traffic platforms
    .NET + AzureC#, ASP.NET, Azure SQL, Azure DevOpsMicrosoft-centric enterprises
    Example: reading a JD for the stack
    "Spring Boot, Oracle, Angular, at a bank" is a Java enterprise stack: source from banks and insurers. "React, Express, MongoDB, early-stage startup" is MERN: source from startups and product companies.

    🪜 Titles & seniority

    Titles vary by company, so judge by scope, not label.

    LevelRough experienceWhat they can do alone
    Junior0–2 yearsFix bugs and build small features with guidance
    Mid-level2–5 yearsOwn a feature end to end
    Senior5–9 yearsDesign systems, mentor others, make trade-offs
    Lead / Staff8+ yearsSet technical direction across teams
    ⚠️
    Years of experience are a hint, not proof. Someone with 8 years of repeating the same task is often less senior than someone with 4 years of varied ownership.
    Example: judging by scope
    A JD says Senior but the candidate's examples are all "fixed bugs under guidance": mismatch. Another candidate with 4 years led a payments migration and mentors two juniors: senior-level scope despite fewer years.

    🪤 Common beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "Java and JavaScript are the same."
    • "Full-stack means expert in everything."
    • "More keywords means better candidate."

    ✅ Reality

    • They are different languages with different jobs. Java is mainly backend; JavaScript runs in browsers and, through Node.js, on servers.
    • Most are strong on one side and capable on the other.
    • Depth in the required skills beats breadth.
    Example: avoiding the Java trap
    A candidate says "10 years of Java" and the JD is a React and Node.js role. Wrong conclusion: "great, 10 years!". Right move: "Java is different from JavaScript. Have you used Node.js or React?"

    Module 03 · Cloud

    Cloud Basics: AWS, Azure & GCP

    Understand what "the cloud" is, how the three big providers map to each other, and what each certification really tells you.

    ☁️ What is the cloud?

    The cloud means renting computing power over the internet instead of buying and housing your own servers. You pay for what you use and can scale up in minutes.

    Cloud comes in three service levels. A pizza analogy makes them easy to remember:

    ModelYou managePizza analogyExample
    IaaSApps, data, operating systemBuy ingredients, bake at homeAWS EC2, Azure VMs
    PaaSOnly your app and dataTakeaway: you add drinksElastic Beanstalk, Azure App Service
    SaaSJust use itDine at the restaurantGmail, Salesforce
    ServerlessOnly code snippetsPay per slice eatenAWS Lambda, Azure Functions
    Example: one website, four ways
    A startup hosts its site on EC2 (IaaS: it manages the OS), on Elastic Beanstalk (PaaS: it manages only the app), as Lambda functions (serverless: pay per request), or uses Gmail (SaaS: no management at all).

    🔄 AWS vs Azure vs GCP

    The three providers sell almost the same things under different names. A candidate who knows one can learn another quickly, so concepts matter more than brand names.

    CapabilityAWSAzureGCP
    Virtual serversEC2Virtual MachinesCompute Engine
    Serverless functionsLambdaFunctionsCloud Functions / Cloud Run
    Object storageS3Blob StorageCloud Storage
    Managed SQLRDS / AuroraAzure SQLCloud SQL / Spanner
    NoSQLDynamoDBCosmos DBFirestore / Bigtable
    KubernetesEKSAKSGKE
    Identity & accessIAMEntra ID + RBACCloud IAM
    Private networkVPCVNetVPC
    Infrastructure as codeCloudFormation / CDKBicep / ARMDeployment Manager
    💡
    Example. A JD asks for "S3 experience" and a candidate has "Azure Blob Storage for 4 years". Same concept, different brand. Worth a conversation.
    Example: translating a JD between clouds
    A JD asks for S3, Lambda and EKS. The candidate has used Blob Storage, Functions and AKS. These are the same three jobs (file storage, serverless code, managed Kubernetes) under different brand names. Ask the hiring manager whether cross-cloud experience is acceptable before rejecting.

    🏢 Who uses which

    🟠
    AWS
    Largest and broadest. Startups to enterprises. Biggest talent pool.
    🔵
    Azure
    Strong in enterprises already using Microsoft (Windows, Office, .NET).
    🟢
    GCP
    Known for data, analytics, AI/ML and Kubernetes. Smaller talent pool.
    🟣
    Multi-cloud
    Many large firms use two or more. Valuable but harder to find.
    Example: matching company to cloud
    A bank on Microsoft 365 and .NET most likely uses Azure. A media startup analysing 50 TB of data may pick GCP (BigQuery). A retailer with Black Friday spikes often runs on AWS with autoscaling.

    🎓 Certification ladder

    Certifications show structured learning. They do not prove hands-on skill, but they are a useful first filter, especially at the professional level.

    LevelAWSAzureGCP
    FoundationalCloud PractitionerAZ-900Cloud Digital Leader
    AssociateSolutions Architect, Developer, SysOpsAZ-104, AZ-204Associate Cloud Engineer
    Professional / ExpertSolutions Architect Professional, DevOps ProfessionalAZ-305 (Solutions Architect Expert), AZ-400Professional Cloud Architect

    ⚠️
    Always check: cert expiry dates and credential IDs, since many certifications expire and must be renewed. Rules change, so confirm current details on the provider's own certification page.
    Example: certification plus proof
    "AWS Solutions Architect Professional, built a multi-account landing zone for 40 teams" = certification with evidence. "AZ-900 only, Azure expert for 6 years" = foundational cert only; ask for hands-on projects.

    🏷️ Cloud role titles

    TitleFocusTypical question
    Cloud EngineerBuilds and runs cloud resources"How do you deploy a new service?"
    DevOps EngineerPipelines, automation, releases"Describe your CI/CD pipeline."
    SREReliability, monitoring, incidents"Tell me about an outage you resolved."
    Solutions / Cloud ArchitectDesigns the overall system"Why this design and not another?"
    Example: same cloud, different job
    For a Cloud Engineer ask: "How do you deploy a new service?" For an SRE ask: "Tell me about the last outage you resolved." Different titles, different proof.

    Module 04 · Screening Skills

    Screening Node.js, React, Java & Go

    What each technology is, where it is used, the vocabulary to listen for, and the questions that separate hands-on engineers from résumé decorators.

    📶 The depth ladder

    Every skill on a resume sits on one of three rungs. Your goal is to find which one.

    RungMeaningSounds like
    ListedAppears in a skills list only"Skills: React, Node, AWS, Docker…"
    UsedWorked with it on a project"Built forms in React using hooks."
    OwnedDesigned, led or fixed hard problems"Migrated 60 class components to hooks and cut bundle size by 30%."
    💡
    Probe question for any skill: "Tell me about the hardest problem you solved with it." Listed skills collapse here; owned skills come alive.
    Example: placing three resume lines on the ladder
    Skills: Go → Listed
    Wrote a Go service that processes uploads → Used
    Redesigned Go pipeline, 3x throughput, mentored 2 developers → Owned

    🟩 Node.js (backend JavaScript)

    Node.js lets developers run JavaScript on a server. It is popular for APIs and real-time apps (chat, notifications, streaming) because it handles many connections at once without blocking.

    ExpressNestJSnpmasync/awaitevent loopRESTTypeScriptJest
    Screening questionStrong answer containsWeak answer
    "How did you structure your last Node API?"Routes/controllers/services, validation, error handling, auth (JWT/OAuth)"I wrote some endpoints."
    "What does async/await solve?"Readable asynchronous code; avoids callback nesting; handles non-blocking I/O"It makes code faster."
    "How do you handle a slow database query?"Indexes, caching (Redis), pagination, profiling"Restart the server."
    ✅
    Senior signal: mentions testing, logging, rate limiting, TypeScript, and how they scaled beyond one server.
    Example: a high-depth Node.js answer
    "We used Express with JWT auth, Joi for validation and a central error handler. Jest tests ran in CI. We moved from one EC2 instance to ECS behind a load balancer when traffic tripled." Structure, security, testing and scaling: this is an owned skill.

    ⚛️ React (frontend)

    React is a JavaScript library for building user interfaces from small reusable pieces called components. Almost every modern product company has React somewhere.

    HooksuseState / useEffectReduxContext APINext.jsReact QueryTesting Library
    what a component looks like (you don't need to write this)function Greeting({ name }) {
  return <h1>Hello, {name}!</h1>;
}
// Used on a page as: <Greeting name="Asha" />
    LevelWhat you hear
    BeginnerComponents, props, simple state, forms
    IntermediateHooks, Redux or Context, API calls, routing, unit tests
    AdvancedPerformance tuning, server-side rendering with Next.js, design systems, accessibility, micro-frontends
    📝
    Example question: "A page feels slow. What would you check first?" Good answers mention unnecessary re-renders, large bundles, image sizes, and measuring before guessing.
    Example: probing a React claim
    Candidate: "We used Redux." You ask: "Why Redux and not Context?" Strong reply: "State was shared by 30+ components and we needed time-travel debugging." Weak reply: "It was the team's choice."

    ☕ Java (enterprise backend)

    Java powers banks, insurers, e-commerce and large enterprise systems. Most modern Java backends use Spring Boot.

    Spring BootHibernate / JPAMaven / GradleMicroservicesJUnitKafkaJVM

    🟢 Good signals

    • Java 11, 17 or 21 (modern versions)
    • Built REST microservices and handled transactions
    • Writes unit and integration tests

    🔴 Watch outs

    • Only Java 6/7/8 for many years
    • "Struts" or "EJB" only, with no Spring
    • No mention of testing or deployment
    Example: checking a Java timeline
    Resume: Java 8, Struts 1.x, 2012–2022. Ask: "Have you used Spring Boot or Java 17?" If they answer "I migrated a Struts app to Spring Boot last year", that is a positive sign. If they answer no, label it legacy-only and tell the client.

    🐹 Go (cloud-native backend)

    Go (Golang) is fast, simple and built for concurrency. It is widely used for cloud tooling and high-traffic services (Docker and Kubernetes are written in Go). Good Go engineers are scarcer, so expect higher rates and longer sourcing time.

    goroutineschannelsgRPCGin / EchoProtobufKubernetesconcurrency
    💡
    Sourcing tip. Engineers from Java, C++ or Python backgrounds who have shipped Go in production are good targets when pure Go talent is rare. Confirm real production use, not a weekend tutorial.
    Example: a Go sourcing win
    A Python backend engineer at a fintech rewrote the log pipeline in Go and now handles 50,000 events a second in production. Not a "Go developer" by title, but a strong, real candidate for a Go req.

    🔀 Equivalent & transferable skills

    Strict keyword matching hides good candidates. These pairs transfer well.

    If the JD asks forAlso considerWhy
    ReactVue, Angular (with some React)Same component thinking
    Node.jsPython (FastAPI), Go, JavaSame API concepts, different syntax
    Java + SpringKotlin + Spring, C# + .NETSame architecture patterns
    AWSAzure or GCPServices map one to one (see Module 03)
    ⚠️
    Always ask the hiring manager first: "Is a strong Vue developer acceptable for React?" Their answer defines your search, not your guess.
    Example: using equivalents
    JD: React + AWS. Candidate: Vue for 5 years, Azure for 4, led a Vue 2 → Vue 3 migration. Ask the manager: "Would you take this profile with a two-week ramp-up?" A "yes" widens your pool; a "no" saves a wasted submittal.

    Module 05 · GitHub Review

    Reviewing GitHub Profiles

    GitHub is a public portfolio of how an engineer thinks and works. Learn a fast, fair routine for reading it without being a developer.

    🐙 Why review GitHub?

    GitHub is where developers store and share code. A profile can show real projects, consistency, collaboration and communication, none of which a resume can prove.

    ⚠️
    Important: an empty GitHub does not mean a weak engineer. Most professional work is private. Use GitHub as extra evidence, never as a pass/fail test.

    Key words to know:

    TermMeaning
    Repository (repo)A project folder with its full history
    CommitA saved change, with a message describing it
    Pull request (PR)A proposal to merge changes, reviewed by others
    ForkA personal copy of someone else's repo
    StarA bookmark that signals popularity
    READMEThe front page explaining what the project does
    Example: no public GitHub
    Priya's repos are private because her employer's code is confidential. Instead of rejecting her, ask for a 15-minute project walkthrough on screen share.

    🔍 What to look at

    AreaWhereWhat it tells you
    Pinned reposTop of profileWhat they are proud of
    README qualityInside a repoCommunication: setup steps, screenshots, purpose
    Commit messagesCommits tabDiscipline: "Fix login timeout bug" beats "update"
    Languages barRight side of repoDoes it match the claimed stack?
    Tests & CIFolders named test, .github/workflowsProfessional habits
    PRs & issuesPull requests tabCollaboration and code review skills
    Open-source contributionsActivity overviewWorks with others, merged into real projects
    Example: commit messages compared
    update · fix · final · final2 (40 times) shows little discipline. Add retry with exponential backoff for payment webhook shows clear thinking and communication.

    🚦 Green & red flags

    🟢 Green flags

    • Clear README with run instructions
    • Meaningful commit messages over time
    • Tests and a CI pipeline
    • Original projects that solve a real problem
    • Reviews or comments on others' PRs

    🔴 Red flags

    • Only forked repos with no changes
    • Everything uploaded in one commit
    • Tutorial clones (todo app, weather app) only
    • Code copied with someone else's name still inside
    • Language mix does not match the resume
    Example: language mismatch
    Resume says Node.js, but the repo language bar shows 95% Python. Ask neutrally: "Your public work is mostly Python. Where did you use Node.js?" There may be a good answer: it was in private work.

    ⏱️ The 5-minute review

    1. Minute 1: Open the profile. Read the bio and pinned repos. Does it match the resume?
    2. Minute 2: Open the best repo. Read the README. Can you understand what it does?
    3. Minute 3: Click Commits. Is there a steady history with sensible messages?
    4. Minute 4: Check for tests, a CI folder, and the language bar.
    5. Minute 5: Note one or two questions to ask on the call, such as "Tell me about this project and why you built it."
    💡
    Your goal is not to judge code quality. It is to collect conversation starters and confirm the work is theirs. If still unsure, ask an engineer to review one repo for 10 minutes.
    Example: notes you carry into the call
    After five minutes you write: "Why PostgreSQL over MongoDB in shop-api?" and "How did you test the rate limiter?" You now start the call with two specific, informed questions.

    ⚖️ Pitfalls & ethics

    • Green squares are not skill. A busy contribution graph can come from scripts, tiny edits, or long hours at a job that uses private repos.
    • Stars can be bought. Look at the content, not the count.
    • Bias risk. Judging candidates by free-time coding favours people with spare time. Consider caregivers and people with demanding jobs.
    • Respect privacy. Use publicly shared contact details, be transparent about where you found them, and never scrape or misuse personal data. Follow your company's policy and local data protection rules.
    Example: a fair alternative
    A strong candidate works at a bank with all code private and has an empty contribution graph. Offer a project walkthrough or a short, paid or time-boxed exercise rather than judging free-time coding.

    🔬 Worked example

    profile snapshotCandidate: Priya (resume: Senior Full-Stack, React + Node + AWS)

Pinned repo "shop-api":  Node.js + Express, Dockerfile, GitHub Actions
README:                  explains setup, endpoints, architecture diagram
Commits:                 140 commits over 8 months, e.g. "Add rate limiting to /login"
Tests folder:            present (Jest)
PRs:                     12 reviewed in an open-source library
    ✅
    Assessment: strong match. Languages and tools agree with her resume, and she shows documentation, testing and collaboration habits. Call question: "Why did you add rate limiting, and how did you test it?"
    Example: when the GitHub profile is empty
    Candidate B has no public repositories because all of her work is for a bank. You do not reject her. You ask for a 15-minute walkthrough of one private project (what it did, her part, what broke) and score her with the same scorecard as everyone else.

    Module 06 · Architect Vetting

    Vetting Cloud Architects

    Architects are expensive and easy to fake. Learn what real architecture experience sounds like and how to test for it without being an architect yourself.

    📐 Architect vs engineer

    🛠️ Cloud engineer

    Builds what was designed. Writes scripts, deploys services, fixes problems. Answers "How?"

    📐 Cloud architect

    Decides what should be built and why. Balances cost, risk, security and speed. Answers "What and why?"

    💡
    Analogy. An engineer lays the bricks. An architect decides there should be a bridge, where it goes and what happens in a flood.
    Example: spot the role
    "Plan how our 20 applications move to Azure" → architect task (decisions). "Write the Terraform for the network that was designed" → engineer task (implementation).

    🏛️ Six design pillars

    The "Well-Architected" idea, used by AWS and mirrored by Azure and GCP, gives architects a shared checklist. A real architect refers to these naturally.

    ⚙️
    Operational excellence
    Monitoring, automation, learning from incidents.
    🔒
    Security
    Least privilege, encryption, network isolation.
    🛡️
    Reliability
    Failover, backups, disaster recovery.
    ⚡
    Performance
    Right sizing, caching, scaling.
    💰
    Cost optimisation
    Pay only for what is needed.
    🌱
    Sustainability
    Efficient use of resources.
    Example: pillars in a candidate's words
    Security: "least-privilege IAM, KMS-encrypted S3, private subnets." Cost: "switched dev environments off nights and weekends, saving 35%." Reliability: "multi-AZ database with a tested failover."

    ✅ Vetting checklist

    TopicListen forWhy it matters
    ScaleNumbers: users, requests per second, data sizeReal systems have numbers
    Trade-offs"We chose X over Y because…"Architecture is choosing under constraints
    MigrationLift-and-shift vs re-platform vs re-architectMost architect work is migrating legacy systems
    ResilienceRTO and RPO, multi-region, backupsShows planning for failure
    SecurityIAM roles, encryption, compliance (SOC 2, HIPAA, PCI)Regulated clients require it
    CostSavings achieved, reserved/spot usage, FinOpsCost control is a core architect duty
    CommunicationCan explain a design to non-engineersArchitects present to executives
    📝
    RTO / RPO in one line. RTO = how long the system can be down. RPO = how much recent data you can afford to lose. "RTO 1 hour, RPO 5 minutes" means back online within an hour, losing at most five minutes of data.
    Example: turning a vague claim into numbers
    Candidate: "I reduced cloud cost." You ask: "By how much and how?" Answer: "From $42k to $27k a month using rightsizing and savings plans." Now it is a verified claim.

    💬 Questions & what good answers sound like

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "Describe the biggest system you designed."Business goal, components, scale numbers, what they would changeA list of AWS service names
    "Why did you pick that database?"Access patterns, consistency needs, cost, team skills"It's the standard."
    "How would you migrate an on-prem app to the cloud?"Assess, pilot, migrate in phases, rollback plan, cutover"Move it all over a weekend."
    "How do you design for a region outage?"Multi-AZ vs multi-region, health checks, DNS failover, tested drills"The cloud never goes down."
    "How did you reduce cloud cost?"Rightsizing, autoscaling, savings plans, deleting idle resources, % saved"I turned things off."
    Example: a strong decision answer
    "Why DynamoDB?" → "Access was by key, traffic spikes were unpredictable and the team was small. We gave up ad-hoc joins and used Athena for reports." Reasons, trade-off and consequence: architect-level.

    🚩 Red flags

    • Title says "Architect" but all examples are tickets they implemented.
    • Lists 20+ cloud services with no project context.
    • Cannot explain a trade-off or a decision they regret.
    • Cert dates and project dates do not line up (certified in 2024 for "10 years of experience" in a service launched later).
    • Avoids numbers: no users, no budget, no uptime, no savings.
    Example: a timeline red flag
    Resume: "Running Kubernetes on Amazon EKS since 2013." EKS became generally available in 2018, so the claim is impossible as written. Ask politely: "Which Kubernetes setup did you use before EKS?" before deciding.

    🔬 Worked example

    Read the resume line, then the recruiter's follow-up and the evaluation.

    resume line"Designed cloud-native architecture on AWS for a retail platform."

    Follow-up: "How many customers did it serve, and what was the hardest decision?"

    🟢 Candidate A

    "About 1.2 million monthly users. We debated DynamoDB versus Aurora. We chose Aurora because reporting needed complex joins, then added ElastiCache to protect it during sales. Peak was 3,000 requests a second."

    Verdict: numbers, trade-off, rationale. Advance.

    🔴 Candidate B

    "It was a large platform with many users. We used microservices, Lambda, S3, RDS, and API Gateway for scalability."

    Verdict: services only, no scale, no decision. Probe deeper before submitting.

    Example: how you would write up Candidate A
    "Designed a retail platform for 1.2 million monthly users. Chose Aurora over DynamoDB because reporting needed joins, and added a cache for sale peaks. Handled 3,000 requests a second." Write-up note: strong architect evidence; confirm multi-region failover design on the technical call.

    Module 07 · Technical Screening Workflows

    Technical Screening Workflows

    Repeatable steps that turn a vague requirement into a short, defensible shortlist, with templates you can copy today.

    🗺️ End-to-end workflow

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    Each stage has an exit rule: a clear yes or no before the candidate moves forward. This keeps quality high and saves the hiring manager's time.

    Example: exit rules
    Resume score below 12/18 → stop. Call confirms all must-haves → move to tech check. Tech check shows "owned" depth on two core skills → write up and submit. Clear rules mean you can explain every decision.

    📞 The intake call

    The best screening starts before sourcing. Ask the hiring manager these questions:

    1. What will this person build in the first 90 days?
    2. Which 3 skills are must-have, and which are nice-to-have?
    3. What years of experience, or what scope, do you really need?
    4. Would you accept equivalent skills (Vue for React, Azure for AWS)?
    5. What is the budget or rate range, and is the role remote or onsite?
    6. What does the interview process look like, and how fast do you decide?
    7. Why did the last candidate fail?

    Example: turning a messy JD into a clear brief.

    JD saysBrief you write
    "Rockstar full-stack ninja with 10+ years, React, Angular, Vue, Node, Java, Go, AWS, Azure, GCP."Must-have: React, Node.js, AWS, 5+ years. Nice: TypeScript, Terraform. Flexible: Vue for React, 4 years if strong.
    Example: the question that saves weeks
    You ask the manager, "Why did the last candidate fail?" The answer: "He couldn't explain how he handled failures in production." You add "Tell me about a failure in production" to every screening call. Your next three submittals all get interviews.

    🔎 Boolean sourcing

    Boolean search combines keywords with AND, OR, NOT and quotes to find exact profiles.

    linkedin / job board("full stack" OR "full-stack" OR "fullstack")

AND ("Node.js" OR NodeJS OR Express)
AND (AWS OR "Amazon Web Services")
NOT (intern OR trainee OR recruiter)
    go + kubernetes, cloud architect search(Golang OR "Go developer") AND (Kubernetes OR K8s) AND (gRPC OR microservices)

("solutions architect" OR "cloud architect")

AND ("AWS Certified Solutions Architect - Professional" OR "AZ-305")
    💡
    Start broad, then narrow. If a search returns 40,000 profiles, add a skill or location. If it returns 5, remove one AND term.
    Example: narrowing a search
    full stack AND React AND Node.js → 40,000 profiles. Add AWS → 9,000. Add Kubernetes → 3,200. Add the client's location → 180, a list you can actually review.

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Calibration, Fraud & Metrics

    What separates a good recruiter from a trusted talent partner: aligning with engineers, reading the market, catching fakes and proving your results with numbers.

    🎯 Calibrating with engineers

    Calibration means agreeing, with real examples, on what "good" looks like before you search at scale.

    1. Pick 3 profiles: one clear yes, one clear no, one maybe.
    2. Send them to the tech lead and ask for a 1-line verdict on each.
    3. In a 20-minute call, ask "What made the maybe a maybe?"
    4. Update your scorecard and repeat after the first 5 interviews.
    💡
    Example. The lead says the "maybe" lacked experience with high traffic. You add "has handled production load over 1,000 requests per second" to your screening questions, and rejected submittals drop.
    Example: calibration in numbers
    Before calibration only 2 of your 10 submittals got interviews (20%). The tech lead explains the "maybe" lacked high-traffic experience. You add that one question to your screen. The next 10 submittals produce 6 interviews (60%).

    📈 Market & compensation

    Pay depends on skill rarity, location, seniority and contract type. Never quote numbers from memory; use current data from your company's reports, job postings and recent placements.

    FactorPushes pay upPushes pay down
    Skill rarityGo, Kubernetes, multi-cloud architectureCommon, widely taught stacks
    SeniorityArchitect, staff, lead scopeJunior, task-level scope
    DomainFintech, healthcare complianceGeneric internal tools
    Contract typeC2C / 1099 (employer overhead is theirs)W2 (employer pays taxes, benefits)
    ⚠️
    Tell the client early when the budget cannot match the market. A 10-minute honest conversation on day one saves two weeks of wasted sourcing.
    Example: same skills, different pay structure
    A candidate asking a higher hourly rate on C2C may accept a lower hourly rate on W2 because the employer then covers taxes and benefits. Always compare total package, not one number (figures vary, so use current market data).

    🕵️ Fraud & verification

    Remote hiring has increased proxy interviews, fake profiles and AI-written resumes. A calm, consistent verification routine protects your client and your reputation.

    Warning signHow to verify
    Answers sound scripted or read aloudAsk an unexpected follow-up: "What broke in production last?"
    Camera off, or avoids showing the faceRequire video; compare with the profile photo
    Resume text identical to othersSearch a unique sentence; check for duplicates in your ATS
    LinkedIn is new, thin, or has no connectionsCheck account age, endorsements, employer match
    Different person appears in later roundsCompare faces and voices; use the same link and identity check each round
    Employment cannot be confirmedVerify through official company channels or a background-check provider
    📝
    Be fair. Nervous, accented or shy candidates are not fraudulent. Use the same checks for everyone and escalate only on evidence.
    Example: a fair verification step
    A candidate pauses four seconds before every answer and gives generic replies to "What broke in production last?" Do not accuse. Schedule a camera-on follow-up with screen sharing, and use the same step for every candidate.

    📊 Metrics that matter

    MetricFormulaExample
    Submittal-to-interviewInterviews ÷ submittals6 ÷ 10 = 60%
    Interview-to-offerOffers ÷ interviews2 ÷ 6 = 33%
    Offer acceptanceAccepted ÷ offers2 ÷ 2 = 100%
    Time to submit (TAT)First submittal date − req dateMon → Wed = 2 days
    Time to fillStart date − req date38 days
    💡
    Reading the numbers. A low submittal-to-interview ratio (say 15%) means you submit the wrong people: improve screening. A low acceptance rate means pay, process speed or candidate care needs work.
    Example: diagnosing a week
    You submitted 10 candidates; 3 got interviews: 30% versus your 50% target. Review your screening notes: the clients said "not enough Node depth". Add two Node probes to the call script and re-measure next week.

    📖 Full case study

    Req: Senior Full-Stack Engineer: React, Node.js, AWS. Budget fixed. Needed in 3 weeks.

    1. Intake: Must-have React, Node, AWS, 5+ years. Nice: TypeScript. Vue acceptable if strong. Remote US.
    2. Source: Boolean search gives 180 profiles; you shortlist 25 by resume score.
    3. Screen: 12 calls. 5 fail the "owned" test (listed skills only). 7 remain.
    4. GitHub / proof: 3 have strong public work; 2 offer a project walkthrough.
    5. Submit: 4 candidates with write-ups within 5 days.
    6. Result: 3 interviews, 1 offer, 1 acceptance in week 3.
    ✅
    Why it worked: clear brief, consistent scoring, evidence in the write-up, and quick feedback loops with the tech lead.
    Example: what you would say to the client
    "I have four candidates. Three have owned the React and Node.js work in production. The strongest has served 1.2 million users and cut AWS cost by 28%. I recommend interviewing her first, with the Go gap noted."
