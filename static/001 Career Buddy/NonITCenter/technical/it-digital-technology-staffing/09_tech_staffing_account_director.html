    The Role & Enterprise Account Basics

    Before you negotiate an MSA or answer a rate-card bid, understand what a Tech Staffing Account Director owns, how large enterprises buy contract talent, and the words every enterprise account conversation uses.

    🧭 What does an account director do?

    A Tech Staffing Account Director owns the relationship, revenue and profit of large enterprise clients. You win and grow accounts, negotiate the contracts and rates, and make sure your delivery team keeps the promises you make. You are the person the client's senior leaders know and trust.

    🏢
    Manage MSP and VMS programs
    Work inside enterprise systems such as Fieldglass and Beeline to win and keep business.
    📜
    Negotiate MSAs
    Agree the master terms that govern every assignment.
    💲
    Bid rate cards
    Price competitively while protecting margin.
    📈
    Scale relationships
    Grow from one team to many, and from one service to several.
    Example: a day in the role
    At 9 a.m. you review a client's supplier scorecard and see your fill rate dipped. At 11 a.m. you agree a recovery plan with your delivery lead. At 2 p.m. you negotiate payment terms with the client's procurement manager. At 4 p.m. you meet a new business unit head to explore a second team of contractors.

    🌐 The enterprise staffing ecosystem

    Large companies rarely buy contractors one by one. They run a program with a few key parties.

    Example: one req through the ecosystem
    A hiring manager enters a req in the VMS. The MSP checks it and releases it to tier-1 suppliers. Your firm submits two candidates through the VMS. The client interviews, and your contractor starts. Every step, from req to invoice, happens inside the program rules.

    A req breaches its SLA. The recruiter fixes the req, the delivery lead fixes the process, and the account director speaks to the client sponsor about the impact and the plan. Each role is needed, and the director protects the relationship while others fix the work.

    Module 02 · The Enterprise Staffing Landscape

    The Enterprise Staffing Landscape

    How large companies organise, buy and measure contingent talent, and how your firm wins and keeps a place in that system.

    🏛️ Why enterprises use MSP and VMS

    🎛️
    Control
    One process, one set of rules, one place to see all contractors.
    💰
    Cost savings
    Competition between suppliers and rate cards lower costs.
    🛡️
    Compliance
    Consistent worker-classification, insurance and background checks.
    📊
    Visibility
    Reports on spend, speed and quality across the company.
    Example: the problem the client is solving
    A bank had 60 staffing suppliers, no single view of contractor spend and several compliance problems. It created an MSP/VMS program with 12 suppliers. Spend became visible, rates were standardised and compliance incidents fell. Understanding this goal helps you position your firm as part of the solution.

    🧩 Program models

    ModelWhat it isImplication for you
    Full MSP with VMSMSP manages suppliers, reqs and the VMSYou work through the MSP and its rules
    VMS-only (client-managed)Client runs the program in software without an MSPMore direct client contact
    Direct sourcingClient builds its own talent pool and uses suppliers for gapsSuppliers handle harder or overflow reqs
    HybridMix of the above by categoryDifferent rules for different roles
    Example: reading the program
    A client says, "IT reqs go through the VMS to tier-1 suppliers, while executive searches go direct." You now know where to compete on speed and rate (IT reqs), and where relationships and quality matter most (executive searches).

    🏷️ Types of MSP

    TypeDescriptionWatch out for
    Vendor-neutral MSPDoes not supply workers itselfFewer conflicts of interest
    Supplier-associated MSPAlso a staffing supplier or linked to onePossible favouring of its own supply
    HybridMixed arrangementsAsk how reqs are released and measured
    💡
    Ask the MSP how req release rules and scorecards work, and whether its own supply competes with yours. Clear, documented rules are the best protection.
    Example: a conflict you can manage
    You learn that the MSP is owned by a large staffing firm. You ask for the release rules in writing, check whether the MSP's sister company receives reqs first, and keep your own scorecard evidence. If rules are fair, you compete confidently; if not, you raise it professionally with the client.

    🪜 Supplier tiers and req release

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    Waterfall (tiered)Tier-1 first, then tier-2 after a set time

    Example: why tier matters
    At tier-1 your firm sees reqs for 24 hours before tier-2 suppliers. If your first-submittal TAT is 6 hours, you often submit before competitors arrive. If you slip to tier-2, you only see what tier-1 suppliers could not fill, which is often harder and lower-margin.

    📉 Fees and rate compression

    The MSP and the VMS cost money, and in many programs the cost is funded from supplier margin as a percentage of spend. Fees vary by program; the figures below are illustrative.

    LayerTakesRemaining from a $100 bill rate
    Client pays-$100.00
    MSP fee (3%)$3.00$97.00
    Your costs: pay and burden$84.00$13.00

    Example: why fees change your pricing
    If you ignore the 3% fee when quoting, you plan on a 16% margin but actually earn 13%. On $1,000,000 of annual billings that is $30,000 less profit than planned. Always include the program fees in your rate calculation.

    🪤 Common beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "Only the hiring manager matters."
    • "Lowest rate wins every time."
    • "The VMS is just admin work."
    • "We can ignore the MSP."

    ✅ Reality

    • Procurement and the MSP often decide volume and tier.
    • Quality, speed and compliance also count on scorecards.
    • VMS data drives your scorecard and revenue.
    • The MSP controls your access to reqs.
    Example: a trap avoided
    A new account director only talks to hiring managers, who adore the contractors. At renewal, procurement uses scorecard data (late submittals and a rate breach) to move the firm to tier-2. Building the procurement and MSP relationships earlier would have prevented the surprise.

    MSP & VMS Management

    The VMS is where an enterprise account is won or lost, one req and one invoice at a time. Learn how it works, how to keep your scorecard strong and how to be a supplier the MSP wants to give more work to.

    📝
    VMS products, features and ownership change over time. This module explains the common ideas. Always follow the specific guides and rules of each client's program.

    🖥️ What a VMS does

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    FunctionWhat happens in the VMS
    RequisitionHiring manager creates the req with a rate cap and dates
    DistributionMSP releases it to suppliers by tier
    SubmittalsSuppliers upload candidates and rates
    OnboardingChecks, contracts, access
    Time and expenseContractors enter hours; managers approve
    Invoicing and reportsConsolidated invoices and spend analytics
    Example: one record, many steps
    A single contractor record in the VMS shows the req, your submittal date, the interview feedback, the start date, approved timesheets and invoices. Everything the client uses to judge you is already there, so your habits in the system become your reputation.

    🧰 The platforms

    Platform (examples)Notes
    SAP FieldglassWidely used by large enterprises for contingent workforce and services
    BeelineUsed by many programs; strong in workforce management
    Workday VNDLYContingent workforce platform from Workday
    Other platforms and MSP-owned toolsPrograms may use their own or others' systems
    💡
    Learn the concepts (requisition, submittal, worker record, timesheet, invoice) and you can adapt to any platform. Learn the client's rules too, because two clients on the same product may configure it differently.
    Example: same product, different rules
    Client A requires a resume plus a one-page fit summary on every Fieldglass submittal. Client B allows only the resume and a rate. Your team keeps a one-page "client rules" sheet for each program so no submittal is rejected for a formatting rule.

    🔄 Working a req in the VMS

    StepGood practice
    1. Alert receivedAssign an owner within the hour
    2. Read the req fullyRate cap, must-haves, location, dates, interview process
    3. Check capacity and fitBid on reqs you can fill; decline others quickly and politely
    4. SubmitComplete, accurate profile with rate within cap
    5. TrackUpdate status; chase interviews and feedback
    6. Close the loopRecord outcomes and reasons
    Example: declining well
    A req needs a rare mainframe skill that you cannot fill. You reply in the VMS within two hours, "We cannot support this req, but please keep us in mind for Java and cloud reqs." The MSP notes your honesty, and you keep your credibility and your tier.

    🎯 Submittal discipline

    DoAvoid
    Submit within the first hours of releaseWaiting until the end of the window
    Check the rate against the cap before submittingSubmitting over cap and hoping
    Get the candidate's right to represent for this reqSubmitting the same person through two suppliers
    Submit strong, qualified matches (3 to 4 per req)Dumping weak profiles
    Include a short fit noteResume only, no context
    Example: a rate rejection costs tier
    Your team submitted 12 candidates last month, and 3 were above the rate cap. Those 3 rate rejections reduced your rate-compliance score from 100% to 75%, and the MSP flagged you. A simple pre-submittal rate check would have kept the scorecard clean.

    🧾 Timesheets and invoicing

    Many disputes start with small errors. Accurate time and invoices keep cash flowing.

    ItemCheck
    TimesheetContractor submits on time; manager approves by the deadline
    RateMatches the approved rate in the VMS
    PO or SOW numberValid and not expired
    InvoiceMatches approved time, with the correct tax and fees
    Three-way matchPO or SOW, approved time and invoice agree
    Example: a late payment traced to a small error
    An invoice is rejected because the PO expired two days before the timesheet period ended. Payment slips 30 days. You now diary every PO expiry date 30 days ahead, ask the client to extend early, and avoid the cash-flow hit.

    🛡️ Onboarding and compliance

    • Background checks, right-to-work and any client-specific screening completed before the start date.
    • Insurance certificates and contracts signed and uploaded.
    • Worker classification consistent with the program rules (W2, C2C, or other).
    • Access and security training completed.
    • Documentation stored in the VMS and your own system.
    Example: a missed check
    A contractor starts on Monday but a required background check had not been uploaded. The client's security team blocks access, and the contractor loses two days. You add a pre-start checklist where nobody is scheduled until every document shows green in the VMS.

    🤝 Managing the MSP relationship

    HabitWhy it helps
    A named day-to-day contact at the MSPFast answers and early warnings
    Monthly scorecard reviewNo surprises
    Share market insight (rates, candidate availability)Positions you as a partner
    Flag problems honestly and earlyBuilds trust
    Respect the MSP's processNever bypass it to reach hiring managers behind its back
    Example: a partner, not a pest
    Before a rate card refresh the MSP asks suppliers for market intelligence. You send a short, honest summary on the Go and cloud security market, with candidate supply and rate trends. The MSP uses some of it, and mentions your firm as a helpful partner in the next supplier review.

    📈 Improving your tier and scorecard

    Weak measureLikely causeAction
    Fill rateBidding on reqs you cannot fillBe selective; build pipelines in the client's top skills
    Time to submitSlow assignment or sourcingAuto-assign and use a bench
    Submittal-to-interviewWeak screeningMust-have checklist and pre-submittal call
    Rate complianceNo pre-checkCap check before every submission
    SatisfactionPoor communicationWeekly updates; fast feedback
    Example: a scorecard turnaround
    Over six months a supplier raises its submittal-to-interview ratio from 30% to 48% and its rate compliance to 100%. The MSP moves it from tier-2 to tier-1, and monthly req volume rises by about 40%. Scorecard work pays back in revenue.

    🪤 Common pitfalls

    ❌ Pitfall

    • Submitting late and over cap
    • Ignoring the VMS reports
    • Letting POs and SOWs expire
    • Going around the MSP to the client

    ✅ Better

    • Weekly report review for early signals
    • Diary every expiry date
    • Working with the MSP and informing it
    Example: respecting the process
    You hear that a hiring manager needs a rare skill urgently. You tell the MSP program manager first and offer to submit through the VMS. The MSP releases it, you submit within the hour, and the manager is happy while the MSP sees you as a team player.

    Module 04 · MSA Negotiation

    MSA Negotiation

    The Master Services Agreement shapes your risk, cash flow and profit for years. Learn the clauses that matter, how to prepare, how to trade concessions and how to know when to say no.

    📝
    This module explains common commercial terms in plain English. It is not legal advice. Always have your legal team review real contracts and wording.

    📑 MSA, SOW and PO

    DocumentWhat it coversHow often
    MSA (Master Services Agreement)General terms: payment, liability, confidentiality, terminationOnce, then renewed
    SOW (Statement of Work)Scope, deliverables, price and timeline of one projectPer project
    PO (Purchase Order)Authorises spending against the contractPer assignment or period
    Rate scheduleAgreed rates, often an appendix to the MSAReviewed periodically
    Example: how they fit together
    An MSA is signed once with a bank. Each new project gets its own SOW (for example, "migrate 12 databases in 4 months"), and each contractor assignment gets a PO number. You negotiate the hardest points once, in the MSA, so every later deal is faster.

    🔑 The key clauses

    ClauseWhat it doesWhat to watch
    Rates and rate changesSets prices and how they can changeAnnual escalators; unilateral cuts
    Payment termsWhen you are paidNet 60 or 90; pay-when-paid with your contractors
    Limitation of liabilityCaps how much each side can oweNo cap, or a cap far above your insurance
    IndemnityOne party covers losses from specified claimsBroad, one-sided indemnities
    InsuranceRequired cover and limitsLimits you cannot buy or afford
    Non-solicit and conversionRestricts direct hiring; sets fees if convertedFee falling to zero too quickly
    TerminationHow either side can end the contractTermination for convenience with no notice
    SLAs and creditsService targets and penaltiesUncapped credits; unrealistic targets
    Audit rightsClient may inspect recordsFrequency and scope
    Subcontracting and flow-downTerms passed to your suppliersObligations you cannot pass on
    Example: a clause that changes the deal
    The draft says the client may reduce rates by up to 10% each year at its discretion. Over a $2,000,000 annual account that could remove $200,000 of revenue with no say from you. You negotiate it to "rate changes only by mutual agreement, with an annual escalator of an agreed percentage".

    📝 Preparing to negotiate

    1. List your priorities: must-haves, tradeable items and nice-to-haves.
    2. Know the client's priorities: speed, cost, risk, compliance.
    3. Check the numbers: margin floor, cost of terms, cash-flow impact.
    4. Define your walk-away point in advance.
    5. Agree roles: who leads, who handles legal, who decides.
    6. Plan the sequence: start with shared goals, then the hard points.
    Example: a one-page negotiation plan
    Must-have: payment within 45 days and a liability cap tied to fees. Tradeable: a small volume rebate, quarterly reviews. Nice: a rate escalator. Walk away: unlimited liability or net 90. Written before the meeting, this plan keeps you calm when pressure rises.

    ⚖️ Leverage and BATNA

    BATNA means Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement: what you do if no deal is reached. The stronger your BATNA, the more confident you can be.

    Source of leverageExample
    AlternativesYou have other clients and a full pipeline
    PerformanceTop scorecard results and strong client feedback
    Scarce skillsYou supply roles the client cannot fill elsewhere
    RelationshipsExecutive sponsors who value you
    Switching costReplacing you would disrupt running projects
    Example: leverage from results
    Your firm has the best scorecard in the program, and 60% of the client's hard-to-fill cloud roles come through you. When the client asks for longer payment terms, you can calmly explain that your service levels depend on healthy cash flow, and propose a compromise instead of accepting.

    🔁 Trading concessions

    You giveYou ask for
    A small volume-based rebateNet 45 payment instead of net 60
    Faster submittal targetsPause of the clock during client hold
    Flexibility on a conversion windowA standard fee schedule
    Quarterly performance reviewsAnnual rate escalator
    💡
    Never concede for free. Each give should come with an ask, framed as "If we can do X, then we'd need Y." Free concessions teach the other side that more pressure works.
    Example: if-then framing
    "If we agree to the 1% volume rebate above $3 million, we would ask to keep net 45 payment terms." The client sees a fair exchange, and you keep the term that matters most to your cash flow.

    💰 Putting a price on terms

    Every term has a cost. Calculate it, so you trade wisely.

    Item (illustrative)CalculationResult
    Monthly billings-$1,000,000
    Cost of capital-8% a year
    Extra 30 days of payment delay (net 30 to net 60)$1,000,000 × 8% × 30 ÷ 365about $6,575 a month
    Annual cost of that delay$6,575 × 12about $78,900
    Example: a number that wins the argument
    A client asks for net 60 instead of net 30. You show that the extra 30 days costs you about $78,900 a year in financing, then offer net 45, which costs about half of that, in exchange for a 12-month extension. Specific numbers make a fair compromise easier to find.

    🚧 Red lines

    Typical red lineWhyAlternative to propose
    Unlimited liabilityOne incident could threaten the whole firmA cap tied to annual fees or insurance limits
    Rate cuts at the client's sole discretionRemoves pricing controlChanges by mutual agreement
    Uncapped SLA creditsTurns misses into open-ended lossesCredits capped at a percentage of monthly fees
    Terms you cannot flow down to contractorsYou carry risk you cannot manageReasonable, back-to-back terms
    Payment terms beyond your funding capacityCash flow riskShorter terms, or invoice financing
    Example: holding a red line politely
    The draft demands unlimited liability. You reply, "We can't accept unlimited liability, as no insurer would cover it. We can accept a cap of twelve months of fees, which is standard for programs of this size." You state the reason, offer an alternative and stay calm.

    ⚖️ Working with legal

    You doLegal does
    Set business priorities and acceptable trade-offsDraft and review wording; flag legal risk
    Explain the commercial impact of each clauseExplain the legal effect of each clause
    Handle the relationship with the client's procurementHandle the redline process with the client's lawyers
    Keep a log of agreed changesMake sure the final text matches the agreement
    Example: aligning early
    Before the first meeting you spend an hour with your lawyer. She identifies the three clauses that cannot be changed without management approval. In the room, you do not agree to anything that crosses those lines, and no deal has to be reversed later.

    🤝 Negotiation conduct

    • Be calm and factual. Anger weakens your position.
    • Ask questions to understand the real concern behind a demand.
    • Summarise agreements in writing after each meeting.
    • Be honest. Never invent competing offers or facts.
    • Think long term. The relationship continues after the signature.
    Example: finding the real concern
    The client insists on net 90. You ask, "What is driving that?" The answer is a new finance policy for all suppliers. You offer net 60 with early-payment discounts they can choose to use, which fits their policy and protects your cash flow.

    🔬 Worked example

    three draft terms1. Net 90 payment terms
2. Unlimited liability
3. Client may cut rates by up to 10% a year at its discretion
    ✅
    A sensible response: (1) propose net 45, trade a volume rebate if needed; (2) hold the red line, propose a liability cap of twelve months of fees; (3) replace it with rate changes by mutual agreement and an agreed annual escalator. You explain each in terms of the client's goals and give a reason, not just a refusal.
    Example: summarising to the client
    "Thank you for today. To confirm: we agreed net 45 with a 1% rebate over $3 million, a liability cap of twelve months of fees, and rate changes by mutual agreement. We'll send revised wording through legal by Friday."

    Module 05 · Rate Card Bidding

    Rate Card Bidding

    Win the business without losing the profit. Learn how rate cards work, how to build a rate from your costs, when to bid and when to walk away, and how to win on more than price.

    📝
    All rates, burden and fee figures are illustrative, chosen to make the maths clear. Use your finance team's real numbers for real bids.

    📇 What a rate card is

    A rate card lists the maximum bill rate the client will pay for each role, level and sometimes location. In a bid, suppliers propose rates, often at or below the client's targets, and the client compares.

    RoleLevelProposed max bill rate (illustrative)
    Software engineerMid$78
    Software engineerSenior$98
    Cloud architectSenior$125
    QA automation engineerMid$68
    Example: a rate card in action
    A hiring manager wants a senior engineer. The VMS shows a cap of $98 an hour for that role. Every supplier must submit candidates at or under $98, so supplier success depends on pay rates that fit under the card while still leaving margin.

    🧮 Pricing methods

    MethodHow it worksPros and cons
    Fixed rate capsOne maximum bill rate per role and levelSimple; risks being too low for hard skills
    Markup over pay rateBill = pay rate × (1 + markup %)Fair for varied pay; client sees your margin
    Blended rateOne rate averaged across a mix of rolesEasy for the client; depends on the mix staying stable
    Fixed price (SOW)One price for a defined deliverableRewards efficiency; carries delivery risk
    Example: markup pricing
    Pay rate $60 and a 40% markup gives a bill rate of 60 × 1.40 = $84. Gross profit is $24, so gross margin is 24 ÷ 84 = 28.6%. Markup (40%) and margin (28.6%) are different numbers, so say which one you mean.

    🏗️ Building a rate from cost

    Start with what it costs you, add the program fee and your target margin, then work out the bill rate.

    Step (W2 example, illustrative)Value
    Pay rate$70.00
    Burden (20% of pay)$14.00
    Total cost$84.00
    MSP fee3% of the bill rate
    Target gross margin18% of the bill rate
    Cost as a share of the bill rate100% − 3% − 18% = 79%
    Bill rate$84 ÷ 0.79 = $106.33

    Check: MSP fee 3% × 106.33 = $3.19. Margin 18% × 106.33 = $19.14. Cost $84.00 + $3.19 + $19.14 = $106.33.

    Example: forgetting the fee
    If you ignore the MSP fee, you would price at 84 ÷ 0.82 = $102.44. After the 3% fee ($3.07) your real margin becomes (102.44 − 84 − 3.07) ÷ 102.44 = 15%, not 18%. A bid calculator that includes fees protects you from this slow leak.

    🛑 Margin floor and target

    TermMeaningExample
    Margin floorThe lowest margin you will accept12% gross margin
    Target marginThe margin you aim for18%
    Strategic exceptionBelow target, approved for a clear reasonEntry price to win a large account
    💡
    Write the floor down before the bid starts and agree who can approve exceptions. Without a floor, pressure from the client or a competitor pushes you toward unprofitable rates.
    Example: a floor that saves a bad deal
    A client's rate cap leaves you a 7% margin on cloud roles. Your floor is 12%, so you decline that line of the rate card and bid on the others. A year later, the competitor who accepted it is struggling to cover costs on those roles.

    ⚖️ Blended rates

    A blended rate is a weighted average across a mix of roles.

    RoleHeadcountBill rateWeighted
    Senior engineer10$95950
    Mid engineer20$751,500
    Total302,450
    Blended rate2,450 ÷ 30 = $81.67
    Example: when the mix shifts
    You quote a blended $81.67 assuming a one-third senior mix. If the client later asks for mostly seniors (20 senior and 10 mid), the true average cost to serve rises to (20 × 95 + 10 × 75) ÷ 30 = $88.33, while your blended price stays at $81.67. Put a mix assumption and a review clause in the bid.

    📈 Escalators and multipliers

    ItemWhat it isExample
    Annual escalatorPlanned yearly rate increase3% each contract anniversary
    Overtime multiplierHigher rate for hours beyond the standard week1.5 times for non-exempt W2 workers
    Holiday or shift premiumExtra for unsocial hours1.25 times for night shifts
    Location tiersDifferent rates by city or regionHigher rates in high-cost cities
    Example: why escalators matter
    Contractor pay tends to rise each year. A $100 rate with no escalator and 4% annual pay growth loses about 3 to 4 points of margin each year. A 3% escalator in the MSA keeps most of your margin intact, and the client knows what to expect.

    🚦 Bid or no-bid

    FactorWeightScore (1 to 5)Points
    Strategic value of the client30%51.50
    Margin at the proposed rates30%30.90
    Our ability to deliver the skills25%41.00
    Competition and win likelihood15%20.30
    Total100%3.70
    Example: a disciplined decision
    Your firm sets a rule: bid at 3.5 or above, discuss between 2.5 and 3.5, decline below 2.5. This bid scores 3.70, so you bid, but with a plan to protect margin on the roles where it scored lowest. A written rule stops emotional bidding.

    🏆 Winning beyond price

    LeverHow to show it
    SpeedYour TAT data and bench size
    QualitySubmittal-to-interview ratios and references
    ComplianceAudit results, background check process
    SpecialisationCase studies in hard-to-fill roles
    PartnershipMarket intelligence, dedicated account team
    DiversityCertified diverse supplier status or inclusive sourcing results
    Example: winning on proof
    You are 4% higher than the cheapest bidder, but you present a case study: 11-hour median first-submittal time, 52% interview ratio and a 90-day retention rate of 96%. The client's evaluation panel scores quality heavily, and you win the tier-1 position.

    ⚠️ Pricing risks

    RiskWhat happensProtection
    Winner's curseYou win by underpricing, then lose moneyMargin floor; sensitivity checks
    Mix shiftMore expensive roles than assumedMix assumption and review clause
    Cost inflationPay rises faster than ratesAnnual escalator
    Fee changesMSP or VMS fee increasesFee-change clause
    Rate compression through tiersEach layer takes a share, leaving less for contractorsUnderstand the full chain before bidding
    Example: a sensitivity check
    At 18% target margin the bid works. You test a case where pay rates rise 5% and the MSP fee goes to 4%. Margin falls to about 13%, only just above your 12% floor. You add an escalator clause to the bid so that this downside is covered.

    🔬 Worked example

    senior software engineer line (W2)Pay rate           $70.00
Burden (20%)       $14.00   -> cost $84.00
MSP fee            3% of bill rate
Target margin      18% of bill rate

Bill rate = 84 / (1 - 0.03 - 0.18) = 84 / 0.79 = $106.33
Client rate cap for this role: $98

Largest pay rate that works under the cap at 18% margin:
cost allowed = 98 x 0.79 = $77.42  ->  pay = 77.42 / 1.20 = $64.52
    ✅
    Reading: to hit your target at a $98 cap, your pay rate must be about $64.52, below the $70 the market is asking. You can (a) propose a higher cap to the client, (b) accept a lower margin that stays above your floor, or (c) decline that line. The numbers make the choice clear.
    Example: what you would say to the client
    "At a $98 cap, we cannot reach strong senior engineers, who are asking about $70 an hour. A cap near $106 would let us fill these roles within your time targets. We can also share our market data supporting that figure."

    Enterprise Relationship Scaling

    Growing an account is about trust at many levels. Learn how to map stakeholders, spread relationships beyond one contact, expand services responsibly and hold great quarterly reviews.

    🌱 From supplier to partner

    Supplier mindsetPartner mindset

    Reacts to problemsFlags risks early with a plan

    Example: anticipating a need
    You learn from a hiring manager that her team will launch a data platform in six months. You share a short plan on the roles, timeline and market rates, before any req is raised. When the budget is approved, she calls you first.

    🗺️ Stakeholder mapping

    StakeholderCares aboutYour approach

    ProcurementCost, compliance, contract termsScorecard data; transparent pricing

    HR, IT and securityOnboarding, access, riskClean compliance and fast onboarding
    Example: a relationship map
    You draw a map: 1 executive sponsor (met twice a year), 2 procurement contacts (monthly), 9 hiring managers (as needed) and 1 MSP manager (weekly). The map shows executive contact is thin, so you plan a short, value-led conversation in the next quarter.

    🧵 Multi-threading

    Single-threading means your whole account depends on one person. If she leaves or changes role, you may lose it. Multi-threading means building several healthy relationships.

    RiskSignFix
    One contact for everythingOnly one name in your emailsAdd contacts in procurement, other teams and leadership
    Champion changes jobsNew leader unfamiliar with youEarly introductions; share your scorecard history
    Relationships only at manager levelNo executive knows your workExecutive sponsor programme with value-led updates
    Example: surviving a departure
    Your only senior contact leaves for another company. Because you had also built relationships with two other directors and procurement, the new head of engineering already knows your team. The account continues without a break.

    📈 Land and expand

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    New servicesManaged teams and SOW projects
    New regionsFrom the US to Europe and India

    Example: a successful expansion
    You start with 3 Java contractors for one team. After 9 months of strong delivery you are asked to staff two other teams, then propose a managed QA team. A year in, you have 21 contractors across five teams and two service types.

    📊 The QBR

    💡

    Example: a QBR that changes decisions
    The QBR shows hard-to-fill cloud roles took 31 days against a client target of 21. You propose a talent pool for cloud roles and a faster approval step. The client agrees, and the next quarter's time to fill falls to 19 days.

    💎 Value narrative

    Value claimEvidence to show
    Faster fillsMedian first-submittal TAT and time to start vs program average
    Higher qualitySubmittal-to-interview ratio, extension rate, client ratings
    Lower riskZero compliance incidents; audit results

    PartnershipMarket insight and contributions to the program
    Example: evidence, not adjectives
    Instead of "we're very fast", you show: "Our median first submittal is 7 business hours against a program median of 19. Our contractors extend at 74%, versus 58% for the program." Numbers from the client's own VMS are the most persuasive evidence.

    🧮 Share of wallet

    Item (illustrative)Value
    Client's annual contingent spend$10,000,000
    Your annual billings$1,500,000
    Wallet share1.5 ÷ 10 = 15%
    Target wallet share in two years25%, or $2,500,000 at current spend

    Example: a plan from the gap
    To reach 25% you need $1,000,000 more per year. You identify three sources: tier-1 status for cloud reqs (about $400,000), a managed QA team ($350,000) and two new business units ($250,000). A target with named sources is a plan, not a hope.

    ⚖️ Ethics in enterprise accounts

    • Gifts and hospitality: follow both your company's and the client's policies; get approval; avoid anything that could look like influence.
    • Conflicts of interest: disclose relationships that could affect decisions.
    • Confidentiality: never share one client's information with another, or with competitors.
    • Fair competition: never agree prices or bids with competitors.
    • Honesty: never exaggerate performance or capability.
    📝
    Rules on gifts, bribery and competition law vary by country and company. This is a general guide, not legal advice; ask your compliance team when unsure.
    Example: the safe call
    A client's procurement manager mentions she loves a certain sports team. You do not offer tickets. Instead, you invite her to a program-wide supplier workshop that her own policy allows. The relationship stays warm without any risk to either of you.

    🏗️ Scaling your account team

    Account sizeTypical team
    Small ($0.5M to $1.5M)Account director (part-time), a delivery lead, 2 recruiters
    Medium ($1.5M to $5M)Account director, account manager, delivery lead, 4 to 6 recruiters, a sourcer
    Large ($5M and above)Account director, several account managers, delivery managers, pods of recruiters, onboarding and compliance support
    Example: staffing for growth
    An account grows from $1.2M to $3.4M in 18 months. You add an account manager for day-to-day contact, a second delivery lead and a sourcer. Without that support, speed and quality would have fallen with growth, and the scorecard would have slipped.

    Account Planning & Governance

    A good account director is also a good business manager. Learn to write an account plan, read an account P&L, manage cash, run a governance rhythm and plan renewals well before they arrive.

    📋 The account plan

    SectionContent
    SnapshotSpend, wallet share, margin, contract dates, tier
    Stakeholder mapWho matters and the relationship strength
    Client goalsWhat the client wants to achieve this year
    OpportunitiesTeams, skills, regions and services to expand into
    RisksConsolidation, scorecard gaps, single-threading, cash
    Actions and ownersSpecific steps with dates
    Example: a two-page plan that gets used
    A two-page plan for a bank account lists: wallet share 12%, MSA renewal in 9 months, risk of supplier reduction, three expansion targets and ten dated actions. The team reviews it monthly. Short plans that are reviewed beat long plans that are filed.

    🔮 Revenue and margin forecasting

    With 6 expected starts and 4 roll-offs, you forecast monthly revenue growing from $360,000 to $388,800. If two of the starts slip a month, the forecast falls by $28,800 for that month, so you track start dates weekly and update the forecast.

    🧾 Account P&L basics

    Line (monthly, illustrative)Amount
    Revenue (billings)$500,000
    Contractor pay and burden−$410,000
    MSP and VMS fees (3% of billings)−$15,000

    Example: seeing what really remains
    A $500,000 account looks large, but contributes only $30,000 after fees, costs and direct team. If a client demands a 3% rate cut ($15,000 a month), contribution halves to $15,000. That is why you understand the P&L before agreeing to any discount.

    💵 Cash and DSO

    DSO (days sales outstanding) measures how many days, on average, it takes to collect payment.

    Item (illustrative)Value
    Accounts receivable (unpaid invoices)$900,000
    Monthly billings$500,000
    Daily billings$500,000 ÷ 30 = $16,667
    DSO900,000 ÷ 16,667 = 54 days
    Contract payment termsNet 45
    Gap54 − 45 = 9 days late on average
    Example: acting on late payment
    The 9-day gap equals about $150,000 of cash tied up ($16,667 × 9). You trace it to invoices rejected for expired POs, set an alert for PO expiry, and DSO falls to 47 days within two months, with no change in contract terms.

    🎯 Incentives and alignment

    • Avoid incentives that encourage cutting corners on compliance.
    Example: fixing a misaligned bonus
    Sales is paid only on revenue, and agrees to a low-margin deal that delivery cannot staff well. The firm changes the bonus to a mix of revenue, margin and client scorecard score. Within a quarter, new deals are priced more carefully and delivery complaints fall.

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Pressure, Conflict & Judgement

    What separates a good account director from a trusted one: holding your ground on price, handling conflicts of interest, protecting scope, learning from losses and always acting with integrity.

    💸 Price pressure

    Almost every large client asks for a rate cut at some point. A good response is calm, informed and creative.

    StepWhat you do
    1. Understand the goalIs it a budget target, a policy, or a competitor quote?
    2. Check your numbersMargin floor, impact on contribution, cash effect
    3. Show your valueScorecard, savings, quality and risk evidence
    4. Offer a tradeVolume tiers, longer term, faster payment, simpler process
    5. Hold the floorDecline below your floor, politely, with reasons
    Example: a 10% cut request
    The client asks for 10% off all rates. On a $2,000,000 account that would remove $200,000 of revenue, while margin is about 15% ($300,000), so profit would fall by two-thirds. You propose 2% off for volumes above $2.5 million, a 24-month term and net 45 payment. The client accepts a version of this, and your floor holds.

    ⚔️ MSP conflicts of interest

    Warning signResponse
    MSP's sister firm sees reqs before youAsk the client to confirm release rules in writing
    Your submittals are rejected for unclear reasonsRequest specific feedback and keep a dated log
    Your scorecard seems inconsistent with the dataCompare your own numbers; request a data review
    The MSP discourages contact with hiring managersRespect the process but build executive and procurement relationships
    💡
    Raise concerns with facts, not accusations, and through the right channel: first the MSP, then the client's program owner. Keep a professional tone, because you may need to work with both for years.
    Example: a factual escalation
    You collect eight reqs where a sister company of the MSP was submitted first and yours arrived 20 hours later. You show the dated list to the client's program owner and ask, "Could you help us understand how reqs are released?" The client reviews its rules, and release timing becomes transparent.

    📐 Scope creep on SOWs

    SituationBest response
    Client asks for extra work not in the SOWAgree a change request with price and timeline before starting
    Requirements keep shiftingDocument each change; reset the plan and cost
    Verbal promises by a team memberConfirm in writing; correct the record respectfully
    Deliverables are unclearClarify acceptance criteria before the next phase
    Example: the cost of "just one more thing"
    A fixed-price SOW to migrate 12 databases grows to 15 as the client adds "just three more". Without a change request you absorb about 25% more work for the same price. With one, you agree an additional fee and timeline, and the client appreciates the clarity.

    📉 Losing a bid

    1. Thank the client and ask for a debrief.
    2. Learn the reasons: price, fit, evidence, relationships, risk.
    3. Be honest internally about what you could improve.
    4. Keep the relationship: stay in touch and offer help on hard skills.
    5. Apply the lessons to the next bid.
    Example: learning from a loss
    After losing a bid, the debrief shows your price was within 2% of the winner, but your response had no evidence on speed. You build a one-page performance summary for future bids. Six months later, a smaller RFP from the same client is awarded to you.

    🛡️ Competition law and ethics

    Do notWhy
    Discuss or "align" prices, rates or bids with competitorsCan breach competition law, even in casual conversation
    Agree to share out clients or territories with competitorsSame reason
    Share one client's confidential data with anotherBreach of trust and contract
    Offer gifts that could influence a decisionBribery and policy risk
    Exaggerate your capability to winDamages trust and may breach contract
    ⚠️
    If a competitor raises prices or bids with you, end the conversation clearly and report it to your legal or compliance team. Rules vary by country. This is general guidance, not legal advice.
    Example: stopping a risky conversation
    At an industry event a contact from a rival firm says, "We should agree not to undercut each other on this bank's rate card." You answer, "I can't discuss pricing," end the conversation, and tell your compliance team the same day. A clear, quick response protects you and your firm.

    🎭 Hard scenarios

    ScenarioBest response
    Client wants an across-the-board 10% rate cutUnderstand the goal, show value, trade for volume or terms, hold your floor
    Client is cutting suppliers from 20 to 8Present scorecard evidence, build executive support and differentiate
    MSP appears to favour its own sister firmCollect dated facts, raise it professionally with the MSP and the client program owner

    An account has one champion, a scorecard that fell from 4.2 to 3.6, and DSO of 80 days. You write a 30-day plan: add two contacts (owner: you), fix the top two scorecard drivers (owner: delivery lead) and trace late invoices (owner: finance). Three problems become three owned actions with dates.

    📖 Full case study

    Situation: A global bank's MSA renewal is in 8 months. Your account generates $3.0M a year at 14% gross margin, wallet share is 12%, and the bank plans to reduce suppliers from 14 to 9.

    1. Assess: scorecard rank is 4th of 14, strong on quality (submittal-to-interview 51%), weaker on speed in cloud roles. DSO is 58 days against net 45.
    2. Map stakeholders: one sponsor and two hiring directors know you. Procurement does not.
    3. Fix the basics: a cloud talent pool lowers cloud TAT from 31 to 19 hours; PO expiry alerts bring DSO to 47 days.
    4. Build relationships: meet procurement and the sponsor with a value summary; add two business units.
    5. Prepare the proposal: a volume-linked rate structure above your floor, a managed QA service offer and a liability cap of twelve months of fees.
    6. Negotiate: trade a 1.5% volume rebate for net 45, rate changes by mutual agreement and an annual escalator.
    7. Result: you stay among the nine suppliers, move to tier-1, and wallet share rises toward 18% over two years.
    ✅
    Why it worked: early start, fixing the weak scorecard areas, relationships beyond one contact, evidence over adjectives, and negotiating trades instead of free concessions.
    Example: what you would say to the bank's sponsor
    "We are rank 4 of 14 and improving. Cloud time-to-submit is down from 31 to 19 hours, our interview ratio is 51%, and our DSO is now inside terms. We would like to discuss a volume-linked structure and a managed QA service that could save your team time."
