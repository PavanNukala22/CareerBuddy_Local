    The Role & Data/AI Hiring Basics

    Before you hunt for MLOps engineers or LLM specialists, understand what a Data & AI Talent Acquisition Specialist owns, why this market behaves differently from regular tech hiring, and the words every conversation uses.

    🧭 What does this specialist do?

    A Data & AI Talent Acquisition Specialist finds, screens and wins people who work with data and artificial intelligence: the engineers who move data, the engineers who run machine-learning systems in production, the specialists who build with large language models, and the researchers who invent new methods. You are not expected to build a model. You are expected to tell a real practitioner from someone who just uses the right buzzwords.

    🎯
    Identify
    Recognise MLOps engineers, LLM engineers and data pipeline architects, and know how they differ.
    📡
    Track
    Follow AI research talent through papers, conferences and open-source work, long before a role opens.
    💰
    Structure pay
    Build offers for scarce skills: base, bonus, equity, sign-on and non-cash value.
    🤝
    Close
    Coordinate interviews, handle competing offers and get a yes.
    Example: one request, three very different people
    A client says: "We need someone for our AI team." That could mean a data engineer (builds the data flows), an MLOps engineer (keeps models running) or a research scientist (invents new methods). Your first job is to ask questions until the sentence becomes one specific profile.

    ⚖️ Why data & AI hiring is different

    FactorGeneral tech hiringData & AI hiring
    Talent poolLarge, many equivalent candidatesSmall for niche skills (LLM, MLOps at scale, research)
    TitlesFairly standardInconsistent: "AI Engineer" can mean five different jobs
    Where talent shows upLinkedIn, job boardsPapers, GitHub, Hugging Face, Kaggle, conferences
    PayNarrow bandsWide ranges, heavy on equity, sharp premiums for scarce skills
    CompetitionNormalCandidates often hold several offers
    HypeModerateHigh: many resumes claim AI skills after a short course
    Example: a title that hides the real job
    Two postings both say "AI Engineer". Posting A wants someone to connect a chatbot to the company's documents. Posting B wants someone to train and tune models on GPUs. Same title, different people, different pay. Always read the duties, not the title.

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Client side

    • Head of Data / AI: owns the budget and goals.
    • ML or data lead: judges technical depth.
    • HR and finance: approve pay bands and equity.

    🧑‍🔬 Candidate side

    • Practitioners: data and ML engineers in industry.
    • Researchers: publish papers, often with PhDs.
    • Hybrids: research engineers who turn papers into products.
    Example: a 10-minute calibration
    You send an ML lead three profiles: a clear yes, a clear no and a maybe. They say the maybe lacked "production experience". You add "Have you deployed and monitored a model in production?" to every screen from then on.

    📡 Signal vs AI buzzwords

    AI washing means overstating AI skill or use. Many resumes list "LLM, RAG, generative AI" after a weekend tutorial. Signal is specific evidence: what was built, with what data, how it was measured, what broke.

    🔴 Low signal

    "Worked on cutting-edge generative AI solutions using the latest LLM technologies."

    🟢 High signal

    "Built a document-search assistant for 3,000 support agents. Answer accuracy rose from 61% to 84% on our test set after we improved retrieval. Cost per query fell 40%."

    Example: the four follow-ups
    Resume: "Built a recommendation model." Ask: What data? (clicks from 2M users). How measured? (click-through rate in an A/B test). How much? (+7%). What went wrong? (cold-start users, fixed with popularity fallback).

    Module 02 · Technology Literacy

    Data & AI Fundamentals

    A plain-English map of how data becomes an AI product, and which job does which part, so every skill on a resume has a place to live in your head.

    🏭 The data-to-AI factory

    Think of a factory. Raw materials (data) arrive, conveyor belts (pipelines) clean and move them, a machine (the model) learns from them, and quality control (monitoring) keeps the output good.

    🧺
    Raw data
    Orders, clicks, documents, images, sensor readings.
    🚚
    Pipelines
    Collect, clean and deliver the data on schedule.
    ⚙️
    Models
    Programs that learn patterns and make predictions.
    🔍
    Monitoring
    Checks that the model still works as the world changes.
    Example: a food-delivery app
    Data: past orders and delivery times. Pipeline: nightly job cleans and loads them. Model: predicts "arrives in 28 minutes". Monitoring: alerts if predictions drift off after a new city launches.

    🪆 AI, ML, deep learning, LLM

    These are nested ideas, like Russian dolls. Each one sits inside the one before it.

    TermMeaningExample
    AIAny machine doing tasks that normally need human intelligence.A chess program, a voice assistant
    Machine learningAI that learns patterns from data instead of fixed rules.Spam filter learned from labelled emails
    Deep learningML using many-layered neural networks, strong on images, speech, text.Photo recognition
    LLM / GenAIDeep-learning models trained on huge text that can write and answer.A chat assistant drafting an email
    Example: matching claims to terms
    "We use rules: if amount over $10,000, flag it" is plain software, not ML. "The system learned fraud patterns from 5 years of payments" is ML. "Our assistant summarises contracts" is an LLM application.

    🎓 Types of learning

    🏷️ Supervised

    Learns from labelled examples: "this email is spam".

    🧩 Unsupervised

    Finds structure in unlabelled data, such as customer groups.

    🎮 Reinforcement

    Learns by trial and reward, like a game-playing agent.

    📚 Self-supervised

    Learns from the data itself, such as predicting the next word. LLMs start this way.

    Example: which type?
    Predicting house prices from past sales with known prices is supervised. Grouping shoppers by behaviour with no labels is unsupervised. A robot learning to walk from rewards is reinforcement.

    🔁 The model lifecycle

    Every ML system goes through these steps. Different roles own different steps, which is why one title never covers everything.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    StepTypical owner
    Collect, pipelineData Engineer, Data Pipeline Architect
    Features, training, evaluationData Scientist, ML Engineer, Applied Scientist
    Deploy, monitor, retrainMLOps Engineer, ML Engineer
    New methodsResearch Scientist
    Example: where did the model fail?
    Accuracy dropped after three months. Was it the pipeline (bad data arriving), the model (stale patterns), or monitoring (nobody noticed)? Knowing the lifecycle lets you ask the candidate which one they owned.

    🪜 Titles vs scope

    Titles vary wildly. Judge by scope: what did they own, how big, how long?

    LevelWhat they can do alone
    JuniorRun analyses or small tasks with guidance
    MidOwn one pipeline or model end to end
    SeniorDesign systems, choose tools, mentor
    Staff / PrincipalSet technical direction across teams
    Example: same title, different scope
    Candidate A: "Senior Data Scientist" who builds reports in notebooks. Candidate B: "Data Scientist" who owns a fraud model serving 2 million requests a day. B has the bigger scope, despite the shorter title.

    🪤 Beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "Data Scientist and Data Engineer are the same."
    • "Anyone using ChatGPT is an LLM engineer."
    • "A PhD means they can ship products."

    ✅ Reality

    • Scientists model data; engineers build the systems that deliver it.
    • Using a tool is not building with one.
    • Research and production are different skills; ask for shipped work.
    Example: fixing a wrong search
    A recruiter searches only for "Data Scientist" for a pipeline-heavy role and gets notebook people. After the intake call they search Data Engineer OR Analytics Engineer with Spark and Airflow, and the shortlist quality jumps.

    Module 03 · Identifying Data Pipeline Architects

    Data Pipeline Architects

    Every AI system lives or dies on its data. Learn how data platforms are designed, the tools behind them, and how to tell an architect from an engineer who only follows tickets.

    🚰 What a data pipeline is

    A data pipeline is an automated set of steps that collects data from sources, cleans and reshapes it, and delivers it somewhere useful: a dashboard, a warehouse or a model. Think of city plumbing: sources are reservoirs, the pipeline is pipes and filters, the tap is the dashboard or model.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: a retailer's nightly pipeline
    At 1 a.m. a job pulls the day's orders from the shop database, removes duplicates, converts currencies, and loads them into a warehouse. By 7 a.m. the sales dashboard and the demand-forecast model both use the fresh numbers.

    🔀 ETL vs ELT

    🔧 ETL

    Extract → Transform → Load. Clean the data first, then load the finished result. Common in older systems.

    📥 ELT

    Extract → Load → Transform. Load raw data first, then transform inside the cloud warehouse. Common with modern cloud tools and dbt.

    Example: the same task both ways
    ETL: a script cleans customer files on a server, then loads the clean table. ELT: raw files land in the warehouse untouched, then SQL models (often built with dbt) create the clean tables. ELT keeps the raw data for re-processing later.

    ⏱️ Batch vs streaming

    StyleHow it worksGood forExample
    BatchProcesses data in chunks on a scheduleReports, nightly training dataDaily sales totals
    StreamingProcesses events as they arriveFraud alerts, live recommendationsCard payment checked in seconds
    Example: choosing the right style
    A bank must block a stolen card within seconds → streaming. The same bank's monthly regulatory report → batch. A strong candidate explains why they chose each instead of using streaming everywhere.

    🏞️ Lake, warehouse, lakehouse

    StoreWhat it holdsAnalogy
    Data lakeRaw data in any format, cheap to storeA real lake: everything flows in
    Data warehouseCleaned, structured data ready for analysisA tidy supermarket shelf
    LakehouseLake storage with warehouse-style tables and speedA lake with a well-organised shop on the shore
    Example: where would this go?
    Raw website click logs and images → lake. Finance's cleaned monthly revenue table → warehouse. One platform serving both analysts and ML teams → lakehouse.

    🧰 Tool map

    JobCommon tools
    Orchestration (scheduling and ordering steps)Apache Airflow, Dagster, Prefect
    Large-scale processingApache Spark, Flink
    Streaming and messagingApache Kafka, Kinesis, Pub/Sub
    Transformation in the warehousedbt, SQL
    Cloud warehousesSnowflake, BigQuery, Redshift, Synapse
    Lakehouse platformsDatabricks, Delta Lake, Apache Iceberg
    AirflowSparkKafkadbtSnowflakeBigQueryDatabricksIceberg
    Example: tools are interchangeable, concepts are not
    A JD asks for Snowflake and the candidate used BigQuery for four years. Same job (cloud warehouse), different brand. Ask: "How did you design tables and control cost?" Concepts transfer; keywords do not tell the story.

    📐 Architect vs engineer

    🛠️ Data engineer

    Builds and runs pipelines. Answers "How?" Writes code, fixes failures.

    📐 Data pipeline architect

    Designs the whole platform, chooses tools, sets standards for quality and security. Answers "What and why?"

    Example: spot the architect
    "I wrote the Spark job that joins orders and customers." → engineer. "I chose a lakehouse over a warehouse because ML teams needed raw data too, and set data-quality rules for 40 sources." → architect.

    💬 Screening questions

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "Describe the largest pipeline you owned."Sources, volume, schedule, failures, cost, who used it"It moved lots of data."
    "What happens when a step fails at 3 a.m.?"Alerts, retries, safe re-run, backfill plan"Someone restarts it."
    "How do you check data quality?"Tests, null and duplicate checks, schema monitoring, lineage"We look at the dashboard."
    "Batch or streaming here, and why?"Ties choice to latency needs and cost"Streaming is more modern."
    "What does idempotent mean?"Re-running gives the same result with no duplicatesCannot explain
    Example: a strong answer in the wild
    "Our payment pipeline processed 40 million events a day. A schema change upstream once broke it. We added schema checks and alerts, and made every step idempotent, so we could safely replay a day of data." Numbers, failure, fix, design principle.

    🚩 Red flags

    • Lists 25 tools and cannot say which one they chose and why.
    • No numbers: no volumes, latency, cost or users.
    • Never mentions testing, monitoring or failure handling.
    • "Architect" title but every example is a task they implemented.
    • Cannot describe a trade-off they regret.
    Example: probing a red flag
    Resume: "Architected enterprise data platform." You ask: "How many sources, how much data a day, and what did you decide against?" If the answer is vague, treat the title as unproven and ask the tech lead for a deeper interview.

    Module 04 · Identifying MLOps Engineers

    MLOps Engineers

    Building a model is only the start. MLOps engineers keep models running, accurate and up to date in the real world. They are scarce and in demand, so you need to recognise them precisely.

    🛠️ What MLOps is

    MLOps (machine learning operations) is the set of practices for taking a model from a notebook to a dependable production service, then monitoring and updating it. If a data scientist bakes a cake once, the MLOps engineer builds the bakery that bakes it reliably every day.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: notebook to production
    A data scientist's fraud model works in a notebook. The MLOps engineer packages it in a container, tests it, registers version 7, deploys it behind an API, tracks accuracy daily, and retrains it automatically when quality drops.

    🔎 MLOps vs DevOps vs ML engineer

    RoleFocusExtra challenge
    DevOps EngineerShips and runs software reliablyCode changes only
    ML EngineerBuilds model-powered softwareModel quality and performance
    MLOps EngineerAutomates the full model lifecycleCode and data and models change; quality can decay silently
    Example: the silent failure
    A normal web app either works or crashes. A model can keep running while getting steadily worse because customer behaviour changed. Catching that silent decay is what makes MLOps different from DevOps.

    🧰 The MLOps toolkit

    NeedCommon tools
    Experiment tracking, model registryMLflow, Weights & Biases
    Workflow on KubernetesKubeflow, Argo, Airflow
    Managed ML platformsAWS SageMaker, Google Vertex AI, Azure Machine Learning
    Packaging and servingDocker, Kubernetes, KServe, FastAPI
    Feature storeFeast, Tecton, platform-native stores
    CI/CD and infrastructure as codeGitHub Actions, Jenkins, Terraform
    MonitoringPrometheus, Grafana, Evidently
    📝
    Two key terms. A model registry is a versioned library of trained models. A feature store keeps the same computed inputs available for both training and live predictions, so they match.
    Example: equivalent stacks
    Candidate A: SageMaker, Docker, Terraform on AWS. Candidate B: Vertex AI, Docker, Terraform on Google Cloud. Different brands, same lifecycle skills. Ask the manager if cross-cloud experience is acceptable before rejecting.

    📉 Drift and monitoring

    🌊 Data drift

    The inputs change from what the model saw in training. Example: new customers from a new country.

    🔄 Concept drift

    The relationship between inputs and the right answer changes. Example: fraud tactics evolve.

    Example: a drift story
    A delivery-time model was accurate in January. In June a promotion doubled order volume and times rose. Inputs looked normal, but the true relationship had shifted. An MLOps engineer who tracks live accuracy catches this within days, not months.

    🚀 Deployment patterns

    PatternMeaningWhy use it
    ShadowNew model runs silently beside the old oneCompare safely, no user impact
    CanarySend a small share of traffic to the new modelLimit damage if it is worse
    A/B testSplit users between versions and measure outcomesProve the business impact
    RollbackSwitch back to the previous version quicklyRecover from a bad release
    Example: a safe release
    The team shadows model v8 for a week, canaries it to 5% of traffic, sees conversion up 2%, then promotes it to 100%. When a bug appears at 3 a.m., they roll back to v7 in minutes.

    💬 Screening questions

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "How do you get a model into production?"Containerise, test, register, deploy gradually, monitor, roll back"I hand it to the engineers."
    "How do you know a live model is degrading?"Track accuracy proxies, input distributions, alerts"Users complain."
    "How do you version models and data?"Registry, data snapshots, reproducible pipelines"File names with dates."
    "How do you decide when to retrain?"Scheduled or triggered by drift thresholds, with validation gates"Every now and then."
    "How do you control GPU cost?"Autoscaling, spot instances, batching, right-sizing"We turned off the big one."
    Example: a strong answer
    "We served a ranking model on Kubernetes with autoscaling. Alerts fired if click-through dropped 10% below baseline. Retraining ran weekly with automated tests, and a canary release protected users. This cut incident time from hours to minutes."

    🚩 Red flags & worked example

    • "MLOps" on the resume but all examples are model training in notebooks.
    • Cannot explain monitoring beyond "dashboards".
    • No mention of testing, rollback or versioning.
    • Lists every ML tool, owns none.

    🟢 Candidate A

    "Moved 14 models to a registry-based pipeline on SageMaker. Added drift alerts and shadow testing. Release time fell from 3 weeks to 2 days."

    Verdict: owned, measured. Advance.

    🔴 Candidate B

    "Experienced in MLOps using Kubernetes, Kubeflow, MLflow, Airflow, SageMaker and Vertex AI."

    Verdict: tools only. Probe for a real project.

    Module 05 · Identifying LLM Engineers

    LLM & GenAI Engineers

    The hottest and most over-claimed skill in the market. Learn the building blocks, what real LLM engineering looks like, and the questions that separate builders from tutorial-followers.

    💬 How LLMs work (simply)

    A large language model has read huge amounts of text and learned to predict the next word, again and again, until it can write, summarise and answer. It works in tokens (small chunks of text) and you instruct it with a prompt.

    ✍️
    Prompt
    The instructions and question you give the model.
    🧱
    Token
    A small piece of text. Cost and limits are usually counted in tokens.
    🪟
    Context window
    How much text the model can consider at once.
    🌀
    Hallucination
    A confident answer that is false or unsupported.
    Example: why hallucination matters
    A law-firm assistant invents a court case that sounds real. The model was not lying on purpose; it produced plausible text. That is why serious LLM engineers talk about grounding answers in real documents and testing for errors.

    🧩 Key building blocks

    TermMeaningExample
    EmbeddingA list of numbers that captures the meaning of text"refund policy" and "money back rules" get similar numbers
    Vector databaseStores embeddings and finds the most similar ones fastPinecone, FAISS, pgvector, Weaviate
    ChunkingSplitting documents into pieces for retrieval500-word sections of a manual
    Fine-tuningFurther training a model on your examplesTeaching a model your support tone
    LoRAA cheaper way to fine-tune by training small add-on weightsTuning a large open model on one GPU
    AgentAn LLM that can call tools and take stepsLooks up an order, then drafts a reply
    GuardrailsRules that limit unsafe or off-topic outputBlock personal data in answers
    Example: embeddings in one sentence
    A search for "how do I get my money back" finds the article titled "Refund policy" even though no words match, because their embeddings are close together.

    📚 RAG explained

    RAG (retrieval-augmented generation) means the system first looks up relevant company documents, then gives them to the model with the question, so the answer is based on real sources.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: an HR assistant
    Employee asks, "How many sick days do I get?" The system retrieves the current HR policy page, adds it to the prompt, and the model answers using that page, citing it. When the policy changes, you update the document; no retraining needed.

    ⚖️ RAG vs fine-tuning

    QuestionRAGFine-tuning
    Best forFresh or private factsConsistent style, format, or specialised behaviour
    Updating knowledgeEdit the documentsRetrain the model
    Cost to startLowerHigher (data, compute, evaluation)
    Source citationsNaturalHard
    💡
    Rule of thumb. Need the model to know your latest facts → RAG. Need it to behave a certain way → fine-tune. Many products use both.
    Example: choosing correctly
    A bank wants answers from policies that change monthly → RAG. A legal team wants every reply in a strict clause format the base model keeps breaking → fine-tuning. A strong candidate explains this choice in two sentences.

    🧰 The LLM engineering stack

    LayerTypical tools
    Model accessHosted model APIs, open-source models such as Llama or Mistral families, Hugging Face
    OrchestrationLangChain, LlamaIndex, custom code
    RetrievalVector databases, search engines, rerankers
    EvaluationTest sets, human review, automated graders
    Serving and costvLLM, caching, batching, quantization
    SafetyGuardrails, content filters, access control
    Example: tools change fast
    Today's popular framework may be replaced next year. Ask "What would you do if LangChain disappeared?" A real engineer describes the underlying steps (retrieve, prompt, evaluate) and could rebuild them with other tools.

    📏 Quality, cost and safety

    ✅
    Quality
    Measured on a test set of real questions with correct answers, not by feel.
    💲
    Cost
    Tokens per query, model size, caching, smaller models for easy tasks.
    ⚡
    Latency
    How fast users get an answer; streaming, batching, smaller models help.
    🛡️
    Safety
    Privacy, harmful output, prompt injection, access to private data.
    Example: a trade-off in numbers
    Using the biggest model gave 90% accuracy at 6 seconds and high cost. A smaller model plus better retrieval gave 87% in 1.5 seconds at a fraction of the cost. The team shipped the smaller one. That is the kind of decision you should hear about.

    🔬 Real vs superficial

    🟢 Real LLM engineer

    • Talks about evaluation sets and failure cases
    • Explains retrieval quality, chunking, reranking
    • Quantifies cost and latency
    • Mentions safety and data privacy

    🔴 Superficial

    • "I use ChatGPT daily"
    • Lists many frameworks, no project
    • Judges quality by "it looks good"
    • No view on cost, errors or privacy
    Example: two answers to the same question
    "How did you improve your assistant?" A: "I tweaked the prompt a lot." B: "Accuracy was 61%. Most failures were bad retrieval, so we changed chunk sizes, added a reranker and reached 84% on a 300-question test set." B is the engineer.

    💬 Screening questions

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "How did you evaluate your LLM app?"Test set, metrics, human review, regression checks"We tried it and it seemed fine."
    "Answers were wrong. What did you check first?"Retrieval relevance, chunking, prompt, data freshness"Used a bigger model."
    "RAG or fine-tuning for this case, and why?"Matches need (facts vs behaviour), mentions cost and updates"Fine-tuning is better."
    "How did you control cost and latency?"Caching, smaller models, batching, shorter prompts"We didn't worry about it."
    "How did you handle sensitive data?"Access control, redaction, no private data in prompts without approval"Not a concern."
    Example: a follow-up that works
    Candidate says, "We used RAG for customer support." You ask, "What fraction of answers were correct, and how did you know?" A prepared engineer gives a number and a method. An unprepared one pauses and speaks in generalities.

    Module 06 · Tracking AI Research Talent

    Tracking AI Research Talent

    The best researchers are rarely job-hunting. Learn where they appear, how to read their work without being a scientist, and how to build relationships long before a role opens.

    🔬 Who is AI research talent?

    RoleWhat they doTypical proof
    Research ScientistInvents new methods, publishes papersPeer-reviewed papers, PhD, citations
    Research EngineerImplements and scales research ideasStrong code, reproduced results, open-source
    Applied ScientistUses research to solve product problemsShipped models, measurable impact
    Postdoc / PhD studentEarly-career researcher in a labFirst-author papers, advisor reputation
    Example: the right role for the need
    A client wants to invent a new training method → research scientist. A client wants to make a published method run fast in production → research engineer or applied scientist. Mixing these up wastes months and money.

    📍 Where they show up

    SourceWhat you find
    arXivPreprints (early papers), often not yet peer-reviewed
    ConferencesNeurIPS, ICML, ICLR (machine learning); CVPR (vision); ACL (language)
    Google Scholar / Semantic ScholarAuthor profiles, papers, citations
    GitHub / Hugging FaceCode, models, datasets, community contributions
    KaggleCompetition results, practical modelling skill
    Lab and university pagesTeam members, PhD cohorts, alumni
    NeurIPSICMLICLRCVPRACLarXivHugging FaceSemantic Scholar
    Example: from a paper to a person
    A new paper on efficient fine-tuning is popular. Its authors' Scholar pages show their lab, other work and GitHub. You add the two industry-based authors to your talent map and note their interests, months before a role opens.

    📡 Reading the signals

    You do not need to understand the maths. You need to ask a few simple questions about any paper or profile.

    🏛️
    Where published?
    Top peer-reviewed venue, workshop, or preprint only?
    🧑‍🔬
    What was their part?
    First author, middle, or senior author?
    🔁
    Is it reproducible?
    Code released? Others using it?
    📈
    Is it recent?
    AI moves fast; a 2016 profile may be out of date.
    Example: a 2-minute paper check
    Title matches your client's problem. Venue: ICLR. Candidate is first author. Code on GitHub with 2,000 stars. Published this year. That is a strong, current signal. Add to the shortlist and prepare a specific message.

    ✍️ Authorship & citations

    📌 Author order

    In many ML papers the first author did most of the hands-on work, and the last author is often the senior lead. Conventions vary by field, so ask the candidate.

    📊 Citations

    Show influence, but differ by sub-field, age and number of co-authors. The h-index is only a rough guide.

    ⚠️
    High citations do not prove someone can build or ship. A researcher with 5,000 citations from 40-author papers may have had a small role. Always ask: "What exactly did you contribute?"
    Example: two profiles compared
    Person A: 3,000 citations, always one of 30 authors. Person B: 400 citations, first author on two papers, released the code everyone uses. For a hands-on role, B may be the stronger match. Ask both about their personal contribution.

    🗺️ Talent mapping workflow

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    Pipelining means building relationships with people you may hire later. Log each person's interests, last contact and what would make them move.

    Example: a talent map row
    Name: Dr. A. Rao. Area: efficient fine-tuning. Signal: ICLR first author, open code. Status: happy at current lab, interested in applied work. Next touch: share a relevant paper in 3 months.

    ✉️ Outreach that works

    🔴 Generic

    "Hi, I have an exciting AI opportunity at a top company. Are you open to chat?"

    🟢 Specific

    "I read your ICLR paper on efficient fine-tuning. A client is applying similar ideas to on-device models and has a team with its own GPU cluster. Would a 15-minute chat about the problem be useful?"

    💡
    Researchers care about problems, freedom to publish, compute access and colleagues as much as pay. Lead with those.
    Example: a message that gets replies
    It names the paper, explains the client's real problem, mentions publication freedom and compute, and asks for a short chat, not a job application. Reply rates on messages like this are usually far higher than generic templates.

    ⚖️ Ethics & compliance

    • Use public, professional information. Do not scrape private data or build hidden profiles.
    • Follow your company's policy and local data protection law (such as GDPR); honour opt-out requests.
    • Respect candidates' current employers: check non-solicitation and non-compete issues with legal before approaching.
    • Do not judge by university prestige alone; it can add bias.
    Example: a safe habit
    You record where you found each contact ("author page, ICLR 2026"), store only professional details, and delete a person's record immediately when they ask. If a candidate's contract may restrict approaching them, you check with legal first.

    🔬 Worked example

    Client need: "Someone to improve how our language models are fine-tuned on limited hardware."

    Assessment: Candidate 1 is the best current match: relevant, hands-on, reproducible. Candidate 2 is a promising future hire. Candidate 3 needs a conversation: great history, unclear current activity. Next step: send Candidate 1 a specific message referencing the paper.

    Module 07 · Compensation Structuring for Niche Tech

    Compensation for Niche Tech

    When talent is scarce, a single salary number rarely wins. Learn the parts of a package, how to calculate and compare them, and how to design offers that attract the right person without breaking fairness.

    📝
    All numbers in this module are illustrative, chosen to make the maths clear. Real pay varies by country, company and time. Always use your company's current market data.

    💰 Total compensation

    Total compensation (total comp) is everything of value an employee receives, not just the base salary.

    💵
    Base salary
    Fixed pay, usually yearly.
    🎯
    Bonus
    Performance-based, often a % of base.
    📈
    Equity
    Company shares (RSUs) or options.
    🎁
    Sign-on & extras
    One-time cash, relocation, learning budget, compute access.
    Example: first-year total comp
    Base $180,000 + target bonus 10% ($18,000) + RSUs worth $200,000 vesting over 4 years ($50,000 a year) + sign-on $20,000. Year-1 total = 180,000 + 18,000 + 50,000 + 20,000 = $268,000. Year 2 (no sign-on) = $248,000.

    ⚖️ Cash vs equity

    TypeWhat it isRisk
    RSUShares granted that you receive as they vestValue moves with the share price
    Stock optionRight to buy shares at a fixed strike priceWorthless if the share price stays below strike
    Cash bonusPaid if targets are metMay be lower or zero
    Example: option maths
    Options to buy 10,000 shares at a $5 strike. If the share is worth $20, each option gains $15, so 10,000 × $15 = $150,000 before tax. If the share stays at $4, they are worth nothing. RSUs would still be worth $4 each.

    ⏳ Vesting explained

    Vesting is the schedule that earns equity over time. A common pattern is 4 years with a 1-year cliff: nothing vests until the first anniversary, then it continues monthly or quarterly.

    TimeVested share of a $200,000 grant
    Month 60% ($0), before the cliff
    Month 1225% ($50,000)
    Month 2450% ($100,000)
    Month 48100% ($200,000)
    Example: leaving early
    A candidate leaves at month 9 and gets $0 of the grant because of the cliff. That is why a sign-on bonus or a front-loaded schedule can matter when asking someone to leave a role where equity is about to vest.

    🦄 Why niche skills cost more

    FactorEffect on pay
    Few qualified people (supply)Push pay up sharply
    High business impact (revenue, risk)Push pay up
    Proven production experiencePremium over theory-only candidates
    Rare research track recordPremium, often through equity
    Common, widely taught skillsNarrower, lower bands
    Example: two roles, one band?
    A Data Analyst (many candidates) and an MLOps lead who runs models serving millions of users (few candidates). Putting both in the same pay band would make the second role nearly unfillable. Niche premiums exist for a reason.

    📊 Benchmarking

    • Salary surveys from reputable providers and your own placement history.
    • Live job postings in places that publish pay ranges.
    • Recent offers you won and lost, with reasons.
    • Client's internal bands, to avoid pay compression.

    Always compare like with like: same level, same location, same skill depth.

    Example: building a range
    From 12 recent placements for similar senior MLOps roles you see base from $160k to $195k. You propose a band of $165k to $195k with equity, then adjust for the candidate's depth and the client's location.

    🧾 Contract rates

    Contractors are paid by the hour. To compare with an annual salary, a common rule is 2,080 hours a year (40 hours × 52 weeks).

    ItemW2 employeeC2C / 1099 contractor
    Taxes and benefitsEmployer contributesContractor covers their own
    Paid leaveYesUsually none
    Hourly rateLower for same valueHigher to cover costs and gaps
    Job securityHigherProject-based
    Example: hourly to annual
    A $90 per hour contract × 2,080 hours = $187,200 a year of billable income. After self-paid benefits, tax costs and unpaid time off, it may compare to a lower W2 salary. Compare the whole package, not one number.

    🧩 Structuring the offer

    When the budget for base is capped, you still have levers.

    LeverWhen it helps
    Higher equityBase is capped but the company has equity budget
    Sign-on bonusCandidate loses an unvested bonus or equity by leaving
    Title or scopeCandidate wants growth, not only money
    Remote or flexible workCandidate values location freedom
    Compute, publishing, conference budgetResearchers and builders who want to do great work
    Faster review (6 months)Candidate is joining slightly under their target
    Example: working within a cap
    The band's base maximum is $190k but the candidate wants $205k total cash. You offer $190k base, a $20k sign-on to cover a bonus they forfeit, and a larger RSU grant. Same budget owner, a package that fits the candidate's real loss.

    🥊 Competing offers

    1. Ask early: "Are you speaking with other companies, and what matters most?"
    2. Compare total comp over several years, not just base.
    3. Understand the non-cash factors: mission, team, compute, growth.
    4. Never invent or inflate a competing offer; stay truthful.
    5. If you must decline, do it professionally so the relationship survives.
    Example: higher base vs higher equity
    Offer X: base $200k, RSUs $80k over 4 years ($20k a year) = $220k a year. Offer Y (yours): base $185k, RSUs $200k over 4 years ($50k a year) = $235k a year. Y is worth more per year if the share price holds, but also carries more risk. Present both views and let the candidate choose.

    🤝 Fairness & transparency

    • Internal equity: a new hire paid far more than similar teammates can cause resentment and resignations (pay compression). Plan with HR.
    • Pay transparency: some places require salary ranges in postings. Know the rules where you hire.
    • Consistency: base offers on skill and scope, not on a candidate's previous pay or negotiation style.
    Example: preventing a compression problem
    A new hire's package would exceed the existing senior engineer's by 25%. Before sending the offer you discuss it with HR, review the senior engineer's band, and agree to a plan, rather than discovering the problem when someone resigns.

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Verification, Intelligence & Metrics

    What separates a good recruiter from a trusted talent partner in AI: catching inflated claims, reading the market, measuring your results and handling the hard situations calmly.

    🕵️ Verifying AI claims

    Because AI skill is in demand, claims are often inflated. A calm, consistent verification routine protects your client and treats every candidate the same.

    ClaimHow to verify
    "Published at a top conference"Search the official proceedings or the author's Scholar page; ask for the paper title
    "Top 1% on Kaggle"Check the public Kaggle profile and competition names
    "Built an LLM product used by thousands"Ask for scale, evaluation results, what broke, their personal part
    "8 years building LLM applications"Check the timeline against when the technology became widely available
    "Led the MLOps platform"Ask for team size, models served, incidents, cost savings
    📝
    Be fair. A missing paper might be a name change or a different spelling. Verify first, ask politely, and use the same checks for everyone.
    Example: a polite verification
    Resume says "NeurIPS 2025 paper". You cannot find the name in the proceedings. You ask: "Could you share the paper title or link? I want to read it before the interview." A genuine author sends it in minutes, and you can proceed without any accusation.

    🌐 Market intelligence

    QuestionHow to find out
    Who is hiring for this skill?Job postings, funding news, company blogs
    Who is losing talent?Profile moves on professional networks, layoffs, team changes
    Which skills are rising?Posting trends, conference topics, open-source activity
    What does competition pay?Posted ranges, recent offers, surveys
    Example: turning news into a search
    A competitor announces layoffs in its AI platform team. You quickly identify MLOps engineers there, contact them respectfully, and bring two strong candidates to your client the next week, with the story of why they are available.

    📊 Metrics that matter

    MetricFormulaExample
    Outreach reply rateReplies ÷ messages sent18 ÷ 60 = 30%
    Submittal-to-interviewInterviews ÷ submittals6 ÷ 10 = 60%
    Offer acceptanceAccepted ÷ offers3 ÷ 4 = 75%
    Time to fillStart date − req date52 days
    Pipeline depthWarm, qualified people per target role12 for each niche role
    💡
    Read the numbers. Low reply rate → your messages are generic. Low submittal-to-interview → screening is letting through poor fits. Low acceptance → pay, speed or candidate care needs work.
    Example: diagnosing a week
    You sent 60 messages and got 6 replies: 10% against a 25% goal. You rewrite messages to name a paper and a real problem. Next week: 60 sent, 17 replies, 28%.

    ⚖️ Fair, responsible hiring

    • Use the same scorecard and core questions for every candidate.
    • Do not filter on university brand alone; it narrows and biases the pool.
    • Be careful with automated screening tools: test them for unfair results and keep a human in the loop.
    • Give candidates a clear process, timeline and feedback.
    Example: widening a narrow funnel
    A search limited to three famous universities produced no women and few career-changers. After switching to a skills-based scorecard and open-source evidence, the shortlist doubled in diversity and the hiring manager rated quality as equal or better.

    🎭 Hard scenarios

    ScenarioBest response
    Budget far below market for a niche skillShow market evidence and discuss trade-offs in budget, scope, timeline or flexibility on day one
    Candidate wants more than the bandExplore equity, sign-on, title, scope; involve HR early
    Hiring manager rejects with "not senior enough"Ask for specific examples, update the scorecard
    Candidate accepts, then receives a counterofferDiscuss real reasons for leaving before they resign; stay honest
    Candidate profile looks inflatedVerify with a project deep dive and public evidence
    Example: a budget conversation
    Client wants a senior LLM engineer at $140k in a market where comparable roles post $175k to $210k. You show five current postings and offer options: raise the band, hire a mid-level with a growth plan, or allow remote hiring from a lower-cost region. The client chooses a mid-level hire with a clear path.

    📖 Full case study

    Req: Senior MLOps Engineer for a retail company running 12 models. Needed in 5 weeks. Base capped at $190k.

    1. Intake: must-have: deployment, monitoring, retraining on a major cloud. Nice: feature stores. Cross-cloud experience acceptable.
    2. Map: 30 target companies in retail, logistics and fintech with large model fleets.
    3. Source and screen: 45 profiles, 14 calls. 6 fail the "owned" test (tools only). 8 remain.
    4. Verify: 4 have public talks or open-source work; 2 share project walkthroughs.
    5. Submit: 4 candidates with write-ups in week 2.
    6. Offer: top candidate wants $205k cash. You structure $190k base, $20k sign-on for a forfeited bonus, and a stronger RSU grant. HR confirms internal equity.
    7. Result: accepted in week 4, joined at the 5-week mark.
    ✅
    Why it worked: clear intake, mapped talent, evidence-based screening, and an offer built around the candidate's real loss instead of fighting the base cap.
    Example: what you would say to the client
    "I have 4 candidates. Two show production-scale monitoring and cost savings. One is the strongest, and I recommend a structure of base, sign-on and equity that fits your band and keeps pay fair with your current team."
