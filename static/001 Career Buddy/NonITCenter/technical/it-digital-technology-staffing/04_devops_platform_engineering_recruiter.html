    The Role & DevOps Hiring Basics

    Before you screen for Kubernetes, CI/CD or Terraform, understand what a DevOps & Platform Engineering Recruiter owns, why these titles are so confusing, and the words every conversation uses.

    🧭 What does this recruiter do?

    A DevOps & Platform Engineering Recruiter finds, screens and places the engineers who build and run the machinery behind software: the pipelines that ship code, the clusters that run it, and the infrastructure defined in code. You are not expected to run a cluster. You are expected to screen for Kubernetes, CI/CD and Terraform skill, understand infrastructure-as-code hiring, and assess whether a candidate's tech stack fits the client's.

    ☸️
    Screen the core skills
    Kubernetes, CI/CD pipelines and Terraform, with depth, not just keywords.
    🏗️
    Hire for IaC
    Understand infrastructure as code and what real ownership looks like.
    🧩
    Assess stack fit
    Compare the candidate's tools with the client's and judge ramp-up time.
    🤝
    Close
    Handle on-call expectations, offers and competing interest.
    Example: one request, four different people
    A client says: "We need a DevOps engineer." That could mean a CI/CD engineer (builds the delivery pipeline), a Kubernetes platform engineer (runs the clusters), an SRE (keeps services reliable) or a cloud infrastructure engineer (builds with Terraform). Your first job is to ask questions until the sentence becomes one specific profile.

    🌀 Why DevOps hiring is tricky

    ChallengeWhat it looks like
    Inconsistent titlesDevOps, SRE, platform, cloud, infrastructure and release engineer often overlap
    Tool soupJDs list 20 tools; only three or four really matter
    Depth is invisible"Used Kubernetes" can mean deploying one app or running 50 clusters
    Fast-changing toolsPopular tools change every few years, so concepts matter more than names
    On-callMany roles include out-of-hours support, which filters candidates
    Scarce senior talentPeople who have run production platforms at scale are rare
    Example: two postings, one title
    Posting A, "DevOps Engineer": keep a small team's Jenkins jobs running. Posting B, "DevOps Engineer": design a multi-region Kubernetes platform used by 200 developers. Same title, very different level and pay. Always read the duties.

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Client side

    • Head of platform / engineering: owns the budget and goals.
    • SRE or platform lead: judges technical depth.
    • Developers: the internal customers of the platform.
    • Security and HR: compliance, access, pay.

    🧑‍💻 Candidate side

    • DevOps and SRE engineers: pipelines and reliability.
    • Platform engineers: internal tooling and clusters.
    • Cloud and infrastructure engineers: IaC and networking.
    • Release engineers: build and release automation.
    Example: talk to the internal customers
    You ask a platform lead, "What do your developers complain about most?" The answer, "deployments take two hours and often fail", tells you the real hire: someone who has fixed slow, flaky pipelines, not just someone who knows Kubernetes.

    📡 Signal vs tool lists

    DevOps resumes are walls of logos. Signal is specific evidence: what was built, at what scale, with what result, and what broke.

    🔴 Low signal

    "DevOps engineer experienced in Kubernetes, Docker, Jenkins, Terraform, Ansible, AWS, Azure, Prometheus and Grafana."

    🟢 High signal

    "Migrated 30 services to Kubernetes on EKS and moved deploys from Jenkins to GitHub Actions. Deploy time fell from 45 to 8 minutes, and we ship about 20 times a day with rollbacks under 2 minutes."

    Example: the four follow-ups
    Resume: "Built CI/CD pipelines." Ask: What stages? (build, test, scan, deploy). What scale? (40 repos). What result? (deploys from weekly to daily). What went wrong? (flaky tests; fixed by quarantining them).

    Module 02 · Technology Literacy

    DevOps Fundamentals

    A plain-English map of how software gets from a developer's laptop to real users, and which job does which part, so every skill on a resume has a place to live in your head.

    🤝 What DevOps means

    DevOps joins Development (writing software) and Operations (running it). Before DevOps, developers "threw code over the wall" and operations teams struggled to run it. DevOps replaces the wall with shared responsibility and automation.

    🧱 Before

    • Releases every few months
    • Manual server setup
    • Blame between teams
    • Big, risky deployments

    🚀 With DevOps

    • Small, frequent releases
    • Automated builds and servers
    • Shared ownership
    • Fast, safe rollbacks
    Example: a restaurant analogy
    Developers are the chefs inventing dishes. Operations is the dining room and kitchen equipment. In old-style teams, chefs hand over a recipe and leave. In a DevOps team, everyone owns whether the customer gets a good meal, and the kitchen is automated so every dish comes out the same way.

    ♾️ The delivery lifecycle

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    StageWhat happensTypical tools
    CodeDevelopers write and review changesGit, GitHub, GitLab
    Build and testCode is compiled and checked automaticallyJenkins, GitHub Actions, GitLab CI
    Release and deployApproved versions go liveArgo CD, Spinnaker, Helm

    Example: a change travelling the loop
    A developer fixes a bug and pushes to Git. Within minutes the pipeline builds it, runs tests, and deploys it to staging. After approval it reaches production. Monitoring watches the error rate; if it jumps, the team rolls back. That loop is DevOps in practice.

    Example: a result with numbers
    "We moved from monthly releases to daily, cut lead time from 3 weeks to 2 days, and held change failure rate under 10%." That one sentence touches three DORA metrics. A candidate who can say this has probably owned real delivery improvement.

    🪤 Beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "DevOps is a tool you buy."
    • "Docker and Kubernetes are the same."
    • "More tools on the resume means more skill."

    ✅ Reality

    • DevOps is a way of working supported by tools.
    • Docker builds and runs containers; Kubernetes manages many of them.
    • Depth in the required tools beats a long list.
    Example: fixing a wrong search
    A recruiter searches only "DevOps" for a reliability role and gets pipeline people. After the intake call they search SRE OR "site reliability" OR "production engineer" with Prometheus and incident response, and the shortlist fits.

    Module 03 · Screening CI/CD Talent

    CI/CD Engineers

    The people who automate how software is built, tested and released. Learn the vocabulary, the tools, and the questions that separate pipeline owners from people who only edit a YAML file.

    🏭 What CI/CD is

    CI (Continuous Integration) means developers merge changes often, and every change is automatically built and tested. CD (Continuous Delivery or Deployment) means the tested change is automatically prepared, or sent, to users. Think of a car factory line: every part is checked as it moves along, and a faulty one is stopped immediately.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: a day without CI/CD
    A team of 10 merges code once a month. Integration bugs pile up, and release day takes a weekend of stress. With CI/CD they merge daily, each change is tested in 10 minutes, and release day is a non-event.

    ⚖️ Delivery vs deployment

    📦 Continuous delivery

    Every change is ready to release. A human presses the button to go live.

    ⚡ Continuous deployment

    Every change that passes tests goes to production automatically, with no manual step.

    Example: which is right?
    A bank may use continuous delivery: changes are always ready, but a manager approves production releases. A consumer web app may use continuous deployment: dozens of small changes go live automatically every day. A strong candidate explains why their team chose one.

    🔬 A pipeline, stage by stage

    StageWhat it checksWhy it matters
    BuildCode compiles and packagesCatches broken code early
    Unit and integration testsBehaviour is still correctPrevents regressions
    Static analysis and security scanCode quality and known vulnerabilitiesShifts security left
    ArtifactA versioned package or container image is storedThe same package goes everywhere
    DeployRelease to staging and then productionRepeatable, low-risk releases
    VerifySmoke tests and monitoring after releaseDetects a bad release fast
    Example: a pipeline in one sentence
    "Our pipeline builds a Docker image, runs 1,200 tests in 9 minutes, scans for vulnerabilities, pushes the image to a registry, deploys to staging, runs smoke tests, then waits for approval before production." Every stage named, with a number. This is an owner.

    🧰 Tool map

    JobCommon tools
    CI/CD servers and servicesJenkins, GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, CircleCI, Azure DevOps Pipelines
    GitOps deployment to KubernetesArgo CD, Flux
    Continuous delivery platformsSpinnaker, Octopus Deploy
    Artifact and image registriesDocker Hub, Amazon ECR, Artifactory, Nexus

    JenkinsGitHub ActionsGitLab CIArgo CDHelmArtifactoryTrivy
    Example: tools are interchangeable
    A JD says GitHub Actions and the candidate spent five years on Jenkins. Both define the same stages: triggers, jobs, steps, secrets. Ask: "What was your most complex pipeline and what made it hard?" Concepts transfer; the YAML syntax can be learned in weeks.

    🚦 Deployment strategies

    StrategyHow it worksBenefit
    RollingReplace instances graduallyNo downtime, simple
    Blue-greenRun old (blue) and new (green) environments, then switch trafficInstant rollback by switching back
    CanarySend a small share of traffic to the new version firstLimits damage from a bad release
    Feature flagsShip code switched off, turn on laterSeparates release from deployment
    Example: a safe release
    A team deploys version 2.4 to 5% of users (canary), watches errors for 20 minutes, then ramps to 100%. At 3 a.m. a different release misbehaves, and they roll back by switching traffic to the old (blue) environment in under two minutes.

    🌿 GitOps

    GitOps means the desired state of your systems is stored in Git. A tool such as Argo CD or Flux continuously compares the live system with Git and corrects any difference. To deploy, you change a file and merge a pull request.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: GitOps in action
    To change from 3 to 6 copies of an app, an engineer edits one number in Git and a colleague reviews it. After the merge, Argo CD updates the cluster. If someone changes it by hand later, the tool puts it back. Everything is reviewed, recorded and reversible.

    🔐 Security in the pipeline

    DevSecOps builds security checks into the pipeline rather than adding them at the end.

    • Secrets (passwords, keys) live in a secrets manager, never in code.
    • Scans check code, dependencies and container images for known problems.
    • Least privilege: pipelines get only the permissions they need.
    • Signed artifacts show an image came from your pipeline.
    Example: a red flag and a green flag
    Candidate A: "We kept the database password in the repo for convenience." Red flag. Candidate B: "Secrets came from a managed secrets service, rotated every 90 days, and the pipeline failed on any leaked key." Green flag.

    💬 Screening questions

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "Describe the biggest pipeline you built or owned."Stages, scale, duration, tools, results, failures"I used Jenkins."
    "How do you keep builds fast?"Caching, parallel jobs, splitting tests, smaller images"Bigger servers."
    "What do you do when tests are flaky?"Find root cause, quarantine, fix or remove, track metrics"Re-run until green."
    "How do you roll back a bad release?"Versioned artifacts, blue-green or canary, automated checks"Fix forward quickly."
    "How do you handle secrets?"Secrets manager, rotation, least privilege"Environment variables in the repo."
    Example: a strong answer in the wild
    "Our pipeline took 50 minutes. I split tests into four parallel jobs, cached dependencies and trimmed the Docker image. It dropped to 9 minutes, and developers started merging three times more often." Problem, action, number, outcome.

    🚩 Red flags

    • Cannot describe a stage-by-stage pipeline they owned.
    • Only edited existing pipeline files, never designed one.
    • No testing, rollback or secrets story.
    • Lists eight CI tools, owns none.
    Example: probing a red flag
    Resume: "Implemented CI/CD." You ask: "Which stages, how long did the pipeline take, and what did you improve?" If the answer is vague, ask the tech lead for a deeper interview before submitting.

    Module 04 · Screening Kubernetes Talent

    Containers & Kubernetes

    The most over-claimed skill in DevOps. Learn what containers and Kubernetes really are, how deep "I used Kubernetes" can go, and how to probe it without being an engineer.

    📦 Containers explained

    A container packages an application with everything it needs, so it runs the same on a laptop, a test server or the cloud. Think of a shipping container: the contents are packed once and any ship, train or truck can carry it.

    IdeaMeaning
    ImageThe packaged blueprint of the app
    ContainerA running copy of an image
    DockerfileThe recipe for building an image
    RegistryA library where images are stored
    Example: it works on my machine
    A developer's app works locally but fails on the server because of a different library version. Packaged as a container, the same image runs identically everywhere. This is why nearly every modern platform uses containers.

    🎼 Why Kubernetes exists

    One container is easy to run. Hundreds, across many machines, need something to place them, restart them, scale them and connect them. Kubernetes is that conductor, an orchestrator. It originated at Google and is now hosted by the Cloud Native Computing Foundation (CNCF).

    Example: Kubernetes at 3 a.m.
    A server fails overnight. Kubernetes notices that three copies of an app disappeared, starts new ones on healthy servers and re-connects traffic, all before anyone wakes up. Without an orchestrator, someone would be paged to do it manually.

    🧩 Core Kubernetes ideas

    ConceptPlain meaning
    ClusterThe whole set of machines Kubernetes manages
    NodeOne machine in the cluster
    PodThe smallest unit: one or more containers running together
    DeploymentSays "keep N copies of this app running and update them safely"
    ServiceA stable address to reach a group of pods
    IngressRoutes outside web traffic into services
    NamespaceA way to separate teams or environments inside one cluster
    ConfigMap and SecretSettings and sensitive values given to apps
    HPAHorizontal Pod Autoscaler: adds or removes copies based on load
    HelmA package manager for Kubernetes applications
    📝
    Kubernetes Secrets are meant for sensitive data, but by default they are only encoded, not strongly encrypted, so teams add extra protection. A strong candidate knows this.
    Example: a small picture
    You tell Kubernetes: "Keep 3 copies of the shop app." That is a Deployment. Each copy is a pod. A Service gives them one address, and an Ingress lets customers reach it. When traffic doubles, the HPA adds copies.

    ☁️ Managed Kubernetes

    CloudManaged Kubernetes service
    AWSEKS (Elastic Kubernetes Service)
    AzureAKS (Azure Kubernetes Service)
    Google CloudGKE (Google Kubernetes Engine)
    Self-managedClusters built and maintained by the team (more control, more work)
    Example: equivalent experience
    A JD asks for EKS and the candidate has run AKS in production for three years. The core Kubernetes skills are the same; only cloud-specific parts such as networking and identity differ. Ask the manager if cross-cloud experience is acceptable before rejecting.

    📶 Depth ladder

    RungWhat it sounds likeTypical role
    Listed"Skills: Kubernetes, Docker, Helm"Unproven
    User"Deployed our apps to Kubernetes with kubectl and Helm"Mid-level developer or DevOps
    Operator"Ran production clusters: upgrades, autoscaling, debugging, access control"Platform or SRE engineer
    Builder"Designed the multi-cluster platform, networking, security and cost model"Senior or staff platform engineer
    💡
    Linux Foundation certifications help as a signal: CKAD (application developer), CKA (administrator) and CKS (security) are hands-on exams. Still ask for real production stories.
    Example: placing a candidate
    "We ran 12 clusters on EKS, did quarterly upgrades with zero downtime, set resource limits to stop one team starving others, and used RBAC to give each team its own namespace." That is a clear operator, fit for a platform role, and worth a senior conversation.

    🛠️ Day-two skills

    Deploying an app is "day one". Keeping the platform healthy is "day two", and it is where real experience shows.

    ⬆️
    Upgrades
    Updating Kubernetes versions safely.
    📏
    Resources
    CPU and memory requests and limits.
    🔑
    Access control
    RBAC, network policies, secrets.
    🔍
    Debugging
    Logs, events and restarting loops.
    Example: a debugging story
    A pod keeps restarting (a state often shown as "CrashLoopBackOff"). A strong candidate says, "I check the pod's logs and events, then the configuration and resource limits. Last time it was a missing environment variable after a deploy." A weaker one says, "I delete the pod."

    💬 Screening questions

    AskStrong answerWeak answer
    "How big was your largest cluster and what ran on it?"Nodes, workloads, traffic, teams, cloud"A few apps."
    "How did you upgrade Kubernetes?"Plan, test in staging, rolling node upgrades, rollback plan"We rebuilt it."
    "How do you stop one app starving others?"Resource requests and limits, quotas, autoscaling"Add more nodes."
    "How do teams get access?"RBAC, namespaces, least privilege"Everyone has admin."
    "What was your worst production incident?"Cause, steps, fix, prevention"Nothing serious."
    Example: a strong answer
    "A memory leak made pods restart every few minutes. We found it from metrics, set memory limits so one app could not take down a node, and added an alert. Restarts dropped from 200 a day to 2."

    🚩 Red flags & worked example

    • "Kubernetes" only as a list item, with no cluster details.
    • Never handled an upgrade, outage or access control.
    • Treats Docker and Kubernetes as the same thing.
    • Cannot explain a pod, a service or a deployment in plain words.

    🟢 Candidate A

    "Operated 6 EKS clusters for 40 services. Quarterly upgrades, autoscaling, RBAC per team. Cut cloud cost 25% by right-sizing."

    Verdict: operator with numbers. Advance.

    🔴 Candidate B

    "Kubernetes, Docker, Helm, Istio, Prometheus, Terraform, Jenkins, Ansible, ArgoCD and more."

    Verdict: tool list only. Probe for a real cluster.

    Example: how you would decide
    You advance A and ask about the cost-cutting method. For B you ask, "Tell me about one cluster you ran: size, workloads and a problem you fixed." If B cannot answer, you do not submit B for a platform role.

    Module 05 · Terraform & Infrastructure-as-Code Hiring

    Terraform & Infrastructure as Code

    Infrastructure defined in files instead of clicked together in a console. Learn the ideas, the Terraform vocabulary, and how to tell a real IaC engineer from someone who only edits other people's files.

    🏗️ What IaC is

    Infrastructure as Code (IaC) means servers, networks and databases are described in text files that are stored in Git, reviewed and run by a tool. Think of an architect's blueprint: anyone can rebuild the same building from the plans, and changes are visible and reviewable.

    🖱️ Clicking in a console

    • Hard to repeat exactly
    • No history of who changed what
    • Environments drift apart
    • Disaster recovery is guesswork

    📄 Infrastructure as code

    • Repeatable, same result each time
    • Changes reviewed in Git
    • Environments match
    • Rebuild from files after a disaster
    Example: a disaster rebuild
    A region goes down. Team A built everything by hand over three years and needs weeks to recreate it. Team B has everything in Terraform: they run the code in another region and are back in about an hour. This is the business case for IaC.

    ⚖️ Declarative vs imperative

    StyleYou describeExample
    DeclarativeThe end result you want"I want 3 servers." Terraform and CloudFormation work this way.
    ImperativeThe steps to take"Create server 1, then 2, then 3." Scripts work this way.
    Example: why declarative helps
    You ask for 3 servers and 3 already exist. A declarative tool does nothing. An imperative script that blindly "creates 3" would give you 6. Declarative tools compare what exists with what you asked for and change only the difference.

    🧩 Terraform concepts

    TermPlain meaning
    ProviderA plug-in that talks to a platform (AWS, Azure, GCP, Kubernetes)
    ResourceOne thing to create (a server, a network, a database)
    VariableAn input that changes behaviour, such as the environment name
    ModuleA reusable bundle of resources
    StateTerraform's record of what it has created
    BackendWhere state is stored (for teams, a shared remote location)
    PlanA preview of what would change
    ApplyMake the changes
     
    what Terraform looks like (you don't need to write this)resource "aws_s3_bucket" "logs" {
  bucket = "acme-app-logs"
}
    Example: reading it in plain English
    The snippet says: "I want a storage bucket named acme-app-logs in AWS." If it does not exist, Terraform creates it. If it exists, Terraform does nothing. If someone renamed it by hand, Terraform notices and proposes to fix it.

    ⚠️ State: the key risk

    Terraform's state is a file mapping your code to real resources. If it is lost, corrupted or edited by two people at once, serious damage can follow. Mature teams store state remotely with locking, so only one change can run at a time.

    PracticeWhy it matters
    Remote backend (shared storage)The whole team sees the same state
    State lockingPrevents two applies from colliding
    Separate state per environmentA mistake in dev cannot touch production
    Encryption and access controlState can contain sensitive values
    Example: a state horror story
    Two engineers apply at the same time with state saved on a laptop. The second apply overwrites the first, and resources become "orphaned" and unmanaged. A candidate who tells this story, and describes the fix (remote state with locking), has real scars and real knowledge.

    🔁 Plan and apply

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    Drift means the real infrastructure no longer matches the code, usually because someone changed it by hand. Good teams detect and correct drift.

    Example: reading a plan like a headline
    A plan summary says "2 to add, 1 to change, 1 to destroy." A careful engineer pauses on the "destroy": which resource, why, and is it production? Candidates who describe this habit of reading the plan before applying are experienced.

    🧰 The IaC tool map

    ToolTypeNotes
    TerraformProvisioning, multi-cloudMost widely used; uses its own configuration language
    OpenTofuProvisioning, multi-cloudOpen-source fork of Terraform, with very similar skills
    PulumiProvisioning, multi-cloudUses general programming languages
    CloudFormationProvisioning, AWS onlyAWS-native
    Bicep / ARMProvisioning, Azure onlyAzure-native
    AnsibleConfiguration managementSets up software on existing machines, agentless
    💡
    Provisioning vs configuration. Terraform creates the building (servers, networks). Ansible furnishes the rooms (installs and configures software). Many teams use both.
    Example: transferable skills
    A JD asks for Terraform; the candidate has three years of CloudFormation on AWS. Both are declarative, state-aware provisioning tools, so a strong CloudFormation engineer can usually become productive in Terraform quickly. Ask the manager about ramp-up tolerance.

    🧪 Modules, testing, policy

    PracticeWhat it isWhat a mature team does
    ModulesReusable infrastructure building blocksShared, versioned modules
    TestingChecking code before it touches real infrastructureValidation, linting, automated tests in the pipeline
    Policy as codeRules written as code (for example, "no public databases")Automated checks with tools such as OPA
    Cost checksEstimating cost before applyCost estimates in pull requests
    Example: policy as code
    A rule blocks any Terraform change that would create a database open to the internet. When a junior engineer tries, the pipeline fails with a clear message before anything is created. The mistake never reaches production.

    🎯 Hiring for IaC

    RungWhat it sounds like
    Listed"Terraform" in a skills list
    Used"Edited existing Terraform files to add resources"
    Owned"Wrote reusable modules, managed remote state, ran plans in CI"
    Led"Designed the multi-account structure, policies and module library for 20 teams"

    The HashiCorp Terraform Associate certification shows foundational knowledge. It is a helpful signal but does not replace real project evidence.

    Example: placing a candidate
    "I created a module library for networking and databases used by 15 teams. Plans run in CI, state is remote with locking, and policy checks block risky changes." That is an owned or led profile, suitable for a senior infrastructure role.

    "Describe a risky change you handled."Plan review, staged rollout, backup, rollback plan"I just ran it."
    Example: a strong answer
    "We had 40 near-identical environments. I replaced copy-pasted files with one tested module and variables. Creating a new environment dropped from two days to 20 minutes, and configuration mistakes fell sharply."

    🚩 Red flags

    • Only ever edited existing files, never designed structure.
    • State kept locally or in a shared drive.
    • Never read plan output carefully; "apply and hope".
    • Does not know what drift is.
    • Treats Terraform and Ansible as the same thing.
    Example: a fair probe
    Resume: "Terraform expert, 6 years." You ask, "Walk me through how you structured the last Terraform project, where state lived and how changes were reviewed." A confident, specific answer is an owner. Vagueness means you ask the platform lead for a technical interview first.

    Module 06 · Tech Stack Assessment

    Tech Stack Assessment

    The core of DevOps recruiting: deciding whether a candidate's experience fits the client's stack. Learn to map a stack, separate must-haves from transferable skills, score fit fairly, and estimate ramp-up time.

    🧱 What a tech stack is

    A tech stack is the set of technologies a team uses, layered from the cloud up to the monitoring screens. Assessing stack fit means comparing the client's layers with the candidate's experience, layer by layer.

    LayerExample choices
    CloudAWS, Azure, Google Cloud
    Infrastructure as codeTerraform, CloudFormation, Pulumi, Bicep
    Compute and orchestrationKubernetes (EKS, AKS, GKE), ECS, VMs
    CI/CDJenkins, GitHub Actions, GitLab CI, Argo CD
    ObservabilityPrometheus, Grafana, Datadog, ELK, OpenTelemetry
    Languages and scriptingPython, Bash, Go
    SecurityVault, cloud secret managers, scanners
    Example: one stack, written out
    "AWS + Terraform + EKS + GitHub Actions + Argo CD + Datadog + Python." That one line is a complete, comparable stack. Writing it for every req and every candidate turns a fuzzy "good fit?" into a layer-by-layer comparison.

    🗺️ Mapping the client stack

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    Questions that fill the map quickly:

    1. Which cloud, and is more than one used?
    2. Terraform or something else? Is it fully in code, or partly manual?
    3. Kubernetes or another runtime? How many clusters and services?
    4. Which CI/CD tool, and are you migrating to another?
    5. What do you use to monitor and get paged?
    6. What is the team size and on-call pattern?
    Example: a 10-minute mapping call
    The manager says, "AWS, Terraform for most things, EKS, Jenkins moving to GitHub Actions, Datadog." You record one key fact: the Jenkins to GitHub Actions migration is happening, so a candidate who has done a CI migration is more valuable than a pure Jenkins specialist.

    ⚖️ Must-have vs transferable

    CategoryMeaningExample
    Must-haveCannot start without it, or takes too long to learnProduction Kubernetes experience for a platform role
    TransferableA close equivalent can be learned in weeksGitLab CI for GitHub Actions
    Nice-to-haveHelpful, but easy to teachExperience with a specific monitoring tool
    Example: sorting a JD
    JD lists AWS, Kubernetes, Terraform, Jenkins, Datadog, Python. Manager confirms: must-have Kubernetes and Terraform; transferable Jenkins (any CI tool); nice Datadog and Python. Now your search is about two real skills instead of six logos.

    🔀 Equivalent tools

    JobTool AClose equivalentsTransfer difficulty
    CI/CDJenkinsGitHub Actions, GitLab CI, CircleCIEasy
    Managed KubernetesEKSAKS, GKEEasy to medium
    IaC provisioningTerraformOpenTofu (very easy), Pulumi, CloudFormation, BicepEasy to medium
    MonitoringPrometheus + GrafanaDatadog, New Relic, DynatraceEasy

    Kubernetes itselfKubernetesECS, Nomad (a different model)Harder, a different way of thinking
    ⚠️
    Easy or hard is a guide, not a rule. Always ask the hiring manager how much ramp-up they will accept, and tell candidates honestly where the gaps are.
    Example: a good equivalent
    Client needs Jenkins; the candidate built GitLab CI pipelines for 4 years with caching, parallel stages and rollbacks. The concepts are the same, so you submit with the note: "Strong CI/CD owner, new to Jenkins syntax, estimated 2 to 4 weeks to be productive."

    Rate each layer on a four-step depth scale, then weight the layers the client cares about most.

    ScoreDepthMeaning

    1Aware or listedUsed a little, or only listed
    2Used or ownedBuilt or ran it in a real project
    3LedDesigned it, mentored others, fixed hard problems

    20 out of 27 is about 74%. Strong on the two must-haves (Kubernetes 3, Terraform 2), weaker on observability. You submit and note: "Needs a few weeks to learn the client's monitoring tool." The number supports the story; it does not replace it.

    ⏱️ Ramp-up time

    GapTypical ramp-up (guide only)
    Same tool, different company1 to 2 weeks to learn the environment
    Close equivalent tool (Jenkins to GitHub Actions)2 to 4 weeks
    Same concept, different cloud (AWS to Azure)1 to 3 months to full confidence

    📝

    Example: telling the client the truth
    "Candidate has 5 years of AWS and strong Terraform, but your platform is on Azure. I estimate 2 to 3 months to full confidence, shorter for tasks they already know. Is that acceptable, or is Azure a must?" The client decides with full information.

    🧩 Reading a messy JD

    🔴 The JD says

    "Rockstar DevOps ninja with 10+ years: AWS, Azure, GCP, Kubernetes, Docker, Terraform, Ansible, Puppet, Chef, Jenkins, GitLab, Python, Go, Prometheus, Splunk."

    🟢 Your brief says

    Must: AWS, Kubernetes, Terraform, any CI/CD. Transferable: GitLab for Jenkins. Nice: Go, Prometheus. Ignore: Puppet, Chef, Splunk unless the manager insists.

    Example: the question that cuts through
    Ask the manager: "If you could only keep four of these, which four?" The reply usually reveals the real stack within a minute, and saves you from searching for a candidate who does not exist.

    🏚️ Legacy vs modern stacks

    TypeWhat it looks likeCandidate to look for
    LegacyOn-premise servers, manual changes, older toolsPragmatic, patient, enjoys modernising
    TransitioningPart in cloud, part on-premise, migrating CIMigration experience, flexible
    Modern cloud-nativeKubernetes, GitOps, IaC, observabilityPlatform builders with depth
    Example: a motivation match
    A client in transition needs someone to move 30 on-premise apps to Kubernetes. A candidate who spent 3 years doing that exact migration is more valuable than a pure cloud-native specialist who has never touched legacy systems.

    🔬 Worked example

    Client stack: AWS, Terraform, EKS, GitHub Actions, Datadog. Must-haves: Kubernetes and Terraform.

    candidate comparisonCandidate 1: AWS, Terraform (modules, remote state), EKS (3 clusters), Jenkins, Prometheus
Candidate 2: Azure, Bicep, AKS (1 cluster), GitHub Actions, Datadog
Candidate 3: AWS, CloudFormation, ECS (no Kubernetes), GitHub Actions, Datadog
    ✅
    Assessment: Candidate 1 matches both must-haves and the cloud; the gaps (Jenkins for GitHub Actions, Prometheus for Datadog) are quick to close. Candidate 2 has the right tools but a different cloud and IaC language: good, with a longer ramp-up. Candidate 3 lacks Kubernetes, a must-have, so is a weaker fit. Submit 1 and 2, with honest notes on ramp-up.
    Example: what you would write to the client
    "Two candidates fit. Candidate 1 matches AWS, EKS and Terraform directly, and needs about 2 to 4 weeks to move from Jenkins to GitHub Actions. Candidate 2 has the right tools on Azure and needs 2 to 3 months to reach full confidence on AWS. I recommend interviewing Candidate 1 first."

    Sourcing & Screening DevOps Talent

    Repeatable steps that turn a vague requirement into a short, evidence-based shortlist, with search strings, a scorecard, a call script and a submittal template you can copy today.

    🗺️ End-to-end workflow

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.

    Each stage has an exit rule: a clear yes or no before the candidate moves forward. That keeps quality high and saves the hiring manager's time.

    Example: exit rules in action
    Resume score under 15 out of 27 → stop. Call fails the "owned" test (cannot describe a real pipeline or cluster) → stop. Practical scenario answered well → write up and submit. Every decision can be explained to the client in one line.

    📞 The intake call

    1. What problem will this person solve in the first 90 days?
    2. Walk me through the stack, layer by layer. Which parts are must-haves?
    3. Which close equivalents are acceptable, and how much ramp-up?
    4. How big is the platform: services, clusters, team, deploys per day?
    5. What is the on-call pattern and how often do pages happen?
    6. What is the budget, contract type and location policy?
    7. What does the interview process look like and how quickly do you decide?
    Example: turning a vague ask into a brief
    "We need a DevOps person." After the call: Platform Engineer, AWS and EKS, Terraform must-have, GitHub Actions (Jenkins acceptable), weekly on-call rotation of six people, remote within the US, 5+ years, budget agreed.

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Reliability, Platforms & Judgement

    What separates a good recruiter from a trusted platform-talent partner: understanding reliability thinking, spotting inflated claims, handling migrations and hard conversations, and measuring your results.

    🎯 SRE concepts

    TermMeaningExample
    SLIService Level Indicator: a measurement of healthShare of requests that succeed
    SLOService Level Objective: the target for the SLI99.9% of requests succeed each month
    Error budgetThe unreliability the SLO allows0.1% of requests may fail
    PostmortemA blameless review after an incidentWhat happened, why, what we change
    💡
    Why the error budget matters. If a team has budget left, it can ship faster. If the budget is spent, it focuses on reliability. It turns "speed versus stability" into a shared, measured decision.
    Example: error budget in numbers
    A 99.9% monthly target over a 30-day month (43,200 minutes) allows about 43 minutes of failure. If an outage already used 40 minutes, the team pauses risky releases and fixes reliability. A candidate who explains this clearly has done real SRE work.

    🛍️ Platform as a product

    Strong platform engineers treat developers as customers. They build self-service tools and "golden paths", the easy, safe way to do common things, instead of acting as a ticket queue.

    🎫 Ticket-queue mindset

    "Developers file a request and wait three days for a new environment."

    🛍️ Product mindset

    "Developers create an environment themselves in 10 minutes from a template, and we measure how many do."

    Example: a platform win
    A platform engineer built a template that creates a new service with pipeline, monitoring and security checks. New-service setup dropped from 2 weeks to 1 hour, and satisfaction scores from developers rose. That is a platform-as-product story.

    🕵️ Verifying claims

    ClaimHow to verify
    "Kubernetes expert"Cluster size, upgrades, incidents, access control, cost; a debugging story
    "Terraform expert"Module design, state management, drift, reviewing plans
    "Built our CI/CD"Stages, duration before and after, scale, rollback
    "Open-source contributor"Public commit history or merged pull requests under their name
    "Led the migration"Phases, risks, what was not migrated, how rollback worked
    📝
    Be fair. Much DevOps work is private, so an empty public profile proves nothing. Use public evidence as a bonus, and use the same questions for everyone.
    Example: a polite verification
    A candidate claims to maintain a popular open-source Helm chart. You find the repository and see the name in many merged pull requests. You mention it positively and ask, "What was the hardest change you reviewed?" Genuine maintainers answer instantly and in detail.

    🚚 Migrations

    Many roles are really about migration: moving from old to new without breaking the business.

    MigrationWhat good experience includes
    Jenkins to GitHub ActionsPhased rollout, parallel running, secrets handling, documentation
    VMs to KubernetesContainerising apps, networking, staged cutover, rollback
    Manual console to TerraformImporting existing resources, avoiding downtime, review process
    On-premise to cloudAssessment, pilot, data move, cost control
    Example: a depth question
    "What did you decide not to migrate, and why?" A strong candidate says, "We left a fragile batch system on VMs because the risk outweighed the benefit, and revisited it later." Knowing what not to move is a mark of experience.

    🎭 Hard scenarios

    ScenarioBest response
    Client wants Kubernetes, Terraform and Jenkins expert, 10 years, at a junior budgetShow market evidence; prioritise must-haves; offer options on level, budget or scope
    Strong candidate uses a different tool from the JDAsk the manager about equivalents and ramp-up; submit with an honest estimate
    Candidate claims "expert" but cannot describe an incidentProbe with a scenario; treat the claim as unproven
    Great candidate rejects the on-call patternShare it with the client; ask whether compensation or rotation size can change
    Hiring manager rejects with "not senior enough"Ask for specific examples and update the scorecard
    Example: a budget conversation
    Client wants a senior platform engineer, "Kubernetes, Terraform, Jenkins, 10 years", at a mid-level budget. You show current postings and offer: (1) raise the budget, (2) hire a mid-level engineer with strong Kubernetes and a mentoring plan, or (3) widen to remote candidates. The client picks option 2.

    📊 Metrics that matter

    MetricFormulaExample
    Screen-to-submittalSubmittals ÷ screening calls5 ÷ 20 = 25%
    Submittal-to-interviewInterviews ÷ submittals6 ÷ 10 = 60%
    Interview-to-offerOffers ÷ interviews3 ÷ 6 = 50%
    Offer acceptanceAccepted ÷ offers2 ÷ 3 = 67%
    Time to fillStart date − req date46 days
    💡
    Read the numbers. A low submittal-to-interview ratio points to poor screening or an unclear brief. A low offer acceptance points to pay, on-call or speed issues.
    Example: diagnosing a week
    You submitted 10 candidates and only 3 got interviews: 30%, well under your 50% target. Feedback says "not enough Kubernetes depth". You add a debugging scenario to the call script. The next batch of 10 gets 6 interviews: 60%.

    ⚖️ Ethical sourcing

    • Use public, professional information and respect each platform's terms.
    • Follow data-protection law and honour opt-outs.
    • Do not send mass copy-paste messages to open-source maintainers; personalise or do not contact.
    • Never use a candidate's current employer's confidential information.
    • Respect non-solicit agreements; check with legal first.
    Example: a clean habit
    You find an open-source maintainer's public profile and note the source. You message once, politely, mentioning their project. When they decline, you thank them and record "not now". Maintainers talk to each other, and a respectful reputation is your best sourcing channel.

    📖 Full case study

    Req: Senior Platform Engineer for a fintech: AWS, EKS, Terraform, moving Jenkins to GitHub Actions, weekly on-call. Needed in 6 weeks.

    1. Intake: must-haves: Kubernetes operations and Terraform ownership. Transferable: Jenkins or any CI tool. Nice: Datadog. On-call: six-person rotation.
    2. Source: 70 profiles from networks, GitHub and community groups.
    3. Screen: 18 calls. 8 fail the "owned" test (tool lists only). 10 remain.
    4. Practical: 30-minute plan-reading scenario: 6 pass.
    5. Stack fit: scores range from 17 to 23 out of 27; two have CI migration experience.
    6. Submit: 4 candidates with honest ramp-up notes in week 2.
    7. Result: 3 interviews, 1 offer, accepted in week 4, started at week 6.
    ✅
    Why it worked: a layer-by-layer stack map, depth-based screening, a fair practical, and write-ups that named gaps and ramp-up honestly.
    Example: what you would say to the client
    "I have 4 candidates. Two have run EKS in production with Terraform modules. One has just completed a Jenkins to GitHub Actions migration, which matches your roadmap. I recommend that candidate first, with a one-month ramp on your monitoring tool."
