    The Role & Sourcing Basics

    Before you join a Discord server or write a personalised message, understand what a Digital Talent Sourcing Specialist does, who you are trying to reach, and the ground rules that keep you welcome in the communities where engineers spend their time.

    🧭 What does a sourcer do?

    A Digital Talent Sourcing Specialist finds and starts conversations with technical people who are not applying for jobs. You build pipelines of interested candidates before the recruiter needs them, using online communities, networking and carefully written outreach, and you keep everything organised in a CRM.

    💬
    Community sourcing
    Find talent in places like Discord servers and Stack Overflow.
    🤝
    Networking
    Build real relationships in tech communities, online and in person.
    ✉️
    Personalised outreach
    Write messages that show you actually read the person's work.
    🗃️
    CRM workflows
    Track, tag and nurture contacts so nobody falls through the cracks.
    Example: a sourcer's week
    Monday you map two open-source communities for a Rust role. Tuesday you answer questions in a Rust Discord server and meet three people who might fit. Wednesday you send eight personalised messages. Thursday you log replies in the CRM. Friday you hand two interested candidates to the recruiter with notes.

    ⚖️ Sourcing vs recruiting

    AreaSourcerRecruiter
    Main jobFind and engage potential candidatesScreen, submit, negotiate and close
    FocusPipeline and first conversationsReqs and hiring decisions
    Success looks likeQualified, interested people handed overPlacements and starts
    TimeframeOften ahead of a reqDuring a req
    Example: a clean handoff
    You message a backend engineer, learn she is open to a change in three months, and record her interests in the CRM. When a matching req opens in March, the recruiter reads your notes and calls her the same day, with context already in hand.

    🔎 Active and passive candidates

    🙋 Active candidates

    Are looking now: they apply, post a CV, ask around. Many recruiters compete for them.

    🌱 Passive candidates

    Are employed and not looking, but may move for the right opportunity. This is where sourcers add the most value.

    Example: why passive matters
    A senior engineer is happy at her company and never visits job boards. A thoughtful message that mentions her open-source work and a problem your client is solving gets a reply: "I wasn't looking, but this sounds interesting." A job board would never have reached her.

    🏘️ Why developer communities

    Engineers gather where they learn, ask and help each other. Showing up thoughtfully in those places gives you access to people, signals about their skills and chances to build trust.

    PlaceWhat people do there
    Discord and Slack communitiesChat, ask for help, discuss tools and projects
    Stack OverflowAsk and answer programming questions
    GitHubShare code, contribute to open source
    Meetups and conferencesLearn, present, meet in person
    Hackathons and online eventsBuild quickly, show skills
    Example: seeing skill in action
    In a public channel you read an engineer explaining, clearly and patiently, how to fix a tricky database lock issue. You learn more about his skill and communication from that one answer than from a resume. You note his name for later, ready to reach out respectfully.

    🤲 Ground rules

    • Be transparent: say who you are and why you are writing.
    • Respect community rules: many spaces restrict recruiting or unsolicited messages.
    • Give before you ask: help, share, be useful.
    • Honour opt-outs immediately.
    • Use only public, professional information and follow data-protection law.
    Example: a rule that protects you
    A server's rules say "No unsolicited DMs from recruiters; use #jobs." You post one clear, honest message in #jobs and answer questions in the thread. Members who are interested contact you, and the moderators appreciate that you followed their rules.

    Sourcing Fundamentals

    The core skills behind every search: briefing yourself on the role, picturing the person, writing a good search, reading signals and keeping your records lawful and tidy.

    📝 Start with a sourcing brief

    Question for the recruiter or hiring managerWhy it matters
    What problem will this person solve?Gives you a story to tell candidates
    Must-have vs nice-to-have skills?Shapes search terms
    Equivalent skills accepted?Widens the pool wisely
    Location, remote policy, time zones?Filters realistically
    Pay range and what makes the role attractive?Lets you be honest and compelling
    Target companies and communities?Focuses your search
    Example: a brief that saves a week
    The hiring manager says: "We need a Rust engineer for low-latency payments. Go or C++ with strong systems experience would also work. Remote in Europe. Range published." You now know the core skill, acceptable alternatives, location and a message you can be open about.

    🧑‍💻 Personas

    A persona is a short picture of the type of person you want to reach: what they do, what they care about, where they spend time.

    PersonaCares aboutHangs out in
    Senior backend engineerScale, ownership, clean architectureLanguage communities, conferences, GitHub
    Data engineerTooling, data quality, modern stackData community Slack groups, meetups
    Early-career developerLearning, mentorship, growthBootcamp alumni groups, coding Discord servers
    Example: persona shapes the message
    For the senior backend persona you write about "owning the payments core and designing for scale". For the early-career persona you write about "mentorship and a clear growth path". Same company, different message, because they care about different things.

    Example: a signal that needs a conversation
    A candidate has no public code because her employer is a bank. You do not skip her. You mention her professional background in your message, ask about the problems she solves, and let the conversation show her skills.

    🏃 Sourcing sprints

    Day 3Send outreach to the first 20

    Example: a sprint result

    🔒 Privacy and legal basics

    • Collect only public, professional information, and only what you need.
    • Follow data-protection law (such as GDPR in Europe) and your company's policy.
    • Tell people who you are and how you found them.
    • Provide a simple opt-out and honour it immediately.
    • Do not filter by protected traits such as age, gender or ethnicity.
    📝
    Rules differ by country and platform and change over time. This is a general guide, not legal advice. Ask your legal or data-protection team about specific cases.
    Example: a lawful first message
    "I found your public conference talk and am contacting you about a role. If you'd prefer I did not contact you again, just let me know and I will remove your details." It states the source, the purpose and the opt-out in two sentences.

    🪤 Common beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • Sending the same message to hundreds of people
    • Judging only by popularity or follower numbers
    • Ignoring community rules
    • Never recording what you did

    ✅ Better

    • Fewer, more relevant messages
    • Judging by evidence of relevant work
    • Reading and following each space's rules
    • Logging every contact in the CRM
    Example: quality beats volume
    Sourcer A sends 200 generic messages and gets 4 replies (2%). Sourcer B sends 40 personalised messages and gets 12 replies (30%). B did a fifth of the sending, and produced three times as many conversations.

    Module 03 · Discord & Stack Overflow Sourcing

    Discord & Stack Overflow Sourcing

    Two of the richest places to see engineers think out loud. Learn how each works, the etiquette that keeps you welcome, and how to read the signals without crossing lines.

    📝
    Platform features, rules and terms change often. Always read the current rules and terms of service of each community before you act, and follow your company's policy.

    💬 How Discord works

    Discord is a chat platform organised into servers (communities), each with channels (topics). Members have roles, and moderators enforce the rules. Many developer communities run on it: language communities, open-source projects, bootcamps and tool vendors.

    TermMeaning
    ServerA community space, such as a Rust learners' server
    ChannelA topic area, such as #help, #show-and-tell or #jobs
    RoleA label with permissions, such as member or moderator
    DMA direct message to one person
    ThreadA focused sub-conversation under a message
    Example: reading a server map
    A Python server has channels #help, #code-review, #show-and-tell and #jobs. #help shows who answers questions well. #show-and-tell shows who builds things. #jobs is the one place recruiters may post. You use each channel for the right purpose.

    📜 Rules of engagement

    DoDon't
    Read the server rules and pinned messages firstJoin and immediately start sending DMs
    Use the designated jobs channel, or ask a moderator how to share rolesPost jobs in help or general channels
    Identify yourself and your company clearlyPretend to be a community member only to recruit
    Contribute value: answer questions, share resourcesOnly ever appear when you have a role to fill
    Respect "no" immediatelyMessage people again after they decline
    ⚠️
    Many servers ban unsolicited recruiter DMs. If the rules say so, do not send them. Ask the moderators what is allowed, and if nothing is allowed, find another route.
    Example: asking permission
    You message a moderator: "Hi, I'm a recruiter for a payments company hiring Rust engineers. Is there a way to share the role that fits your rules?" The moderator points you to #jobs and a monthly thread. You gain access and a good reputation with the community leaders.

    🗺️ Finding the right servers

    TypeExamples of what to look forGood for
    Language and framework communitiesRust, Python, JavaScript, Go serversSkill-specific talent
    Open-source project serversProject maintainers and contributorsDeep, proven engineers
    Learning and bootcamp communitiesStudy groups, alumni serversEarly-career talent
    Tool and vendor communitiesCloud, DevOps and data platform serversPractitioners using a stack
    Identity-based communitiesGroups for women, LGBTQ+ or regional engineersDiverse talent, with permission
    Example: choosing communities
    For a Rust payments role you shortlist the official Rust community server, two async-runtime project servers and a European Rust meetup group. You skip a general gaming server with thousands of members, because it has little Rust talent and many members are outside the target audience.

    🤝 A respectful approach

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    1. Observe before you speak. Learn the culture and the regulars.
    2. Contribute: share a salary-transparent job market insight, answer a question about hiring, or help with resumes where welcomed.
    3. Be open about who you are in your profile and your messages.
    4. Invite, don't chase: share the role in the right place and let interested people come to you.
    5. Move to private messages only when the person invites it or the rules allow it.
    Example: a value-first approach
    For two weeks you answer interview-prep questions in a community's #career channel. Members start to know your name. When you post a role in #jobs, three people reply publicly, and two DM you. Trust you earned first turned into warm leads.

    📚 Stack Overflow signals

    Stack Overflow is a question-and-answer site for programmers. Public profiles show tags a person answers in, answers others accepted or upvoted, and reputation points.

    SignalWhat it suggestsCaution
    Top tagsAreas of expertiseTags show what they answer, not their whole job
    Accepted and upvoted answersHelpful, correct explanationsPopular questions earn more votes
    Answer qualityClarity, depth, careRead a few, not just the count
    Reputation pointsLong-term participationReflects time and Q&A activity, not total ability
    Recent activityCurrent engagementBusy professionals may be quiet
    Example: quality over points
    Candidate A has 60,000 reputation, mostly from answering popular beginner questions years ago. Candidate B has 8,000, with detailed, recent answers on distributed systems. For a senior distributed-systems role, B is the stronger lead, despite the lower number.

    🔍 Reading a Stack Overflow profile

    StepLook atQuestion to ask yourself
    1Top tagsDo they match the role?
    2Three recent answersAre they clear, correct and kind?
    3Time activeAre they current?
    4Linked profiles (professional network, GitHub)Is there a public, professional place to contact them?
    5Site rulesDoes the platform allow the contact you plan?
    💡
    If a profile shows a professional link they chose to share, contact them there, and say how you found them. If no contact route is offered, respect that and do not look for private details.
    Example: contacting the right way
    A candidate's Stack Overflow profile links to his professional page. You send a short message there: "I read your answer on database locking in Postgres, which was clear and useful. I'm hiring for a team solving similar problems. Would you be open to a short chat?" You used the route he chose to share.

    🌐 Other developer spaces

    SpaceWhat you seeEtiquette note
    GitHubCode, issues, pull requestsDo not message through issue threads; use listed contact details
    Slack communitiesTopic groups and help channelsFollow each workspace's recruiting rules
    Developer blogs and newslettersWriting and opinionsComment thoughtfully; contact through the author's stated route
    Conference speaker listsExperts willing to be publicGreat for warm, respectful outreach
    Example: respecting the channel
    A maintainer's repository has an issue tracker. You do not send a recruiting message there, since that would interrupt project work. Instead you read his profile, find a contact email he chose to publish, and send a short, personal note to that address.

    🛡️ Protecting minors and privacy

    • Many public communities include teenagers. Never recruit or message anyone who may be under 18.
    • Contact only people who are clearly adult professionals, for example those with a professional profile and work history.
    • Do not collect or store personal details beyond what is public and professional.
    • If a person seems young or is unclear, leave them alone.
    Example: a clear boundary
    In a coding Discord you see a very capable member whose posts mention school exams. You do not contact him, however strong his skills. You keep to adults with professional profiles. A safe, simple rule protects young people and your reputation.

    🔬 Worked example

    Role: Senior Rust engineer, payments, remote in Europe.

    planWeek 1: join two Rust community servers; read rules; observe; answer 3 questions
Week 2: post the role in #jobs with salary range; reply to questions openly
Week 3: review Stack Overflow top answerers in the Rust tag with async focus
        (10 profiles); note recent, high-quality answers
Week 4: contact 8 people via their listed professional profiles with a
        personalised message; log everything in the CRM
    ✅
    Reading: the plan builds trust before outreach, follows each community's rules, uses public professional routes and keeps a record. Fewer, warmer contacts are more likely to produce replies than mass DMs.
    Example: results of the plan
    After four weeks: 3 public replies in #jobs, 2 DMs invited by members, and 8 personalised messages that bring 4 replies. Six conversations led to 3 qualified candidates and 2 handoffs. The community also now recognises you as a helpful hiring contact.

    Module 04 · Tech Community Networking

    Tech Community Networking

    The best sourcers are known and trusted in the communities they serve. Learn how to build real relationships, show up usefully and turn those relationships into a steady stream of warm candidates.

    🆚 Networking vs spamming

    🔴 Spamming

    • Appears only when you need something
    • Copy-paste messages to many
    • Ignores community norms
    • Disappears after no reply

    🟢 Networking

    • Shows up regularly and helpfully
    • Knows people by name and interest
    • Follows community norms
    • Keeps in touch even without a role
    Example: two reputations
    Sourcer A is known in a Python community as "the person who DMs everyone". Sourcer B is known as "the person who runs the monthly resume review and shares salary data". When both post roles, B receives a stream of replies while A is muted.

    🎁 Give before you ask

    Way to giveExample
    Share knowledgeA short post on how the hiring process works
    Offer helpA monthly resume or portfolio review session
    Share opportunitiesShare roles at other companies when they fit someone
    Support eventsSponsor a meetup, provide food or a venue
    Be transparentShare pay ranges and honest feedback
    Example: a monthly give
    Each month you host a 45-minute online "ask a recruiter anything" session in a community. Attendees get honest answers on resumes, salary expectations and interviews. Over six months, you are recognised by name, and 11 attendees later ask you about roles.

    🎪 Online and offline events

    Event typeValueHow to use it
    MeetupsRegular, local, relaxedAttend, chat, offer a venue or sponsorship
    ConferencesMany experts in one placeTalk to speakers and attendees; book follow-ups
    HackathonsSee skills in actionMentor, judge, sponsor a prize
    Online talks and AMAsReach distributed audiencesAsk good questions; host a session
    Open-source sprintsCollaboration on real codeSupport the maintainers, not just the attendees
    Example: a hackathon sponsor
    Your company sponsors a prize at a 48-hour hackathon, and you mentor two teams. You meet a team whose database design is clearly strong. You introduce yourself as a recruiter at the end, after the event, asking if they would like to hear about open roles. Two say yes.

    🤝 Community partners

    PartnerWhat they can do
    Community managers and moderatorsTell you what is allowed and where to post
    Developer advocates (DevRel) at your clientIntroduce you to their community and speakers
    Meetup organisersOffer sponsorship slots and announcements
    Bootcamps and universitiesConnect you to early-career talent
    Example: a partnership
    You ask your client's developer-relations lead to introduce you to the organisers of a local Go meetup. The organiser invites you to share a role for two minutes at the start of the next event, and the room of 80 engineers hears about it with the organiser's approval.

    🌟 Referrals and champions

    Trusted members who like your company or your approach can become champions, people who happily introduce you to others.

    StepWhat you do
    1Treat every conversation well, even when there is no fit
    2Ask: "Who is the best engineer you know in this area?"
    3Thank referrers and report back on outcomes
    4Respect referrers' wishes: never name them without permission
    Example: a referral chain
    A candidate declines but says, "A colleague of mine would be perfect, and I'll introduce you." You thank her, and the colleague becomes a qualified candidate. You send her a short note after the first call, which makes her more willing to refer others later.

    🌈 Inclusive networking

    • Support communities for under-represented groups as a genuine ally, not only when hiring.
    • Follow each community's code of conduct and ask before posting roles.
    • Use skills-based, inclusive language in role descriptions.
    • Share pay ranges to reduce unequal negotiation.
    • Never target or filter people by protected characteristics.
    Example: partnership, not extraction
    You help a women-in-engineering group by providing a venue and mentoring sessions. When you later share a role, the organisers share it with members, and several apply. Months of genuine support came before any request.

    Module 05 · Hyper-Personalised Outreach

    Hyper-Personalised Outreach

    Engineers receive dozens of recruiter messages a week and ignore almost all of them. Learn what makes a message worth answering, how to structure it, and how to measure and improve it honestly.

    🎯 What personalisation really is

    Personalisation is not inserting a first name. It is showing, with specific, relevant detail, that you read the person's work and have a real reason to contact them.

    🔴 Fake personalisation

    "Hi Alex, I came across your impressive profile and think you'd be a great fit for an exciting opportunity."

    🟢 Real personalisation

    "Hi Alex, your post on cutting Postgres lock waits by batching writes was very clear. We are solving a similar issue in payments, and I'd like to tell you about the team."

    Example: the swap test
    Remove the name from your message. If it could be sent to anyone, it is not personalised. A message that only makes sense for one person, because it refers to something they did, passes the swap test.

    🪜 Levels of personalisation

    LevelWhat it includesTypical reply rate (illustrative)
    1: Merge fieldsName and company only2% to 5%
    2: Role-awareSkills matched to the role8% to 12%
    3: Person-specificA reference to their work20% to 30%
    4: Value-basedSpecific work plus what is in it for them30% and above
    📝
    The percentages are illustrative. Real results vary by role, market and your own brand. Track your own numbers.
    Example: choosing the level
    For a broad pipeline of 500 front-end developers you use level 2 at scale. For your 20 dream candidates for a staff role you go to level 4, spending 20 minutes on each message. Effort follows value.

    🧩 Anatomy of a message

    PartPurposeExample
    Subject or opening lineEarn the first five seconds"Your talk on async timeouts"
    Why youA specific, honest reason"Your library solves the issue we have"
    The role in one lineClarity"Senior Rust engineer on payments core, remote in Europe"
    What is in it for themRelevance"Ownership of latency work; range: X to Y"
    Easy next stepLow-effort ask"Open to a 15-minute chat this week?"
    Opt-outRespect and compliance"If not, just say and I won't write again."
    Example: the 120-word message
    A message of about 120 words covers all six parts, and is read in 30 seconds. Messages over 250 words, with long company histories, are often skimmed or skipped. Short and specific beats long and thorough.

    ✉️ Good vs weak examples

    weak messageHi there,

I hope you're well! I'm hiring for an exciting opportunity at a fast-growing
company. We're looking for rockstar developers to join our amazing team.
Are you open to new opportunities? Let me know!
    strong messageSubject: Your talk on async timeouts

Hi Sam,

Your Go meetup talk on timeout handling under load changed how I think
about retries. I'm working with a payments team facing a similar problem,
and they're hiring a senior engineer to own the latency work (remote,
Europe, range 85k to 105k euros).

Would a 15-minute chat this week be useful? If not, no problem at all, just
tell me and I won't write again.

Thanks,
Priya
    Example: why the second works
    It shows she watched the talk, names a real problem, says the role and pay range in one line, and makes a small ask. The recipient can decide in under 30 seconds. The first message could go to anyone, so it is treated as spam.

    📡 Channels and timing

    ChannelStrengthNote
    Email (professional or published)Room for a clear messageUse only addresses the person chose to share
    Professional network messageContext and identity visibleOften the expected route
    Community DM (where allowed)Warm if you already contributedOnly where the rules permit
    Event follow-upHighest contextSend within 48 hours

    Timing matters: write when the person is likely to read, usually mid-week mornings in their time zone. Test and learn from your own data.

    Example: time zone care
    You are in London and the candidate is in San Francisco. A 9 a.m. London send arrives at 1 a.m. for her, and sits under other mail. You schedule it for her Tuesday morning, and the reply rate on scheduled sends is noticeably higher.

    🔁 Follow-up cadence

    Read left to right.
    TouchContent
    1The personalised message
    2 (day 3 to 4)A brief, polite nudge, perhaps with a relevant link
    3 (day 8 to 10)A final note that adds new value and says you will not keep writing
    Example: knowing when to stop
    After two follow-ups with no reply, you stop contacting that person for at least three months. A later, relevant role may find them in a better moment, and they will remember you as respectful, not pushy.

    💬 Handling replies

    ReplyBest response
    "Interested, tell me more"Reply quickly with details and offer two times for a call
    "Not now, maybe later"Thank them, note when to check in, and add to nurture
    "Not interested"Thank them, mark as not interested, ask if they know someone
    "Please stop contacting me"Apologise, stop and mark Do Not Contact immediately
    "How did you get my details?"Answer honestly and offer to delete them
    Example: a graceful decline
    A candidate replies, "Thanks, but I'm happy where I am." You write, "Understood, thank you for answering. If you ever want to hear about something similar, feel free to reach out. And if you know anyone in the Go community who might like the role, I'd be grateful for an introduction." Two weeks later she introduces a colleague.

    📊 Measuring and testing

    MetricFormulaExample
    Reply rateReplies ÷ messages sent15 ÷ 60 = 25%
    Positive reply rateInterested replies ÷ messages sent9 ÷ 60 = 15%
    Call conversionCalls booked ÷ messages sent6 ÷ 60 = 10%
    Handoff rateHandoffs ÷ calls4 ÷ 6 = 67%

    A/B testing compares two versions of a message on similar audiences. Change one thing at a time (subject line, opening, ask).

    Example: a simple A/B test
    Version A (generic opening) is sent to 100 people and receives 12 replies. Version B (opening that mentions their talk) is sent to a similar 100 and receives 22 replies. B did better in this test (22% vs 12%). You repeat it with another 100 people before changing your whole approach.

    ⚖️ Pitfalls and ethics

    🚫 Don't

    • Use private details (family, home address, personal accounts)
    • Pretend to know someone or invent a connection
    • Exaggerate the role or hide pay when asked
    • Use "creepy" detail that shows you tracked them

    ✅ Do

    • Reference public, professional work
    • Be honest about who you are and the role
    • Share pay ranges where you can
    • Respect opt-outs at once
    Example: where to stop
    You find a candidate's public conference talk and mention it. You see a photo on a private profile of her family holiday and decide not to mention it. Personalisation uses what a person shared professionally, not everything you can find.

    🔬 Worked example

    sprint of 60 messagesSent: 60    Replies: 15    Interested: 9    Calls booked: 6    Handoffs: 4

    ✅
    Reading: a 25% reply rate is healthy for person-specific outreach, and 4 handoffs from 60 messages is a good return on about 12 hours of work. The next improvement is to see what the 6 non-interested replies had in common (timing, pay, location) and fix the targeting.
    Example: what you would tell the recruiter
    "From 60 personalised messages we have 9 interested people, 6 booked calls and 4 qualified candidates for you. I'll send you notes on each: why they replied, what they want, and what to avoid."

    Module 06 · CRM Workflow Optimization

    CRM Workflow Optimization

    A talent CRM remembers relationships so you do not have to. Learn how to structure pipelines, tag and segment contacts, automate wisely, keep data clean and respect people's consent.

    🗃️ CRM vs ATS

    SystemHoldsUsed for
    Talent CRMPeople who may be hired in future: prospects, past candidates, community contactsBuilding and nurturing relationships
    ATSApplicants and candidates in active hiring processesManaging a live hiring workflow
    Example: two systems, one journey
    You meet an engineer at a meetup and record her in the CRM. Six months later she is interested in a role. She moves into the ATS as a candidate for that req, with your CRM notes attached. Nothing is lost between the "relationship" and the "hiring process".

    🛤️ Pipeline stages

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    StageMeaningNext action

    Before your pipeline had "Replied" and "Interested" as separate stages, every reply was treated the same. Now you see that of 15 replies, 9 are interested and 6 are polite declines. Your daily list shows only those who need a response today.

    Example: a segment in seconds
    A new req appears for a senior Rust engineer in Europe. In 20 seconds you pull up the segment: 34 contacts, 12 interested before. You write to those 12 first, with notes from your earlier conversations, and the first call is booked the same day.

    🤖 Sequences and automation

    AutomationHelps withGuardrail

    Reminders and tasksNurture check-ins, call back datesReview the task list weekly

    ⚠️
    Automate the boring parts (reminders, logging, follow-up timing), not the thinking and the personalisation. A message that begins "Hi {first_name}" because a field was empty destroys trust instantly.
    Example: a merge-field disaster avoided
    Before launching a sequence you send a test to yourself and see "Hi ," because 6 contacts have no first name. You fix the data and add a fallback ("Hi there") only for those six, and no candidate ever sees the error.

    🧹 Data hygiene

    HabitWhy
    Required fields (source, skill tags, consent status, last contact date)Makes every record usable
    De-duplicate regularlyAvoids contacting someone twice
    Log every touch, with date and summaryThe team knows the history
    Update records after callsInterests and timing change
    Remove or archive stale contactsKeeps data lawful and relevant
    Example: a duplicate that nearly cost trust
    Two recruiters message the same engineer within a day because her record exists twice under two email addresses. She replies, "Do you not talk to each other?" After a de-duplication run, and an ownership rule (one named owner per contact), it does not happen again.

    🔒 Consent and retention

    • Record where and when you got each contact, and the lawful basis you rely on, as your company's policy defines.
    • Keep a clear consent or opt-in status on each contact.
    • Set a retention period: review or delete records that are old and inactive.
    • Handle access and deletion requests promptly, with your data-protection team.
    • When someone opts out, remove personal details but keep a minimal suppression entry so they are not added again.
    📝
    Data-protection rules differ by country and change over time. This is a general guide, not legal advice. Follow your company's policy and ask your data-protection team about specific cases.
    Example: a clean opt-out
    A contact writes, "Please delete my data." You pass it to your data-protection team, remove her details from the CRM and lists, keep only a suppression entry with her email hash, and confirm in writing. She is never contacted again, and the request is documented.

    🎚️ Lead scoring

    Lead scoring ranks contacts so you write to the best fits first. Combine fit (do they match the role?) and engagement (are they interested?).

    FactorWeightScore (0 to 5)Points

    Contact A scores 4.25 and Contact B scores 2.60. You write to A first and keep B in nurture. The score does not replace judgement, but it helps you spend your limited time on the people most likely to respond and fit.

    🌱 Nurture workflows

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    TouchContent

    An engineer says in March, "Not now, I just started a project." You tag her "check in September", send one useful article in June and write in September. She replies, "Good timing, the project has ended." Persistence with value, not pressure, produced the conversation.

    MistakeGuardrail

    Sourcing Strategy & Analytics

    Move from "busy" to "effective": read the funnel in numbers, find which sources produce quality, plan pipelines before reqs arrive and report results in a way hiring teams trust.

    🔻 The sourcing funnel in numbers

    StageCountConversion
    Messages sent200-
    Replies5050 ÷ 200 = 25%
    Qualified2020 ÷ 50 = 40% of replies
    Submittals88 ÷ 20 = 40% of qualified

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: where to improve
    From 200 messages you get 2 hires, so the end-to-end rate is 1%. If you raise the reply rate from 25% to 30% (60 replies) and keep other stages the same, you would get 24 qualified, about 9.6 submittals and about 2.4 hires. Each stage gives you a lever, and small gains multiply.

    🔎 Source effectiveness

    💡
    Judge sources by quality (qualified and hires), not just volume. Referrals have the smallest list and the best rate. Also consider the time cost of each channel.
    Example: shifting effort
    Professional-network search gives 120 contacts but only 8% qualified, while referrals give 60%. You spend more time asking existing contacts for introductions, and keep searching as a secondary channel. The same hours now produce more qualified candidates.

    🧮 Pipeline coverage

    Coverage means having enough warm, qualified candidates ready for the reqs you expect. A simple target is a number of warm candidates per open req.

    Item (illustrative)Value
    Target warm candidates per open req5
    Open reqs12
    Warm candidates needed5 × 12 = 60
    Warm candidates now42
    Gap60 − 42 = 18
    Coverage42 ÷ 60 = 70%
    Example: closing a gap
    You are 18 candidates short for 12 reqs, and most of the gap is in Go and data roles. You plan two sprints for those skills, ask your champions for referrals, and move from 70% to 95% coverage in three weeks, before the recruiters complain.

    🗺️ Market mapping

    QuestionHow to find out
    Where do these engineers work?Target-company lists, conference speakers, community members
    How many exist in our location?Profile search counts (estimates only)
    Which companies are laying off or growing?News and public announcements
    What do they care about?Conversations, community discussions, talks
    Example: a map that changes the plan
    Mapping shows only about 400 senior Rust engineers in your time zone and half are at five companies. You adjust the plan: smaller, more personal outreach to the 400, plus adjacent Go and C++ engineers. The map told you volume would never work for this role.

    🏃 Planning a sprint

    StepExample
    Goal5 qualified, interested candidates for a senior Go role
    Expected reply rate25%
    Expected qualified rate (of replies)40%
    Messages needed5 ÷ 0.40 = 12.5 replies; 12.5 ÷ 0.25 = 50 messages
    Time per personalised message12 minutes, so 10 hours
    Example: planning backwards from the goal
    The goal is 5 qualified candidates. At a 25% reply rate and 40% of replies qualified, you need about 50 messages. At 12 minutes each, that is 10 hours of work, which fits in a week. If the plan needed 40 hours, you would adjust the scope or ask for help early.

    🧪 Experiments

    1. Pick one change (subject line, opening, ask, send time).
    2. Send both versions to similar audiences at the same time.
    3. Use a large-enough sample; tiny samples mislead.
    4. Compare results; repeat before making a big change.
    5. Record what you learned in a shared playbook.
    Example: a sample-size warning
    Version A gets 3 replies from 10 messages (30%); Version B gets 2 from 10 (20%). The difference is one reply, which could be luck. You repeat the test with 50 messages each before deciding, and find the two versions perform almost identically.

    🌈 Diversity sourcing

    • Use skills-based criteria, not school names or employer brands alone.
    • Partner with communities and bootcamps that serve under-represented groups, as an ally.
    • Review your funnel for drop-off points across groups (with HR, using lawful, aggregated data).
    • Write inclusive role descriptions and share pay ranges.
    • Never target or exclude people by protected characteristics.
    Example: widening the funnel
    Searching only for people from five well-known companies produced a narrow list. Adding open-source contributors and bootcamp alumni, selected by demonstrated skills, doubled the pool and raised the share of candidates from under-represented backgrounds in the qualified stage.

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Rare Skills, Reputation & Judgement

    What separates a good sourcer from a trusted one: finding people with rare skills, protecting your reputation in communities, using AI responsibly, balancing scale against care and handling difficult moments calmly.

    💎 Sourcing rare skills

    TechniqueHow it works
    Adjacent skillsLook for people who do something close (C++ or Go for Rust) and could learn the rest
    Project and contributor mappingFind maintainers and contributors of the open-source projects the skill depends on
    Conference and paper mappingSpeakers and authors working on the exact problem
    Community championsAsk trusted insiders who is strongest, and who is quietly looking
    Early-career growth pathTrain promising people with mentorship if the client agrees
    Example: finding a rare expert
    A client needs a database-internals engineer. Searching titles returns almost nobody. You map contributors to two well-known database projects, find 14 active ones, and read their public work. Four are in an acceptable time zone, and two reply, one of them says he has been thinking of a change.

    🛡️ Community conflicts and reputation

    SituationBest response
    A moderator warns you about DMsThank them, stop immediately, read the rules again and ask what is allowed
    A member complains publiclyRespond briefly and politely; apologise if you made a mistake; take it to a moderator if needed
    You are banned from a serverAccept it; do not create new accounts; reflect and fix your approach elsewhere
    A colleague spams a community you work inRaise it with them and your lead, and repair relations with the moderators
    💡
    In small communities, one bad message can follow you for years, and your company's brand with you. Reputation is a long-term asset, so protect it above any single lead.
    Example: repairing trust
    You send two DMs in a server that bans them, and a moderator warns you. You reply, "You are right, I misread the rules. Sorry about that. I'll use #jobs only." You post nothing else for a week, then contribute to a help thread. The moderator later tells you the monthly jobs thread is open to you.

    🤖 AI tools in sourcing

    Good useRisk to manage
    Drafting first versions of messages for you to editGeneric text that sounds fake; fix by adding real, checked details
    Summarising a candidate's public work to find a hookErrors or invented details; always verify against the source
    Building search strings and ideasWrong syntax or unsuitable terms; test them
    Automating logging and remindersWrong data; spot-check the output
    • Keep a human in the loop for anything a candidate will read.
    • Never invent personal details or fake connections.
    • Be honest: do not pretend a machine-written message is a personal note about something you did not read.
    • Protect data: do not paste candidates' personal details into tools your company has not approved.
    Example: catching an AI error
    An AI draft says "I loved your talk at KubeCon 2024", but the candidate never spoke there. You check the source, find the real talk was a regional meetup, and correct it. Without the check, the message would have shown immediately that nobody had read her work.

    ⚖️ Fairness and legal risk

    • Skills-based criteria only: do not filter by names, photos, age, gender, ethnicity or other protected traits.
    • Follow data-protection laws and each platform's terms.
    • Be careful with scraping and automation: many platforms prohibit it.
    • Keep records of your search logic and outreach so decisions can be explained.
    • Never contact minors, or anyone who may be under 18.
    📝
    Laws and platform rules differ by country and change over time. This is a general guide, not legal advice. Check with your legal and data-protection team for specific cases.
    Example: fixing a biased filter
    A search for "digital native" and "recent graduate" in a role that does not need them filters out older engineers. You replace it with a skills-based search ("Kubernetes AND CI/CD"), and the list becomes both fairer and stronger for the role.

    ⚖️ Scale vs personalisation

    SegmentApproachTime per contact
    Priority (top 10%)Fully personalised, level 4 messages15 to 20 minutes
    Core (next 30%)Person-specific hook, template structure5 to 8 minutes
    Broad (rest)Role-aware message, careful segmentation1 to 2 minutes
    Example: a tiered plan
    A hiring manager asks you to message 2,000 engineers. You propose tiers: 100 priority contacts get deeply personalised messages, 500 get a person-specific hook, and a test batch of 100 from the broad group is sent to measure results before scaling. You achieve far better replies than a blast, with less risk to your reputation.

    📖 Full case study

    Req: Staff Rust engineer, payments core, remote in Europe. 6 weeks. Very few candidates.

    1. Brief and map: the market holds about 400 senior Rust engineers in time zone; half work at five companies.
    2. Communities: two Rust Discord servers and a European meetup; you read the rules, ask moderators and post the role with pay range in the allowed channel.
    3. Contribution first: for two weeks you host a monthly career Q&A and answer questions.
    4. Targeted outreach: 30 personalised messages to maintainers and speakers through listed professional routes.
    5. Results: 11 replies (37%), 7 interested, 5 qualified, 4 handed to the recruiter. Two referrals arrive from the community.
    6. CRM: everything logged, three people tagged for nurture, one opt-out honoured.
    7. Outcome: 1 offer accepted in week 6; the community now treats you as a trusted contact.
    ✅
    Why it worked: mapping a small market, following community rules, giving before asking, truly personalised outreach and careful CRM follow-up, rather than volume.
    Example: what you would tell the hiring manager
    "The market for this role is about 400 people, so I focused on 30 high-fit contacts rather than a blast. Eleven replied, five qualified and four are with the recruiter now. Two referrals also came from the community, and three more are in nurture for the next role."
