    The Role & Staffing Leadership Basics

    Before you design an offshore strategy or tune gross margin, understand what a Director of Technology Staffing owns, how the staffing business makes money, and the words every leadership conversation uses.

    🧭 What does a director do?

    A Director of Technology Staffing leads a technology staffing business unit and is accountable for its results: revenue, gross margin, client satisfaction and the health of the team. You set direction, build the leaders beneath you, and make sure delivery, sales and operations work together.

    🚚
    Delivery management
    Make sure reqs are filled well, on time, across many teams and clients.
    🌍
    Offshore and nearshore strategy
    Decide where and how to deliver, to widen talent and improve economics.
    📊
    Gross margin
    Protect and grow the profit that each hour billed leaves behind.
    🤝
    Enterprise engagement
    Build executive relationships with the largest clients.
    Example: a day in the role
    At 8:30 a.m. you review the business-unit dashboard: revenue is on plan, margin is a point low. At 10 a.m. you meet delivery managers about a client SLA risk. At 1 p.m. you join an executive call with a bank's head of technology. At 4 p.m. you review a nearshore pilot proposal with your finance partner.

    Example: one hour, one contractor
    A client pays $100 for an hour of a contractor's work. Pay and costs for that hour are $84, leaving $16 of gross profit. Multiply that by hundreds of contractors and thousands of hours a month, and you see why small changes in rate, cost or utilisation move the whole business.

    ⚖️ Director vs other roles

    RoleFocusQuestion they answer
    RecruiterOne req at a time"Can I fill this?"
    Delivery leadTeam speed and quality"Are we hitting our SLAs?"
    Account directorOne or a few large clients"Is this client healthy and growing?"
    Director of technology staffingThe whole business unit"Are we growing profitably, and can we sustain it?"
    Example: different questions on the same day
    A req breaches its SLA. The recruiter fixes the req. The delivery lead fixes the process. The account director calls the client. The director asks: "Is this a one-off, or a sign that our capacity in this skill is too thin across the unit?" and decides whether to invest.

    💰 Where the money is made

    ServiceHow it earnsTypical profile
    Contract staffingMargin on every hour billedSteady, volume-based, lower margin per hour
    Permanent placementA one-time fee as a share of salaryLumpy, high margin per placement
    Managed services and SOW workPrice for outcomes, with delivery riskHigher margin potential, higher risk
    Example: a mix that improves profit
    A unit earns $24M, almost all from contract staffing at about 14% gross margin. Over two years it adds managed teams and permanent placements to 25% of revenue at higher margins. Overall margin rises without winning a single extra contract-staffing client.

    Module 02 · The Staffing Business Model

    The Staffing Business Model

    The maths behind everything a director decides: unit economics, the P&L, fixed and variable costs, productivity, cash, and the levers that grow profit.

    📝
    All figures in this course are illustrative, chosen to make the maths clear. Real margins, costs and fees vary by firm, country and client. Use your finance team's real numbers for decisions.

    🔢 Unit economics

    Start with one contractor for one hour.

    Item (W2 contractor, illustrative)Per hour
    Bill rate (revenue)$100.00
    Pay rate$70.00
    Burden (20% of pay)$14.00
    Direct cost$84.00
    Gross profit$16.00
    Gross margin16 ÷ 100 = 16%
    Per contractor per month (160 hours)$16 × 160 = $2,560 gross profit
    Example: scaling the unit
    200 contractors at $2,560 gross profit each is $512,000 of gross profit a month. If the average margin slips by one point (from 16% to 15%), the same 200 contractors generate $32,000 less each month, about $384,000 a year. Small slips become large numbers at scale.

    💧 The P&L waterfall

    Line (monthly, illustrative)AmountShare of revenue
    Revenue$2,000,000100%
    Direct costs (contractor pay, burden, program fees)−$1,700,00085%
    Gross profit$300,00015%
    Operating expenses (recruiters, sales, tools, rent)−$220,00011%
    Operating profit$80,0004%
    Example: where profit disappears
    A 15% gross margin sounds healthy, but only 4% remains as operating profit. If gross margin slips from 15% to 14% ($20,000 less a month) while costs stay the same, operating profit falls from $80,000 to $60,000, a 25% drop. Margin discipline matters far more than it first appears.

    🧱 Fixed and variable costs

    TypeBehaviourExamples
    VariableRises and falls with billable hoursContractor pay, burden, program fees
    FixedStays about the same in the short termRecruiter salaries, tools, rent, management
    💡
    When revenue grows, fixed costs spread across more revenue, so operating margin can improve even if gross margin stays flat. When revenue falls, the reverse happens, so cost planning matters in downturns.
    Example: operating leverage
    Revenue grows 10% from $2.0M to $2.2M a month at 15% gross margin, so gross profit rises to $330,000. Fixed operating costs rise only to $230,000. Operating profit climbs from $80,000 to $100,000, a 25% increase from 10% growth.

    Contract staffing is 75% of revenue but only 55% of gross profit (2.52 of 4.60). Permanent placement is 8% of revenue but 26% of gross profit (1.20 of 4.60). That is why directors look at profit by service, not only at total revenue.

    💵 Cash and working capital

    ItemWhy it matters
    DSO (days sales outstanding)How long clients take to pay
    Payroll cycleYou pay W2 staff weekly or bi-weekly, long before clients pay
    Working capitalCash needed to bridge the gap
    Credit riskA large client failing to pay can hurt badly
    Example: growth consumes cash
    A unit pays $1.4M a month in contractor pay while clients pay at about 55 days. It must fund roughly 1.8 months of payroll (about $2.5M) before the cash arrives. Faster growth means a larger cash need, so a profitable unit can still run short of cash.

    🚀 Growth levers

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    LeverHow it helps
    Fill rateMore of the reqs you already receive become revenue
    Bill rate and mixMore revenue and profit per hour
    UtilisationFewer idle, paid consultants

    Example: the cheapest growth
    Raising fill rate from 40% to 46% on 1,000 reqs a year adds 60 extra placements, worth far more than winning a small new client. The reqs were already coming in, and the effort went into delivery, not selling.

    ⚖️ Break-even thinking

    Item (illustrative)Value
    Monthly fixed operating costs$220,000
    Gross margin15%
    Break-even revenue$220,000 ÷ 0.15 = about $1,466,667 a month
    Current revenue$2,000,000
    Margin of safety(2,000,000 − 1,466,667) ÷ 2,000,000 = 26.7%
    Example: how far can revenue fall?
    Revenue can drop about 26.7% before operating profit hits zero, assuming costs stay the same. If your largest client (30% of revenue) left, you would be below break-even, so client concentration is a profit risk as well as a revenue risk.

    🪤 Common beginner traps

    ❌ Mistake

    • "Revenue growth is always good."
    • "Gross margin and operating margin are the same."
    • "A profitable business always has enough cash."

    ✅ Reality

    • Growth at low margin can reduce profit.
    • Gross margin ignores operating costs; operating margin includes them.
    • Growth and slow payment can create a cash gap.
    Example: growth that lost money
    A unit wins a big client by cutting rates and revenue rises 30%, but margin falls from 16% to 11%. Because operating costs rise to support the volume, the unit makes less operating profit than before. Growth only helps if the margin and costs allow it.

    Module 03 · Technology Staffing Delivery Management

    Delivery Management at Scale

    Running one team well is a skill. Running many teams, clients and locations consistently is a system. Learn how directors structure delivery, plan capacity, measure productivity and spot risk before clients do.

    ⚙️ From teams to a delivery system

    StageWhat it looks likeDirector's job
    Single teamA lead knows every reqHire well and coach
    Several teamsDifferent habits and results appearSet common standards and metrics
    A delivery systemConsistent process, data and leadershipDesign the operating model and develop leaders
    Example: when growth creates inconsistency
    A unit grows from 2 teams to 6. Team A fills 55% of reqs and Team D only 28%. The directors' job is to find out why (training, req mix, process, tools) and apply what Team A does well to everyone else, rather than blaming Team D.

    🏗️ Operating models

    ModelHow it worksBest for
    Client podsA team dedicated to one large clientBig accounts needing deep knowledge
    Skill podsTeams specialise by skill (cloud, data, security)Hard-to-fill roles across clients
    Centralised sourcingA sourcing team feeds all recruitersPipelines and efficiency
    HybridClient pods plus shared sourcing and specialist poolsMost mid-sized and large units
    Example: choosing a model
    A unit serves three big banks and 20 smaller clients. It creates one pod for each bank, one shared pod for the smaller clients, and a central sourcing team for cloud and data skills. Big accounts get depth, small ones get efficiency, and hard skills are not duplicated.

    🧾 Ownership with RACI

    Decision or taskRecruiterDelivery leadAccount directorDirector
    Submit candidatesRAI-
    Resolve an SLA breachRRAI
    Approve a rate below the floor-CRA
    Open a nearshore centre-CCA

    R = Responsible (does it), A = Accountable (owns the outcome), C = Consulted, I = Informed.

    Example: ending the "who decides?" delay
    A rate exception sat for four days because nobody was sure who could approve it. After agreeing the RACI, the account director proposes and the director approves within one business day. Clear ownership removes delay and avoids hidden decisions.

    🧮 Capacity planning

    New recruiters take about three months to become fully productive, so hiring when the capacity gap appears is already too late. You plan hires one quarter ahead of expected demand, even though it costs a little margin short term.

    📏 Productivity and quality

    MeasureWhy it matters
    Fill rateShare of reqs that become starts
    Time to first submittalSpeed
    Submittal-to-hire ratioQuality and efficiency
    Gross profit per recruiterProductivity that includes margin
    Early terminationsQuality of match and onboarding

    Example: quality hiding behind speed
    A team has the fastest submittal time but the highest early-termination rate: 18% of its contractors leave within 30 days. Faster is not better if matches are poor. You add a pre-submittal quality check, and early terminations fall to 9% with only a small increase in time.

    A recruiter who takes 6 months to ramp instead of 3 costs about 3 extra months of low output. Across ten new hires a year, a structured ramp that saves even a month each recovers 10 recruiter-months, equal to almost one full-time recruiter.

    ⚠️ Risk management

    RiskEarly signMitigation
    Key-person dependencyOne person holds a major client relationshipShared ownership and documentation
    Client concentrationOne client above about a third of revenueDiversify and build contingency plans
    Recruiter attrition spikeRising resignations in one teamExit interviews, pay review, workload check
    Compliance failureMissing checks or classification errorsAudits, training, system controls
    Tool or system outageSlow or failing ATS or VMS accessBackup procedures and support contracts
    Example: a key-person risk
    One account manager handles a $4M client alone, and the client's executive sponsor knows only her. You add a second contact, document the account plan and introduce the delivery lead to the sponsor. When she is on leave for three weeks, nothing slips.

    ⚖️ Standardise or flex?

    🧱 Standardise

    • Core metrics and definitions
    • Compliance steps
    • Reporting templates
    • Ramp-up and training

    🎛️ Allow flexibility

    • Sourcing tactics by skill
    • Client-specific rules
    • Team rituals
    • Local market practices
    Example: a good balance
    Every team uses the same definition of "first submittal" and the same compliance checklist. Each team may choose how it sources: one uses community networking for niche skills, another uses a job board for volume. Common numbers, local methods.

    Module 04 · Offshore & Nearshore Staffing Strategy

    Offshore & Nearshore Staffing Strategy

    Where you deliver changes your cost, your talent pool and your risk. Learn the models, the economics, the compliance questions and a careful way to pilot before you commit.

    📝
    Laws, taxes and market conditions vary by country and change over time. This module explains the thinking and the questions to ask; it is not legal or tax advice. Involve legal, tax and finance advisers before committing.

    🌍 Onshore, nearshore, offshore

    TermMeaningExample for a US client
    OnshoreDelivered in the client's own countryEngineers based in the US
    NearshoreDelivered in a nearby country with a similar time zoneEngineers in Latin America
    OffshoreDelivered far away, often with a large time differenceEngineers in South Asia or Eastern Europe
    Example: same client, three footprints
    A US bank has engineers in New York (onshore), a partner team in Mexico (nearshore) and a support team in India (offshore). Each has different cost, overlap with the bank's working hours and complexity of management.

    🎯 Why go beyond onshore

    💰
    Cost
    Lower total cost can improve margin and help clients save.
    🌐
    Talent access
    Skills that are scarce or expensive at home.
    🕒
    Coverage
    Support across more hours of the day.
    📈
    Scale
    Capacity for large or fast-growing programs.
    Example: a talent-driven decision
    A client needs 25 data engineers in six weeks. The onshore market can supply about eight in that time at high rates. A nearshore partner can supply 17 more in a similar time zone. Without a distributed model you would have to decline most of the order.

    🧩 Delivery models

    ModelWhat it isExample
    Offshore recruiting centreYour own sourcing and recruiting team working abroad for onshore reqsA team in Manila screens candidates for US roles
    Offshore or nearshore talent deliveryPlacing engineers located abroad with a clientTen engineers in Poland join a client team remotely
    Managed teamYou run a team abroad against agreed outcomesAn 8-person QA team in India
    Build-operate-transfer (BOT)You build and run a team, then hand it to the clientA client-owned centre after 24 months
    HybridA mix of onshore leads with nearshore and offshore engineers2 onshore architects plus 8 nearshore developers
    Example: a hybrid that clients like
    A client keeps two onshore architects who own design decisions and works with eight nearshore developers who build. Overlap hours allow daily collaboration, and the client's cost per delivered feature falls by about a quarter.

    🧮 Comparing the economics

    Item per hour (illustrative)OnshoreNearshore
    Bill rate to the client$100.00$70.00
    Direct cost (pay, burden, local costs)$84.00$52.00
    Gross profit$16.00$18.00
    Gross margin16%18 ÷ 70 = 25.7%
    Client saving per hour-$30, or 30%
    ⚠️
    Do not compare headline margin only. Nearshore and offshore also need management time, tools, security, recruiting, travel, ramp-up and extra quality control. Include those costs before deciding the real margin.
    Example: counting hidden costs
    A nearshore team of 20 looks like 25.7% margin. After adding $12,000 a month for management, tools and security, gross profit on the team falls from $57,600 (20 × 160 hours × $18) to $45,600, and margin falls to about 20.3% on $224,000 of revenue. Still attractive, but not as much as the headline.

    🕒 Time zone and communication

    Location (to a US East client)Typical overlap with working hoursStyle of work that fits
    Nearshore (Latin America)Most or all of the dayDaily collaboration, agile teams
    Eastern EuropeA few hours in the morningMixed: overlap windows plus independent work
    South or Southeast AsiaLimited, often early morning or eveningFollow-the-sun support, well-specified work
    Example: designing the overlap
    A team in Eastern Europe has a four-hour overlap with a US client. The team schedules stand-ups and design discussions in the overlap, and does focused coding outside it. Writing clear tickets and documents becomes essential, and productivity improves once the habit is built.

    🛡️ Compliance and risk

    AreaQuestion to ask
    Employment lawEmployees, contractors or an employer-of-record service in that country?
    Worker classificationDoes the local relationship look like genuine self-employment?
    Tax and permanent establishmentCould having people there create a taxable presence?
    Data protectionCan candidate and client data legally move across borders?
    IP and confidentialityAre local agreements enforceable and clear on ownership?
    SecurityDoes the location meet the client's security and audit requirements?
    Currency and political riskHow would exchange rates or events affect costs and delivery?
    Example: a risk found before it cost money
    Before opening a team in a new country, you ask a local adviser about tax. The answer: sending a senior manager there for long periods could create a permanent establishment and a tax liability. You restructure the plan so management is remote, and avoid an expensive surprise.

    📑 Commercial models

    ModelHow you are paidRisk
    Time and materials (staff augmentation)Hourly or monthly rate per personLow delivery risk; margin depends on utilisation
    Managed teamFixed monthly fee for a team and service levelsYou carry delivery risk; higher margin potential
    Fixed priceOne price for a deliverableHigh if scope is unclear
    BOTFees during operation and a transfer feeNeeds trust and a clear exit plan
    Example: matching model to maturity
    A new client starts with time-and-materials for six months while both sides learn to work together. Once delivery is stable you move the team to a managed-team contract with service levels. The client gets predictability and you earn a higher margin for taking on outcomes.

    Example: a pilot with clear exit criteria
    You start with 6 nearshore engineers for one client for four months. Success measures: delivery on time, client satisfaction of 4 out of 5 or higher, attrition under 10% and gross margin of at least 22%. The pilot hits three of the four, and you scale with a fix for the fourth.

    🧭 Governance of a distributed team

    • Local leadership: a strong delivery manager in each location.
    • Common KPIs and tools: the same dashboard and definitions everywhere.
    • Regular cross-site rituals: weekly reviews and visits when practical.
    • Fair treatment: pay, workload and recognition that match local law and good practice.
    • Documentation: processes written so they work across cultures and time zones.
    Example: unified metrics, local leaders
    Offshore and onshore teams are measured on the same fill rate, quality and satisfaction scores. The local manager in each centre adapts working hours and coaching to local culture. When the numbers are shared, the teams see themselves as one unit rather than "us and them".

    Module 05 · Gross Margin Optimization

    Gross Margin Optimization

    Margin is the result of hundreds of small decisions. Learn the levers, how to find leaks, how to price a cut honestly and how to build habits that protect profit without damaging service.

    📝
    All figures are illustrative. Use your own finance data for real decisions.

    ➗ The margin equation

    Gross margin = (Revenue − Direct costs) ÷ Revenue. Every improvement comes from raising revenue per hour, cutting direct cost per hour, or changing the mix toward higher-margin work.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    LeverDirectionEffect
    Bill rate upRevenue up, cost the sameMargin up
    Pay or burden downCost down, revenue the sameMargin up
    Mix to higher-margin servicesAverage margin upMargin up
    Bench time downIdle cost removedMargin up
    Example: one point on a large base
    On $24M of annual revenue, each point of gross margin is worth $240,000. Moving from 15% to 17% adds 2 × $240,000 = $480,000 of gross profit a year, with no extra contractors at all.

    💲 Price levers

    LeverHowWatch out for
    Rate-card disciplinePrice to a margin floor; require approval for exceptionsExceptions that quietly become the norm
    Annual escalatorsPass on cost rises through planned increasesClients resisting automatic rises
    Premium for scarce skillsCharge more for hard rolesClient budget limits
    Volume and rebate designGive rebates only against real volumeRebates given but volume never arrives
    Fees and pass-throughsPrice program fees into ratesForgotten fee leakage
    Example: escalator value
    On $12M of annual revenue under contracts with a 3% annual escalator, the extra billings are about $360,000 a year. If wage inflation was 4%, the escalator offsets most of it; without it, cost rises would eat about 3 points of margin.

    🧮 Cost levers

    LeverHowWatch out for
    Pay-rate disciplineOffer fair, market-informed pay; avoid overpaying to win quicklyUnderpaying causes attrition and poor quality
    Burden managementReview benefits, insurance and admin costs regularlyCutting benefits so far that you lose people
    Contract model mixUse W2, C2C or 1099 appropriately and compliantlyChoosing a model for margin when it does not fit the facts
    Delivery locationUse nearshore or offshore where it suits the workHidden management and quality costs
    Program feesNegotiate fees and fee structuresFees embedded in contracts you cannot change
    Example: a careful cost saving
    A review finds the firm is paying for three overlapping insurance policies. Consolidating them saves 0.4 points of burden on $14M of annual pay, about $56,000 a year, with no change in cover. This is the kind of saving that harms nobody.

    Early terminations and replacementsPoor matches leave quicklyBetter screening, onboarding
    Billing errorsMissed hours, wrong rates, rejected invoicesThree-way match, audits

    Example: finding a leak
    An audit shows overtime paid to W2 consultants was not being billed to one client. Over a year it cost $75,000. Adding an overtime clause to the contract and a billing check removed the leak, and the client agreed because the terms were reasonable.

    ✂️ Price cuts and volume

    A price cut needs more volume just to break even on gross profit.

    Item (illustrative)BeforeAfter a 3% price cut
    Price per hour$100.00$97.00
    Cost per hour$85.00$85.00
    Gross profit per hour$15.00 (15%)$12.00 (12.4%)
    Volume needed to keep the same total gross profit-15 ÷ 12 = 1.25, so 25% more volume
    💡
    A 3% price cut sounds small, but at a 15% margin it removes 20% of gross profit per hour (from $15 to $12). Always translate a discount into the volume you would need to win to stay whole.
    Example: answering a discount request
    A client asks for 3% off and promises "more volume". You calculate that you would need 25% more hours just to break even. You offer a tiered rebate that starts only above a 25% rise in hours, so the discount is earned and the unit's profit is protected.

    For Client C you choose to renegotiate: a 2-point rate increase at renewal, an escalator, and tighter overtime and fee terms. If it reaches 11% margin on $6M, gross profit rises by $0.12M. If the client refuses, you decide whether to keep the volume at low margin or to redeploy capacity to better clients.

    🏛️ Governance for margin

    • Margin floors by service and client segment, with approval levels.
    • Monthly margin review by client, service and team, with owners for actions.
    • Pricing committee for big bids and exceptions.
    • Incentives tied to gross profit, not revenue alone.
    • Margin dashboards that everyone can see.
    ⚠️
    Margin optimisation must never damage compliance, fair pay or service quality. Cutting corners can create bigger costs through penalties, turnover and lost clients.
    Example: a floor with an exception route
    The floor is 12%. A bid at 10% to win a strategic client goes to the pricing committee with a plan to reach 14% in 12 months through mix and escalators. It is approved with conditions. The exception is visible, time-limited and reviewed, rather than hidden.

    12-month margin plan on $24M revenue (starting margin 15%, gross profit $3.6M)Escalators and pricing discipline:       +0.8 points   = +$192,000

    Example: how you would present it
    "Our plan lifts gross margin from 15% to about 17.2% over twelve months through four steps, each with an owner and a measure. None relies on cutting pay or compliance, and the biggest single lever is rate discipline."

    🚨 Handling executive escalations

    6. Fix the system so the same issue does not return.
    Example: a calm response

    Leadership, Talent & Operations

    A director's results come through other people. Learn how to build leaders, design the organisation, align incentives, keep good people, forecast with finance and run a compliant, efficient operation.

    👥 Building the leadership team

    LeaderOwnsQualities to look for
    Delivery managersFill rate, speed, quality for a team or clientOperational discipline; coaching ability
    Account directorsClient relationships and growthExecutive presence; commercial judgement
    Offshore or nearshore centre headLocal team and deliveryCross-cultural leadership; local network
    Operations and compliance leadOnboarding, payroll, riskAccuracy; calm under pressure

    Example: promoting the right person
    A top recruiter is promoted to delivery manager. She is excellent at filling reqs but struggles to coach others. You give her a coaching programme, a mentor and a smaller team to start. Promotion based only on personal results often fails without development.

    🏗️ Org design

    Design questionGuidance
    Span of control (direct reports per manager)About 6 to 10 for managers who also coach and review work
    Layers between recruiter and directorAs few as possible, so decisions stay close to clients
    Client-aligned or skill-aligned teamsMix: client pods for big accounts, skill pods for scarce skills

    Example: span of control
    A manager with 18 direct reports cannot coach any of them properly. You split the team into two groups of 9 under two team leads. Coaching conversations double, and the manager now spends time on clients and hiring.

    🎯 Incentives that fit the strategy

    Incentive designWhat it encourages
    Commission on revenue onlyChasing volume, even at low margin
    Commission on gross profitHealthy margin and pricing discipline
    Bonus including quality and retentionGood matches and extensions
    Team bonus for shared targetsCollaboration across pods
    Penalty only for mistakesFear and hiding of problems (avoid)
    Example: aligning pay with profit
    A unit pays account managers on revenue and sees rates drifting down. After switching to commission on gross profit, with a quality modifier from client satisfaction, average margin on new deals rises from 12% to 15% within two quarters.

    📈 Performance and development

    • Clear goals using balanced measures: speed, quality, outcomes and process hygiene.
    • Regular 1:1s and feedback, not only annual reviews.
    • Development paths: senior recruiter, team lead, account roles, specialist tracks.
    • Succession planning: know who could step into each key role within 6 and 18 months.
    • Fair treatment: consistent standards, transparent promotion criteria.
    Example: a succession chart that worked
    You map six key roles and identify for each a ready-now and a ready-in-12-months successor. When an account director resigns on short notice, a prepared deputy steps in with a handover plan. The clients experience continuity rather than a gap.

    🌏 Leading across cultures

    • Learn local norms about feedback, hierarchy and communication styles.

    • Visit and listen when practical, and invest in relationships, not only reports.
    Example: a small change with big effect
    Your weekly call is always at 9 p.m. for the offshore team. You rotate the time every month so that onshore and offshore share the inconvenience. The offshore manager tells you it was the first time she felt the schedule treated her team as equals.

    Advanced: Strategy, Risk & Judgement

    What separates a good director from a trusted leader: managing a portfolio of clients, planning for shocks, choosing whether to build, partner or buy, handling crises calmly and growing responsibly.

    🗂️ Portfolio strategy

    Client typeStrategyExample
    High margin, high growthInvest and expandAdd managed teams and executive coverage
    High margin, low growthProtect and maintainKeep service strong; steady pricing
    Low margin, high growthFix pricing and mixRenegotiate terms; add higher-margin services
    Low margin, low growthReprice, simplify or exitMove to a low-touch model or politely decline renewals
    Example: a hard but sensible decision
    A client brings $3M of revenue at 7% margin, with flat growth. After a repricing proposal is declined, you move it to a low-touch service model and stop assigning senior recruiters to it. Freed capacity is redeployed to a growing client at 17% margin, and the unit's overall profit rises.

    ⚠️ Concentration risk

    Item (illustrative)Value

    💡

    Example: a diversification plan
    The largest client is 35% of revenue. You set a goal: below 28% in 18 months, by growing five mid-size clients by 20% a year and winning two new strategic accounts. The large client is not reduced; the rest of the business grows around it.

    You partner with a nearshore firm for 12 months to test demand, quality and margin. The pilot succeeds, volume grows to 60 engineers, and you then decide to build your own small centre while keeping the partner for overflow. Learning by partnering reduced the risk of the bigger commitment.

    ⚖️ Ethics and responsible growth

    • Honest reporting: numbers to clients, management and boards must be accurate.
    • Fair labour practices in every location: lawful pay, safe conditions, reasonable hours.
    • Respect for candidates: truthful roles, privacy and non-discrimination.
    • Fair competition: never agree prices or territories with competitors.
    • Integrity in offshore growth: do not choose a location only because standards are lower.

    Example: the long-term view
    An offshore partner offers a lower price but pays staff below the legal minimum and requires excessive hours. You decline. A cheaper rate is not worth the legal, reputational and human cost, and clients increasingly audit this part of their supply chain.

    📖 Full case study

    Situation: A $24M technology staffing unit has 15% gross margin, 18% attrition, one client at 35% of revenue, and a request from that client to cut onshore rates by 8% and use more offshore resources.

    1. Diagnose: margin is thin; the largest client has 15% margin; Client C is 9%; bench is 14%.
    2. Respond to the client: propose a hybrid model: 10 onshore leads plus 20 nearshore engineers for a blended rate about 20% lower, instead of an 8% cut on all roles.
    3. Protect margin: margin floor of 12%; nearshore pilot targets at least 22% margin after management costs.
    4. Diversify: grow five mid-size clients and add two new accounts to reduce concentration to under 28%.
    5. Fix people issues: a retention plan with career paths and workload balance to bring attrition below 12%.
    6. Result after 18 months: gross margin 17.5%, concentration 29%, attrition 11%, client satisfaction up.
    ✅
    Why it worked: turning a price demand into a structural solution, a margin floor and pilot, a plan to reduce dependence on one client, and attention to the people who deliver the work.
    Example: what you would say to the CEO
    "The client's request is a chance to restructure, not just to discount. A hybrid team lowers their cost by about 20% and keeps our margin above 20% on the nearshore part. In parallel we reduce concentration below 30% and cut attrition, so the unit is stronger in 18 months."
