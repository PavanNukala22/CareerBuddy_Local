    The Role & Technical Screening Basics

    Before you send a HackerRank link or sit in on a live coding round, understand what a Technical Assessment & Screening Evaluator owns, why structure matters, and the words every technical screening conversation uses.

    🧭 What does this evaluator do?

    A Technical Assessment & Screening Evaluator runs the part of hiring that checks whether a candidate can actually do the technical work. You do not have to be the best programmer in the room. You make sure the right test is given, fairly, and scored consistently, so hiring managers can trust the result.

    💻
    Administer tests
    Set up and run online coding assessments on platforms such as HackerRank and Codility.
    🎥
    Coordinate live reviews
    Organise live coding sessions and make sure the right interviewers and tools are ready.
    🎙️
    Facilitate interviews
    Keep technical interviews on plan, fair and well documented.
    📏
    Score with rubrics
    Turn performance into comparable scores using clear written criteria.
    Example: a day in the role
    At 9 a.m. you send a 75-minute coding test to three candidates. At 11 a.m. you brief two engineers for a live coding round. At 2 p.m. you collect their independent scores, and at 3 p.m. you run a 15-minute debrief that ends with a clear recommendation for the hiring manager.

    ⚖️ Why structured screening

    Unstructured (ad hoc)Structured
    Each interviewer asks different questionsSame core questions for every candidate
    "I liked her" decidesScores against written criteria decide
    Hard to compare candidatesCandidates are directly comparable
    Bias creeps in unnoticedBias is easier to spot and reduce
    Decisions are hard to explainEvery decision has recorded evidence
    Example: two interviews, one candidate
    Interviewer A asks about algorithms and loves her. Interviewer B asks about databases and is unimpressed. With no shared criteria the team argues for an hour. With a structured plan, each interviewer scores the same defined skills, and the disagreement is visible and easy to resolve.

    Take-home assignmentA task completed on the candidate's own timeRealistic work, shorter interviews
    Live codingSolving a problem with an interviewer in real timeSeeing how someone thinks and communicates

    Example: matching type to role
    For 200 graduate applicants you use an online coding test (volume). For a senior platform engineer you use a system design conversation and a code review exercise (depth). One test type rarely fits every level.

    Example: decoding a message
    "She scored 78 on the Codility test, above the 60 cut-off. Live round tomorrow with the rubric, and debrief Friday."
    Decoded: she passed the online test, goes to a live interview scored against written criteria, and the team will share evidence and decide on Friday.

    👥 Who you work with

    🏢 Hiring side

    • Hiring manager: owns the decision and the role needs.
    • Interviewers (engineers): judge technical work.
    • HR: process, fairness, legal compliance.
    • Platform admin: manages assessment tools.

    🧑‍💻 Candidate side

    • Graduates and juniors: often tested at volume.
    • Experienced engineers: expect realistic, respectful tasks.
    • Candidates needing adjustments: require accessible formats.
    Example: agreeing the plan upfront
    Before a new req opens, you ask the hiring manager: "Which three skills must this test prove, and who will interview?" The answers decide the test type, the rubric and the interviewers, and you avoid redesigning the process mid-search.

    Module 02 · Assessment Design Fundamentals

    Assessment Design Fundamentals

    A good assessment measures what the job needs, in a fair amount of time, with questions that candidates see as relevant. Learn how to choose, size and shape assessments before you ever click "send".

    🎯 Start with the job

    The first question is never "which test?" It is "what must this person do in the first 90 days?" Then pick the skills that matter and test only those.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    Example: from job to skills
    Role: backend engineer maintaining payment APIs. Key skills: writing reliable code, debugging production issues, SQL, explaining trade-offs. A puzzle about rotating a matrix measures none of those, so it is dropped. A debugging exercise on a small API measures several.

    🧩 Choosing the type

    RoleStrong choicesWeaker choices
    Graduate or junior developerOnline coding test, short live roundLong system design
    Front-end developerBuild or fix a small UI componentPure algorithm puzzles
    Data engineerSQL tasks, pipeline design discussionUnrelated recursion puzzles
    QA / automation engineerWrite test cases, small automation scriptHeavy algorithm test
    Senior or staff engineerSystem design, code review, work sampleAnother timed puzzle set
    Example: fixing a mismatch
    A client tests senior front-end candidates with graph algorithms. Strong engineers drop out, and those who pass cannot build UI well. You switch to a 60-minute task: "fix three bugs in this component and add a small feature". Pass rate looks lower, but interview results improve.

    ⏱️ Difficulty and time

    FormatReasonable lengthWhy
    Online coding test60 to 90 minutesEnough for 2 to 3 problems without exhausting people
    Take-home assignment2 to 3 hours at mostLonger tasks deter strong, busy candidates
    Live coding45 to 60 minutesTime to think, code and discuss
    System design45 to 60 minutesNeeds discussion, not a full solution

    🟢 Easy

    Basic skills (loops, simple data). Confirms the fundamentals.

    🟡 Medium

    Typical job tasks. Separates solid from weak.

    🔴 Hard

    Edge cases and optimisation. Separates strong from very strong.

    Example: a balanced 75-minute test
    One easy question (10 minutes), one medium (30 minutes), one medium-hard (35 minutes). Almost everyone completes the first, most the second, and the third separates the strongest. A test of three hard problems in 60 minutes would screen out nearly everyone and teach you nothing.

    🗂️ Question types

    TypeTestsExample
    Algorithm / data structureProblem solving and efficiencyFind duplicate orders in a list
    DebuggingReading code and fixing faults"This function returns the wrong total"
    SQL / dataQuerying and reasoning with dataTop five customers by revenue
    Front-end taskBuilding and fixing UIMake a form validate its fields
    Multiple choiceConcept knowledge (quick, shallow)"Which HTTP status means not found?"
    Design / architectureTrade-offs and big-picture thinkingDesign a URL shortener at a high level
    Example: mixing types
    For a mid-level full-stack role you use one debugging question, one SQL question and one small API task. Together they cover reading, data and building in about 75 minutes, a far better picture than one hard algorithm puzzle.

    🔬 Validity in plain English

    An assessment is valid when its scores really predict how well someone does the job. It is reliable when it gives consistent results for the same performance. You do not need statistics to ask the right questions.

    QuestionGood sign
    Does the task look like the real job?Engineers say "yes, we do this"
    Do high scorers do well on the job later?Scores line up with later performance
    Do two interviewers score the same answer the same?Scores are close
    Is it about skill, not trivia?Can be solved by thinking, not memorising
    Example: a validity check
    After a year you compare test scores with 6-month performance reviews of people you hired. People in the top test band mostly became strong performers, and those near the cut-off varied widely. This suggests the test is useful and that the cut-off may be reasonable.

    ♿ Fairness and accessibility

    • Offer reasonable adjustments (extra time, screen-reader support, alternative formats) and apply them consistently.
    • Let candidates use their strongest suitable language where the role allows.
    • Avoid trick questions and cultural references that disadvantage some groups.
    • Give clear instructions and the same preparation information to everyone.
    • Monitor pass rates across groups, with HR, to spot unfair patterns.
    Example: a reasonable adjustment
    A candidate with dyslexia asks for extra time. You grant 25% more time, record it in the ATS, and score the result against the same rubric as everyone. The adjustment does not change the standard; it removes an unrelated barrier.

    😊 Candidate experience

    DoAvoid
    Explain the purpose, length and format upfrontSending a link with no context
    Reply to questions quicklyLeaving candidates guessing
    Tell them when results will arriveSilence after the test
    Give brief feedback where possibleGeneric rejections only
    Example: a better invite
    "Hi Priya, the next step is a 75-minute coding test with 3 problems. You can choose your language, and you will have 5 days to start it. We will send results within 2 business days. Reply here if you need any adjustments." Clear, respectful and complete.

    🪤 Common design mistakes

    ❌ Mistake

    • Using the same puzzle for every role
    • Setting very long take-homes
    • Testing trivia and memorised facts
    • Never reviewing whether tests predict success

    ✅ Better

    • Match the test to the job
    • Keep tasks under about 3 hours
    • Test reasoning on realistic problems
    • Review results against later performance
    Example: a take-home that backfired
    A company sends a 10-hour unpaid take-home. Half of the strong candidates withdraw, and the rest are people with a lot of free time. Cutting it to a 2-hour task doubled the completion rate and improved the quality of finalists.

    Administering HackerRank & Codility Tests

    Online coding platforms do the marking, but you design the experience. Learn how to set up a test, invite candidates, protect integrity, support people during the test and read the report.

    📝
    Features and names differ by platform, version and plan. This module explains the common ideas. Always check your platform's own documentation for exact steps.

    🗺️ The platform landscape

    Kind of toolExamplesUsed for
    Online assessment platformsHackerRank, Codility, CodeSignal, HackerEarthTimed, auto-scored coding tests
    Live collaborative editorsCoderPad and similar toolsLive coding interviews
    Interview servicesKarat and similarOutsourced technical interviews
    Broad skills testsTestGorilla and similarMultiple-choice and mixed skills
    Example: choosing the tool for the job
    A retailer hires 40 junior developers. They use an online assessment platform for the first filter, and a live collaborative editor for the second round. For a one-off senior hire, they skip the online test entirely and do a live round.

    🛠️ Setting up a test

    Example: the preview that saved a test
    You take your own test as a candidate and notice question 2 asks for a function name that question 1 already uses, and the instructions contradict each other. You fix both before sending to 50 candidates, saving dozens of confused emails.

    ⚙️ What to configure

    SettingWhy it mattersExample choice
    Time limitFairness and quality of signal75 minutes for 3 problems
    Languages allowedFair to the role's real stackJava, Python, JavaScript, C#
    Visible vs hidden test casesShows basic checks, hides edge casesSome examples visible, others hidden
    Scoring weightsHarder problems carry more20 / 35 / 45 points

    AccommodationsAccessibilityExtra time per approved request
    Example: why hidden test cases matter
    Candidate A passes the three visible examples but fails four hidden ones about empty input and very large input. Candidate B passes all. Without hidden cases you could not tell them apart, but hidden cases reveal who thought about edge cases.

    ✉️ Inviting candidates

    Example: a clear invite reduces dropout
    After adding length, languages, window, result timing and an adjustments line, a team's completion rate rises from 62% to 81%. The candidates were never unwilling, only uncertain about what they were being asked to do.

    🛡️ Integrity features

    FeatureWhat it showsCaution
    Plagiarism or similarity detectionSolutions that match others or known sourcesShort, simple problems naturally look similar
    Code playback or replayHow the solution was typed over timeSome people type in bursts or paste their own notes
    Tab-switch and paste trackingLeaving the test window, pasting blocksPeople look things up, and may have a second monitor
    Webcam or screen monitoring (if used)Identity and environmentDisclose clearly, get consent, and consider privacy and accessibility
    ⚠️
    Flags are questions, not verdicts. Treat them as a reason to look closer or ask a follow-up, never as automatic proof of cheating.
    Example: a flag handled well
    A report shows a 30-line paste at minute 12. You check the replay: the candidate pasted a helper function they had written earlier and then spent 40 minutes adapting it. You ask about it on the live round, and she explains her approach clearly. No accusation was needed.

    🆘 Supporting candidates

    SituationBest action
    Link does not openCheck expiry, browser and invitation email; resend or extend the link
    Internet drops mid-testCheck if progress is saved; offer a reasonable retake or extra time and document it
    Question looks ambiguousClarify through the official support route, and tell everyone if needed
    Wrong language chosenFollow the policy; if allowed, reset the attempt
    Candidate asks for more timeApply the adjustments policy consistently
    Example: fairness in a glitch
    A candidate loses power at minute 50 of 75 and returns 20 minutes later. You check that the platform saved her work, grant the lost minutes and record it in the ATS. Another candidate with the same issue would receive the same treatment.

    📊 Reading the report

    Report itemWhat it tells you
    Total scoreOverall result against the cut-off
    Score per questionWhere strengths and gaps are
    CorrectnessHow many test cases pass
    Performance or efficiencyWhether the solution works on large inputs in time
    Time takenSpeed and pacing
    Code and playbackReadability, structure and approach
    Example: same total, different people
    Candidate A scores 70 by passing every small test but timing out on large inputs. Candidate B scores 70 with correct, efficient code on two problems and no attempt on the third. The total is identical, but the stories differ, so you read per-question results before deciding.

    🤖 AI assistance policy

    Candidates may use AI tools. Decide your rules before you test, and tell everyone.

    Policy optionWhat it meansDesign implication
    No AI allowedTest of unaided skillNeeds live follow-up to verify understanding
    AI allowed, disclosedMatches how many teams workQuestions test judgement, review and explanation
    MixedNo AI in the first part, AI allowed in the secondClear separation of tasks
    Example: a policy in the invite
    "You may look up documentation. Please do not use AI assistants for this test. In the next round we will ask you to explain and extend your own solution." The rule is explicit, and the follow-up round makes it practical to check.

    🔧 Common problems

    ❌ Problem

    • Candidate has timezone confusion about the window
    • Test uses a language not in the job
    • Questions are too hard for the time
    • Cut-off never reviewed

    ✅ Fix

    • State the window with date, time and time zone
    • Match languages to the stack
    • Pilot and time the test first
    • Review the pass rate every quarter
    Example: a cut-off that quietly failed
    A test is set with a cut-off of 70. After six months only 4% of candidates pass, and interviewers say the passers are excellent but too few. The team reviews the questions, finds one was unclear, rewrites it and moves the pass rate to 18% with no drop in interview quality.

    Module 04 · Live Coding Review & Technical Interview Facilitation

    Live Coding & Interview Facilitation

    Live rounds show how someone thinks and communicates. Learn how to coordinate them, prepare interviewers, run a fair session and capture evidence while it is fresh.

    🎥 Formats of live review

    FormatWhat happensShows
    Live coding (pairing)Solve a problem together in a shared editorThinking, communication, collaboration
    Debugging sessionFind and fix faults in existing codeReading and diagnosing
    Code review exerciseCritique a sample pull requestJudgement and standards
    System designSketch an architecture and discuss trade-offsSeniority and breadth
    Walkthrough of past work or a take-homeCandidate explains their own solutionReal understanding
    Example: choosing the format
    For a mid-level backend role you choose a debugging session on a small API (45 minutes) and a walkthrough of the candidate's online test solution (15 minutes). Together they show real-world reading skills and whether the earlier test was their own thinking.

    👥 Roles in the room

    RoleDoesDoes not
    Lead interviewerRuns the technical conversation and asks the questionsScore before collecting evidence
    Observer or note-takerRecords observations and quotesInterrupt or give hints
    Shadow (in training)Watches to learn the processScore officially, until trained
    Facilitator (you)Coordinates logistics, timing, fairness and documentationHave to solve the problem
    Example: why separate roles help
    The lead interviewer concentrates on the conversation while a note-taker captures phrases like "I would add an index here because the table will grow." After the session, scoring is based on recorded evidence and not on whatever the interviewer happens to remember.

    📋 Before the interview

    • Interview kit: the problem, sample solutions, follow-up questions, red flags and the rubric.
    • Brief interviewers on the plan, time, hint policy and what not to ask.
    • Test the setup: the shared editor, video link, audio and a backup channel.
    • Prepare the candidate: length, format, tools, language choice and whom they will meet.
    • Plan a backup: a second interviewer or a rescheduling option.
    Example: a pre-flight checklist
    The day before, you send the candidate a link, a note that she may choose her language and a reminder to bring questions. You test the shared editor, confirm both interviewers, and share the rubric. On the day, nothing technical interrupts the session.

    ⏱️ Running the session

    MinutesActivity
    0–5Welcome, introductions, explain the format and the "think aloud" request
    5–10Present the problem; candidate asks clarifying questions
    10–40Candidate codes and talks through the solution
    40–50Test, discuss edge cases, optimise
    50–55Candidate's questions
    55–60Close and explain next steps

    🟢 Good habits

    • Encourage thinking aloud
    • Welcome clarifying questions
    • Stay neutral in tone
    • Take notes on behaviour, not opinions

    🔴 Poor habits

    • Silent staring that unsettles people
    • Giving hints unevenly between candidates
    • Interrupting constantly
    • Judging someone as a "culture fit" without evidence
    Example: a good opening script
    "There are no tricks here. I'd like to see how you think, so please talk through your ideas, ask questions whenever you want, and don't worry about perfect syntax. We'll finish with a few minutes for your questions." Candidates relax, and you see their real reasoning.

    💡 The hint policy

    Agree in advance when and how interviewers help, so every candidate gets the same support, and record how many hints were needed.

    LevelHintExample
    0Clarify the question"The input is a list of integers. Does that help?"
    1Gentle nudge"What happens with a very large list?"
    2Point to an approach"Would a lookup table help here?"
    3Work through it together"Let's write the first step together."
    Example: hints in scoring
    Candidate A needed one level-1 nudge and then finished cleanly. Candidate B needed three level-2 hints. Both reached a working solution, but the scores should differ, and because hints were recorded the difference is fair and visible.

    🆘 When things go wrong

    ProblemWhat to do
    Video or editor failsSwitch to the backup channel, add the lost minutes, note the issue
    Candidate freezesPause kindly, offer a level-0 hint or a short break
    Interviewer runs late or cancelsUse the backup interviewer, or reschedule and apologise promptly
    Session overrunsRespect the time; tell the candidate how you will handle the remaining topics
    Candidate seems to read from another screenAsk a follow-up in a new direction; do not accuse
    Example: a recovered session
    The editor crashes at minute 20. You message the candidate on the backup chat, share a new link and add 15 minutes. You note the issue in the file so that the interviewer does not penalise the lost time. She finishes strongly and later praises the professionalism.

    ⚖️ Interviewer dos and don'ts

    ✅ Do

    • Ask the same core questions of everyone
    • Stick to job-related topics
    • Record evidence of what the candidate said and did
    • Score independently before any discussion

    🚫 Don't

    • Ask about age, family plans, religion, health or similar topics
    • Compare a candidate to yourself
    • Decide in the first five minutes
    • Share opinions before everyone has scored
    Example: stopping a bad question
    An interviewer starts chatting: "Are you planning to have children soon?" You step in gently and redirect: "Let's keep to the role. Do you have any questions about the team?" You also note it and speak to the interviewer afterwards, because a question like that can create legal risk and is unrelated to the job.

    📝 After the interview

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    • Score first, talk later. Independent scores prevent the first speaker from steering the group.
    • Notes should record behaviour and quotes, not vague opinions.
    • Send the candidate a timely answer, with feedback where your policy allows.
    Example: independent scoring in action
    Three interviewers submit scores of 3, 3 and 4 before anyone speaks. In the debrief, nobody has been swayed by a senior voice, and the conversation focuses on why one person gave a 4. That evidence turns out to be a strong example of testing, which the others had missed.

    📅 Coordination logistics

    TaskGood practice
    SchedulingOffer time options, confirm time zones, avoid back-to-back overload
    Interviewer loadShare the work so the same few people are not exhausted
    Panel compositionInclude varied perspectives where possible
    RemindersSend candidate and interviewers a reminder with links
    RecordsStore scores and notes in the ATS
    Example: sharing the load
    One senior engineer ends up doing 14 interviews in a month and starts rushing. You build a rota where each trained interviewer does at most 4 a month, train two more people, and the quality of notes goes up while the scheduling delays fall.

    Module 05 · Rubric Scoring

    Rubric Scoring

    A rubric turns "I liked them" into evidence you can compare and defend. Learn to build one, score with it, avoid common biases and run a debrief that reaches a fair decision.

    📏 What a rubric is

    A rubric lists the skills you are judging and describes what each score looks like in behaviour. Think of a diving competition: judges score the same dive against defined criteria instead of saying "that felt good".

    🔴 Without a rubric

    "Strong candidate, good vibes. I'd say a 4."

    🟢 With a rubric

    "Problem solving: 4. She chose a clear approach, covered edge cases without prompting and compared two options."

    Example: same performance, two readings
    Two interviewers watch the same candidate. One scores 2 ("too slow"). The other scores 4 ("great communicator"). With a rubric, both must point to evidence for the same criteria, so the gap is explained and resolved instead of becoming an argument.

    🧱 Building a rubric

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    RuleWhy
    Keep to 3 to 5 competenciesMore is hard to score fairly in one session
    Use a 4-point scaleRemoves the safe "middle" answer and forces a decision
    Describe behaviour, not personality"Explains trade-offs" is observable; "smart" is not
    Include negative signs tooHelps interviewers spot weak performance
    Example: simplifying a rubric
    A team has 11 criteria, and interviewers fill them in randomly. You reduce to four: problem solving, code quality, correctness and communication. Scores become consistent, notes get longer and more specific, and debriefs shrink from an hour to 20 minutes.

    🧪 A worked rubric

    ScoreMeaningProblem solving: what you observe
    1Strong noCannot structure the problem, even with several hints
    2NoNeeds heavy hints; partial approach with gaps
    3YesReasonable approach, minimal hints, handles main cases
    4Strong yesClear approach independently, covers edge cases and compares options
    CompetencyWeightA 3 looks like
    Problem solving30%Sound approach with small nudges
    Code quality25%Readable, named clearly, reasonable structure
    Correctness25%Works on main cases; may miss a rare edge case
    Communication20%Explains thinking and responds well to questions
    Example: scoring one answer
    Candidate asks clarifying questions, picks a lookup-table approach, handles empty input unprompted, but names variables poorly. Problem solving 4, code quality 2, correctness 3, communication 3. Each score is tied to something observed.

    🧮 Weighted scoring

    Multiply each score by its weight, then add the results.

    CompetencyScoreWeightPoints
    Problem solving40.301.20
    Code quality30.250.75
    Correctness30.250.75
    Communication20.200.40
    Weighted total1.003.10
    💡
    The simple average of 4, 3, 3 and 2 is 3.0, but the weighted total is 3.10, because the strongest score sits in the most important competency. Weights should reflect the role.
    Example: weights that fit the job
    For a client-facing support engineer, communication might weigh 30% and correctness 20%. The same candidate (4, 3, 3, 2 in the original order) would then score differently, and that is intended: the rubric reflects what the role needs.

    🗒️ Evidence-based notes

    🔴 Weak notes

    • "Seemed smart."
    • "Not a culture fit."
    • "I had a good feeling."

    🟢 Strong notes

    • "Asked about input size before coding."
    • "Missed the empty-list case; found it after I asked for tests."
    • "Explained two options and chose one with a reason."
    Example: rewriting a note
    "Not a culture fit" becomes: "Disagreed with the team's code review approach and said so politely; gave a reasoned alternative." The second version is real evidence, and it shows a strength rather than a weakness.

    🪤 Bias traps

    TrapWhat happensFix
    Halo effectOne strong trait colours everythingScore each competency separately
    Horn effectOne weakness colours everythingScore each competency separately
    Similarity biasFavouring people like usUse diverse panels and evidence-based notes
    Recency biasThe last few minutes dominateTake notes throughout
    AnchoringFirst opinion steers the groupIndependent scoring first
    Leniency or severityAlways too kind or too harshCalibrate with examples
    Example: the halo effect
    A candidate is charming and explains well in the first 10 minutes. The interviewer then scores her 4 on everything, including code quality, though her code was messy. A rule to score each competency from separate notes catches this.

    🎯 Calibration

    Calibration means interviewers score the same sample answer and compare results until they agree on what each score means. Agreement between interviewers is called inter-rater reliability.

    StepWhat you do
    1Pick a recorded or written sample answer
    2Each interviewer scores it alone
    3Compare scores, discuss differences against the rubric
    4Adjust the wording of the rubric if it was unclear
    5Repeat every few months, and when new interviewers join
    Example: a calibration session
    Four interviewers score the same sample: 4, 3, 2 and 4. The two who gave 4 cite the clear explanation; the one who gave 2 points to a missed edge case. The group agrees the rubric did not say how to treat edge cases, adds a line, and re-scores: 3, 3, 3, 3.

    🚦 Cut-offs and decisions

    RecommendationTypical meaning
    Strong yesClear evidence of excellence on key skills
    YesMeets the bar
    NoBelow the bar on important skills
    Strong noClear evidence of a major gap

    A common rule combines a minimum total and a floor on critical competencies, for example: weighted total of 3.0 or more and no competency below 2.

    Example: a two-part rule
    Candidate X scores a weighted 3.3 but gets 1 on correctness, a critical skill. The rule says no competency below 2, so X does not pass, even with a high total. The team writes the reason down, which keeps the decision consistent for every candidate.

    🗣️ Running a debrief

    1. Interviewers submit scores and notes before the meeting.
    2. You show the scores side by side.
    3. Each interviewer shares their evidence, junior voices first.
    4. Discuss disagreements by checking evidence against the rubric.
    5. Reach a recommendation and note the reasons.
    6. Record the outcome in the ATS and agree on feedback for the candidate.
    Example: a disagreement resolved
    Scores are 4 and 2 for the same candidate. The interviewer who scored 2 cites a missed edge case. The 4 interviewer notes the candidate later spotted and fixed it unprompted. The group agrees on 3, records both observations, and moves to a clear recommendation in ten minutes.

    Module 06 · Reviewing Results

    Reviewing Results & Interpreting Scores

    A score is a starting point, not a verdict. Learn what automated results can and cannot tell you, how to read a solution without being an engineer, and how to handle borderline and suspicious cases fairly.

    📊 What a score can and cannot say

    ✅ A score can show

    • Whether code passes defined tests
    • Whether it runs fast enough on large inputs
    • Pace and pattern of work

    ❌ A score cannot show

    • How readable and maintainable the code is
    • Whether the person works well in a team
    • Judgement, communication and learning speed
    Example: a high score with a gap
    A candidate scores 92 with correct code that is one 80-line function with unclear names. The score says "it works". An engineer reading it says "I'd struggle to maintain this". Both are true, so the next step is a live round that checks design and communication.

    🔍 Reading a coding solution

    You do not need to understand every line. Look for these surface signals, and ask an engineer for a second opinion on borderline cases.

    SignalGood signWarning sign
    NamingMeaningful names like orderTotalSingle letters everywhere, unclear names
    StructureSmall functions, clear stepsOne huge block
    Edge casesHandles empty or unusual inputOnly the example case works
    Comments and testsBrief, helpful, some testsNone, or comments that repeat the code
    ApproachReasonable method, explainedHard-coded answers for the visible tests
    Example: a hard-coded solution
    A solution passes all visible test cases and fails every hidden one. A quick look shows that it checks for the exact example inputs and returns the stored answers. That is a strong warning sign, even though the visible score looked perfect.

    🌗 Partial credit and borderline cases

    Result patternWhat it may meanNext step
    Passes basic tests, fails large-input testsCorrect idea, not efficientAsk how they would optimise it
    Passes large tests, fails edge casesStrong core, careless about edge casesAsk which edge cases they considered
    Half the score, very fastRushed, or only attempted easier problemsReview time per question
    Just below cut-offBorderlineEngineer review of code before deciding
    Example: a borderline review
    A candidate scores 58 against a cut-off of 60. An engineer reviews the code and finds clean structure and a correct approach on all three problems, with one missed edge case. With the engineer's note, the team gives her a live round, and she excels. A pure cut-off would have lost her.

    🚨 Integrity signals

    SignalPossible innocent reasonBest response
    High similarity to another solutionA common standard approach to a simple problemCompare approach and style; ask a follow-up
    Large pasteOwn helper code or notesCheck replay; ask them to explain it
    Many tab switchesLooking up documentationCompare with your stated rules
    Very fast, perfect solutionCandidate has seen a similar problem beforeFollow-up: change a requirement and ask them to adapt it
    💡
    The most reliable check is a live follow-up: ask the candidate to explain, change or extend their own solution. Someone who wrote it can do this easily; someone who did not will struggle.
    Example: the follow-up test
    A solution looks identical to a published online answer. In the live round you say, "Now suppose the list can contain duplicates. How would you change your code?" The candidate adapts it in two minutes and explains why. The similarity was a common approach, and no accusation was needed.

    ⚖️ False positives and negatives

    ErrorWhat it isHow to reduce it
    False positivePasses screening, cannot do the jobAdd a live explanation round and a realistic task
    False negativeCapable person filtered outAllow borderline reviews, accommodate anxiety and glitches, offer alternatives
    Example: a false negative rescued
    A strong engineer fails an online test after her laptop restarts at minute 40. Her track record is excellent. You offer a re-take under the retake policy, she passes comfortably and later becomes a top performer. A rigid cut-off would have lost a great hire.

    🧩 Combining signals

    SignalStrengthWeakness
    Online testScalable and consistentNarrow, can be gamed
    Live codingShows thinking and communicationStressful, interviewer-dependent
    Past work and referencesShows real resultsHard to compare
    Work sampleMost job-relevantTakes time to build and score
    Example: three signals, one decision
    Test: 65 (just above the cut-off). Live round: strong explanations, rubric 3.4. Work history: led a similar migration. All three together support a "yes", even though no single one was outstanding. Using several signals gives a more reliable decision than any one alone.

    🤝 Working with engineers

    • Ask engineers for short, structured reviews: "Rate readability 1 to 4 and give one sentence of evidence."
    • Give them the problem and the rubric so they judge the same way.
    • Keep reviews blind where possible (hide names and schools) to reduce bias.
    • Respect their time: 10 to 15 minutes per solution, not an hour.
    Example: a 10-minute review request
    "Please review this solution against the rubric. Give a 1 to 4 for readability and efficiency, and one sentence of evidence for each. The candidate's name is hidden. Thanks, about 10 minutes." The engineer replies quickly with consistent notes you can use directly.

    📝 Summarising for the manager

    results summary templateCandidate: A. Rao   |   Role: Backend Engineer   |   Stage: after online test

    Example: why this format works
    The hiring manager sees the score, the human review, the integrity result and the next step in six lines. There is no need to open the platform, and the live-round interviewers already know what to probe.

    🔬 Worked example

    three reports, three next stepsCandidate 1: 88 total, clean code, no flags
Candidate 2: 61 total (cut-off 60), correct but inefficient, one 40-line paste
Candidate 3: 95 total, identical to a known online answer, finished in 12 minutes
    ✅
    Reading: Candidate 1 proceeds. Candidate 2 gets an engineer review and an explanation of the paste before deciding. Candidate 3 proceeds only with a live follow-up that changes the problem and asks them to adapt their solution. No one is rejected on a flag alone.
    Example: what you would write about Candidate 3
    "Strong score, but the solution matches a published answer and was completed in 12 minutes. Recommend a live round with a changed requirement, so we can confirm understanding. No concern recorded against the candidate at this point."

    Workflow, Experience & Compliance

    The best assessment still fails if the process is slow, unclear or careless with data. Learn the end-to-end workflow, service levels, communication templates and the compliance habits that protect candidates and your company.

    📝
    Privacy and employment-law rules vary by country and change over time. This module teaches the habits and questions. For specific legal requirements, ask your HR, legal or data-protection team.

    🛤️ The end-to-end workflow

    StageOwner
    Assessment plan and rubricYou with the hiring manager
    Invitations and supportYou
    Code reviewEngineers, coordinated by you
    Live roundsTrained interviewers, coordinated by you

    Example: a plan on one page
    Role: backend engineer. Plan: 75-minute online test (3 problems), 10-minute engineer review, 60-minute live debugging round with two trained interviewers, debrief within 24 hours of the last interview. The manager signs off before the first invite goes out.

    A service level is a promise about speed. Candidates and hiring managers both notice when you keep it. These numbers are examples to adapt, not rules.

    Example: speed wins offers
    A candidate has two processes running. Company A takes 3 weeks between steps. Yours moves in 8 days from test to decision. She accepts your offer because your process told her you were organised and respectful of her time.

    Example: feedback that builds goodwill
    A rejected candidate receives two specific, kind observations. She later refers a friend who becomes a successful hire. Specific feedback turns rejections into relationships, and costs only a few minutes.

    🎁 Take-home fairness

    RuleWhy
    Keep tasks to about 2 to 3 hoursRespects people with jobs and caregiving duties
    Use fictional data, not real company workAvoids free labour and confidentiality problems
    Share the rubric with candidatesEveryone knows what good looks like
    Pay candidates for longer tasksShows respect and widens the pool
    Offer a walkthrough instead, when suitableSome candidates cannot spare personal time
    Example: a fair alternative
    A senior candidate explains she has no spare evenings for a take-home. You offer a 45-minute live walkthrough of a project from her own work (without confidential details) and score it with the same rubric as the take-home. She stays in the process.

    🔒 Data privacy and retention

    • Tell candidates what data is collected (code, scores, recordings, webcam images) and why.
    • Collect only what you need, and get consent for monitoring or recording.
    • Store results securely with access limited to those who need them.
    • Follow a retention period: delete data when it is no longer needed.
    • Handle access or deletion requests promptly, with your data-protection team.
    Example: a deletion request
    Six months after a rejection, a candidate asks for his data to be deleted. You confirm his identity, pass the request to the data-protection team, delete his assessment results and recordings according to policy, and confirm to him in writing. The request is documented for audit.

    ⚖️ Compliance and records

    HabitWhy
    Use the same process and rubric for every candidate in a roleFairness and defensibility
    Record scores and reasons in the ATSAn audit trail if a decision is questioned
    Track pass rates by group, with HRSpot possible adverse impact early
    Document accommodationsShow equal treatment
    Keep interview questions job-relatedAvoid illegal or unfair questions
    Example: spotting a pattern
    HR notices that candidates who select one programming language pass at much lower rates than others, though the role allows both. You review the questions with engineers and find the sample tests were written for the other language. Fixing it makes the test fairer, and pass rates even out.

    💼 Vendor and cost management

    ItemWhat to watch
    Licence costNumber of tests or seats you actually use
    Question libraryKeep it fresh; retire leaked or overused questions
    IntegrationsDoes it connect to your ATS so results flow automatically?
    Support and uptimeWhat happens during an outage?
    Data location and securityWhere is candidate data stored, and for how long?
    Example: a retired question
    You notice that the same problem has been used for 18 months and solutions appear on public forums. Pass rates have crept up from 35% to 70% without any rise in interview quality. You replace the question, and pass rates fall back while interview results improve.

    📊 Funnel metrics

    MetricFormulaExample

    Of 50 invited, 40 completed (80%), 20 passed (50%), and 16 were interviewed with 8 recommended (50%). If completion had been only 50%, you would look at the invitation clarity and test length first. If the live pass rate was only 10%, the online test might be too lenient.

    🗃️ ATS hygiene

    • Record every stage, score and date, as it happens.
    • Use standard fields so reports are comparable.
    • Attach the rubric and interviewer notes; do not keep them in private files.
    • Mark accommodations and any process deviations.
    • Close the loop: record feedback sent to the candidate.
    Example: finding an answer in 30 seconds
    Months later a manager asks, "Why did we reject candidate X?" You open the ATS and see: online test 58, engineer review, rubric scores 2, 2, 3, 2 with notes, and the feedback sent. The decision is easy to explain and fair on its face.

    Module 08 · Advanced Practice

    Advanced: Validity, Integrity & Judgement

    What separates a good evaluator from a trusted one: checking that your tests really predict success, handling cheating and AI calmly, improving interviewers, and staying fair under pressure.

    🔬 Validating your assessments

    The real test of an assessment is whether people who score well do well on the job. This is called predictive validity.

    Read left to right, then continue on the next row.
    FindingWhat it suggestsAction
    Higher scores, better performanceThe test is usefulKeep and monitor
    No link between scores and performanceThe test measures the wrong thingsRedesign around real job tasks
    Strong performers scored lowThe cut-off or format may be filtering good people outReview the cut-off and borderline process
    Example: a validity check
    Over 18 months a team hired 40 engineers. Those who scored in the top third of the coding test had the best six-month reviews on average, but the test's last question had no link to performance. The team keeps the first two questions and replaces the third with a debugging task.

    📈 Item analysis

    Item analysis looks at each question's pass rate to see whether it separates candidates.

    Question pass rateMeaningAction
    Above about 90%Too easy; almost nobody is separatedMake it harder or use it only as a warm-up
    Between about 30% and 70%Healthy: separates stronger from weakerKeep
    Below about 10%Too hard or unclearCheck wording; replace or simplify
    Strong candidates fail it, weak ones passProbably flawed or leakedInvestigate and retire
    Example: a broken question
    Question 3 has a pass rate of 98% and strong and weak candidates score the same. Question 2 is passed by only 6%, including by engineers who later do well in interviews. You make Q3 harder, rewrite the unclear wording in Q2, and the test now separates people sensibly.

    🤖 Cheating and AI in practice

    ApproachHow it works
    Clear policyState what is allowed, in the invitation
    Fresh, varied questionsRotate and avoid well-known puzzles
    Live follow-upAsk the candidate to explain, change and extend their solution
    Realistic tasksDebugging and code review tasks that need judgement
    Proportionate monitoringUse only what is needed, and disclose it
    💡
    Shift the question from "Did they write every line themselves?" to "Can they understand, judge, adapt and explain this code?" That is closer to modern engineering work, and it is harder to fake.
    Example: an AI-era round
    The live round hands the candidate their own submitted solution with a changed requirement: "Now orders can be cancelled. How does your design handle that?" A real author adapts quickly and discusses trade-offs. Someone who pasted an answer they do not understand struggles within minutes.

    🎓 Raising interviewer quality

    StepWhat it involves
    TrainingRubric, hint policy, bias traps, legal do's and don'ts
    ShadowingNew interviewers observe two or three sessions
    Reverse shadowingA trained interviewer observes and gives feedback
    CalibrationRegular scoring of sample answers
    Feedback on notesReview the quality of evidence, not just the scores
    Panel diversityMix backgrounds to reduce similarity bias
    Example: improving note quality
    You review ten interview notes and find that six contain only opinions such as "strong, good vibes". After a short workshop and a template that asks for "what you saw and heard", evidence-based notes rise from 40% to 90%, and debriefs get shorter and fairer.

    🎭 Hard scenarios

    ScenarioBest response
    Candidate disputes a scoreReview against the rubric with a second reviewer, answer respectfully, and record the result
    Interviewer gives a high score based on "culture fit"Ask for behavioural evidence tied to a rubric competency; set aside vague reasons
    Two interviewers score 4 and 1 for the same candidateCompare evidence against the rubric, then calibrate; do not simply average
    Platform outage affects many candidates mid-testContact everyone promptly, reschedule or add time, document and treat all alike
    Interviewer cancels shortly before a live roundUse a trained backup or reschedule quickly, with a clear apology
    Hiring manager wants to skip the process for a friendExplain the risk to fairness and consistency, and offer the quickest compliant route
    Example: a score dispute
    A rejected candidate emails: "I think my correctness score was unfair." You ask a second engineer to review against the rubric. The reviewer finds one hidden test failed because of an unclear problem statement. You tell the candidate, offer a retake, and fix the question for everyone else.

    MetricFormulaExample

    Drop-off after invite1 − completion rate1 − 0.80 = 20%

    Example: diagnosing a high drop-off
    Only 40% of invited candidates complete a 3-hour test (60% drop-off). You shorten it to 75 minutes, add a clear invitation and a reminder. Completion rises to 80%, and the strongest candidates, who had been dropping out most often, start finishing.

    ⚖️ Ethical practice

    • Treat every candidate the same way, and apply adjustments transparently.
    • Never use a candidate's work for your own gain or real company projects.
    • Be honest about the process, timeline and what is measured.
    • Treat integrity flags as questions, never as guilt.
    • Protect personal data and delete it when it is no longer needed.
    Example: the fairness test
    Ask yourself: "If this candidate saw every note and score about them, would the process feel fair?" If yes, you are likely doing it right. If no, fix the process before the next candidate.

    📖 Full case study

    Req: Backend engineer, payments team. 60 applicants. Decision needed in 3 weeks.

    1. Plan: you agree with the manager on four competencies (problem solving 30%, code quality 25%, correctness 25%, communication 20%) and a rule: weighted 3.0 or more, no competency below 2.
    2. Online test: 75 minutes, 3 problems. 60 invited, 48 completed (80%), 24 passed.
    3. Review: engineers review the top 24 in 10 minutes each; 2 integrity flags are cleared by live follow-up.
    4. Live round: 14 candidates interviewed by trained pairs, with a hint policy and independent scoring.
    5. Debrief: 5 recommended, with evidence recorded. Two disagreements resolved through the rubric.
    6. Decision: 2 offers, 2 accepted. Feedback sent to every candidate within 2 business days.
    ✅
    Why it worked: a plan agreed upfront, a realistic test, human review of results, independent scoring, evidence-based debriefs and timely, respectful communication.
    Example: what you would tell the hiring manager
    "Five candidates meet the bar, with evidence recorded for each. Two stand out: one scored a weighted 3.6 with strong explanations of trade-offs, and one scored 3.4 with excellent debugging. I recommend offers to both, and I have the notes ready for your review."
