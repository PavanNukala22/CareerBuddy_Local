    Course Overview

    Contingent Workforce Specialist: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches the daily craft of a Contingent Workforce Specialist in India. You learn to hire temporary workers in volume, keep their employment within the law and fill rosters fast when clients are under pressure.

    The role matters because clients buy flexibility and expect it to be safe. A specialist who hires well, pays correctly and delivers on time protects both the staffing firm and the client from cost and risk.

    The course is organised as three skills. Each skill has foundations to expert levels, then tools, quality testing, best practices, a project, troubleshooting and interview practice. A summary and assessment close the course.

    Who this course is for

    • Staffing and recruitment executives moving into contingent work
    • Operations and site coordinators handling temporary labor
    • HR and payroll staff who support contract workers
    • Freshers who want a career in workforce solutions

    How the course is organised

    • Skill 1 covers volume hiring: pipeline, funnel, channels and early attrition.
    • Skill 2 covers compliance: worker types, onboarding, pay and overtime, and audit readiness.
    • Skill 3 covers fast fulfillment: demand types, the plan, run rate and the cost of speed.
    • Each skill adds tools and workflow, testing and quality, do and avoid lists, a project and troubleshooting.
    • Interview practice gives five questions per skill with model answers.
    • The summary recaps formulas and gives a self-assessment checklist.
    ⏱️
    Time needed: About 10 to 12 hours

    Filling many temporary roles quickly, for factory and office work alike, with a repeatable pipeline instead of one-off effort.

    Level 1 · Foundations

    🏭 What high-volume temp hiring is and why it matters

    High-volume temporary hiring means filling dozens or hundreds of short-term roles for a client in a short time. Blue-collar roles are hands-on and site-based, such as factory operators, warehouse associates, packers, drivers and security staff. White-collar roles are desk-based, such as data entry, back-office processing, customer support and clerical work.

    Why it matters: clients buy temporary labor to handle peaks, projects and absences. If hiring is slow or chaotic, the client's output suffers, and the staffing firm loses the contract. Volume hiring is won by process: a pipeline that runs the same way every time, with numbers at each stage.

    The skill is to keep three things in balance: speed (how fast roles are filled), quality (whether workers are suitable and stay) and cost (what each hire costs).

    TermMeaning
    RequisitionA client request to fill roles
    SourcingFinding candidates
    ScreeningQuick check of suitability
    OfferA confirmed job offer to a candidate
    JoiningThe candidate actually starts work
    Drop-offCandidates lost between stages
    TATTurnaround time from request to joining
    Offer-to-join ratio = Candidates who joined ÷ Offers made
    🧪
    Worked example: A hiring drive makes 300 offers and 240 candidates join. Offer-to-join ratio = 240 ÷ 300 = 80%. The 60 who did not join must be replaced, which is why offers always exceed the target.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating every hire as a separate project
    • Not tracking numbers at each stage of the pipeline
    • Chasing speed and ignoring whether workers stay

    ✍️ Practice

    A drive makes 200 offers and 150 candidates join. What is the offer-to-join ratio?

    Show answer
    150 ÷ 200 = 75%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Blue-collar and white-collar hiring compared

    The two worker groups need different handling. Blue-collar candidates are usually found through walk-in drives, local referrals, village or community contacts and labor contractors. They are screened on availability, physical fitness for the task, a quick skill or trade check and documents. Pay is often daily or weekly, and early drop-off is common if travel or pay is a problem.

    White-collar candidates come mostly from job portals, social media and referrals. They are screened on typing speed or other basic tests, communication and a short interview. Joining delays are often due to notice periods, so the offer-to-join gap is longer.

    Cost differs too. Cost per hire is total hiring spend divided by the number of hires, and it is tracked separately for each group.

    AspectBlue-collarWhite-collar
    Main sourcesWalk-in drives, referrals, local contactsJob portals, social media, referrals
    ScreeningAvailability, fitness, trade check, documentsSkill test, interview, communication
    Common riskEarly drop-off, transportLate joining, notice period
    Pay patternOften daily or weeklyMonthly
    Cost per hire = Total hiring cost ÷ Number of hires
    🧪
    Worked example: A blue-collar drive spent ₹3,60,000 on advertising, venue, travel and staff time, and made 240 hires. Cost per hire = 3,60,000 ÷ 240 = ₹1,500.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using one screening method for both groups
    • Ignoring transport when hiring for a remote site
    • Counting only advertising spend in cost per hire

    ✍️ Practice

    A drive spent ₹2,52,000 and made 180 hires. What is the cost per hire?

    Show answer
    2,52,000 ÷ 180 = ₹1,400.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🔧 The hiring pipeline, step by step

    A pipeline is the fixed path every candidate follows. Steps: (1) intake: confirm numbers, skills, shift, location, pay and start date with the client; (2) sourcing: open the channels; (3) screening: a short call or desk check; (4) interview or trade test; (5) document verification: identity, address, age proof and bank details; (6) offer with clear pay and terms; (7) onboarding and induction; (8) joining day.

    The key idea is the funnel. Each stage loses some candidates, so you must start with far more leads than the number of joins you need. To find the number, multiply the conversion rate of every stage, then divide the target by that product.

    Tools and documents: a tracker with a row per candidate and a column per stage, a screening checklist, a document checklist and an induction attendance sheet.

    StageExample conversion
    Lead to screened50%
    Screened to interviewed60%
    Interviewed to offered50%
    Offered to joined80%
    Overall0.5 × 0.6 × 0.5 × 0.8 = 12%
    Leads needed = Target joins ÷ (Conversion 1 × Conversion 2 × … × Conversion n)
    🧪
    Worked example: The client needs 100 joined workers. The overall conversion is 12%, so leads needed = 100 ÷ 0.12 = 833.3, so about 834 leads must enter the funnel.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Starting with the exact number of candidates needed
    • Not measuring conversion at each stage
    • Skipping document checks to save time

    ✍️ Practice

    The conversions are 40%, 50%, 60% and 75%. How many leads are needed for 90 joins?

    Show answer
    Overall = 0.4 × 0.5 × 0.6 × 0.75 = 0.09. Leads = 90 ÷ 0.09 = 1,000.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    📊 Scenarios, channel cost and KPIs

    Different channels cost different amounts and bring different quality. A walk-in drive may cost less per hire but needs staff on the ground. Referrals can be cheap and bring stable workers, though the bonus is a cost. Job boards cost more but reach white-collar candidates quickly.

    A manager compares channels on cost per hire, offer-to-join ratio and 30-day retention, not on cost alone. The blended cost per hire shows the overall cost of a mix.

    Core KPIs: time to fill, cost per hire, offer-to-join ratio, 30-day retention and the funnel conversion rates.

    ChannelHiresCost per hireTotal
    Walk-in50₹800₹40,000
    Referral30₹600₹18,000
    Job board20₹1,500₹30,000
    Total100₹88,000
    Blended cost per hire = Σ (Hires × Cost per hire) ÷ Total hires
    🧪
    Worked example: For 100 hires: 50 from walk-in at ₹800 (₹40,000), 30 from referral at ₹600 (₹18,000) and 20 from job boards at ₹1,500 (₹30,000). Total = ₹88,000, so the blended cost = 88,000 ÷ 100 = ₹880.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Choosing the cheapest channel without checking retention
    • Not paying referral bonuses on time
    • Ignoring channels that bring fewer but better candidates

    ✍️ Practice

    60 hires come from walk-in at ₹700, 20 from referral at ₹500 and 20 from job boards at ₹1,400. What is the blended cost per hire?

    Show answer
    60 × 700 = 42,000; 20 × 500 = 10,000; 20 × 1,400 = 28,000. Total ₹80,000 ÷ 100 = ₹800.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: surge hiring, early attrition and fraud control

    At expert level you manage surge demand without letting quality collapse. Early attrition is the main danger: workers who leave in the first week or month waste the whole hiring cost. The 30-day attrition rate is the left-within-30-days count divided by those who joined.

    Causes of early exit are usually fixable: unclear pay, a long journey, work different from what was described or a poor first-day experience. A realistic job preview, a clear offer letter and a welcoming induction help.

    Control risk and fraud: verify identity documents, check for duplicate candidates across drives and watch for agents who submit people who never intended to stay. Build a talent pool of workers who performed well, so the next surge starts with a ready list.

    RiskControl
    Early attritionRealistic job preview, clear offer, good induction
    Fake or duplicate documentsVerification and cross-checks
    Quality drop in surgeKeep screening standards, add screeners
    Dependence on one channelUse two or three channels
    30-day attrition = Left within 30 days ÷ Joined | Replacement cost = Leavers × Cost per hire
    🧪
    Worked example: 240 workers joined and 36 left within 30 days: 36 ÷ 240 = 15%. The target is 10% or less. The replacement cost is 36 × ₹1,500 = ₹54,000, so fixing the cause pays back quickly.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Hiring faster by relaxing screening
    • Not asking leavers why they left
    • Never building a talent pool

    ✍️ Practice

    180 workers joined and 27 left within 30 days. What is the attrition rate, and what is the replacement cost at ₹1,400 per hire?

    Show answer
    27 ÷ 180 = 15%. Replacement cost = 27 × 1,400 = ₹37,800.

    Contingent labor law compliance

    Making sure temporary workers get what the law requires on wages, hours, contributions and records, for both the staffing firm and the client.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    ⚖️ What contingent labor compliance is and why it matters

    Temporary workers are still workers in the eyes of the law. In a contingent arrangement the staffing firm (the contractor) employs the worker and the client (the principal employer) uses the labor. Both carry duties. The contractor must pay wages, make statutory contributions and keep records. The principal employer must check that the contractor is licensed and, in many cases, can be held responsible if wages or dues are not paid.

    Why it matters: non-compliance leads to back pay, penalties, inspections, loss of licence and damage to the client relationship. Compliance is also a selling point, because clients prefer a partner who protects them from legal risk.

    Important: rates, thresholds and rules change and differ by state. The figures in this module are illustrative. Always confirm the current rules with a labour-law advisor or the relevant authority before applying them.

    TermMeaning
    Principal employerThe client who uses the contract labor
    ContractorThe staffing firm that employs the workers
    Statutory duesPayments required by law, such as PF and ESI
    RegisterA legally required record, such as a wage or attendance register
    LicencePermission for a contractor to supply labor
    Minimum wageThe lowest wage that may be paid, notified by the government
    Statutory cost % = (Employer PF + Employer ESI) ÷ Gross wage
    🧪
    Worked example: A worker's gross wage is ₹18,000. Illustrative employer PF is 12% of the ₹15,000 wage ceiling = ₹1,800, and employer ESI is 3.25% of ₹18,000 = ₹585. Statutory cost = 2,385 ÷ 18,000 = 13.25%. The bill rate must cover this before margin.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Believing a temporary worker has no legal rights
    • Leaving all compliance to the contractor without checking
    • Using old rates and thresholds

    ✍️ Practice

    Gross wage is ₹20,000. Employer PF is 12% of ₹15,000 and employer ESI is 3.25% of ₹20,000 (illustrative). What is the statutory cost percentage?

    Show answer
    PF = ₹1,800. ESI = ₹650. Total = ₹2,450. 2,450 ÷ 20,000 = 12.25%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of workers and areas of law

    Different worker types are covered by different rules. Contract labor supplied by a contractor, fixed-term employees, directly engaged casual or daily workers, apprentices or trainees, and independent contractors are all treated differently. Calling a person by the wrong category is one of the biggest risks.

    The law also covers several areas: wages (minimum wage, timely payment), social security (provident fund, state insurance), working hours and overtime, safety and welfare, regulation of contract labor (licensing, registers) and industrial relations. A compliance specialist works through each area for every site.

    AreaWhat it coversExample duty
    WagesMinimum wage, equal pay, payment on timePay at or above the notified rate
    Social securityPF, ESI and similar schemesEnrol and contribute
    HoursDaily and weekly limits, overtimePay overtime at the correct rate
    Safety and welfareSafe workplace, facilitiesInduction, PPE, first aid
    Contract labor rulesLicence, registers, principal employer dutiesDisplay licence, keep registers
    Minimum wage shortfall = (Notified minimum − Wage paid) × Days × Workers
    🧪
    Worked example: The notified minimum wage is ₹520 a day (illustrative) and workers are paid ₹500. Shortfall = 20 × 26 days × 200 workers = ₹1,04,000 a month of underpayment.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating every temporary worker as an independent contractor
    • Not knowing which state's minimum wage applies
    • Ignoring equal pay for equal work

    ✍️ Practice

    The notified minimum is ₹480 and workers are paid ₹465. For 150 workers over 26 days, what is the monthly shortfall?

    Show answer
    15 × 26 × 150 = ₹58,500.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    📋 Compliance onboarding: steps and documents

    Compliance starts before the worker's first day. Steps: (1) check that the contractor holds a valid licence and registrations; (2) verify identity, address and age, and that the worker is of legal working age; (3) issue an appointment or engagement letter with pay, hours and terms; (4) enrol the worker in the required social security schemes; (5) record the worker in the attendance and wage registers; (6) give a safety induction; (7) issue wage slips each pay period.

    Records are the proof. If an inspector asks for a worker's file, it should show the engagement letter, identity proof, enrolment details, attendance and wage records. Missing documents are treated as missing compliance.

    DocumentPurpose
    Contractor licencePermission to supply labor
    Identity and age proofVerify the worker and legal age
    Engagement letterPay, hours and terms in writing
    PF and ESI enrolmentSocial security coverage
    Attendance and wage registersStatutory records
    Wage slipProof of pay and deductions
    Document completeness = Complete files ÷ Files audited
    🧪
    Worked example: An internal audit checks 50 worker files and 44 are complete. Completeness = 44 ÷ 50 = 88%. The 6 incomplete files are missing enrolment proof and are fixed within a week.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Letting workers start before documents are complete
    • Keeping records only in the supervisor's notebook
    • No internal audit before the inspector arrives

    ✍️ Practice

    An audit checks 80 files and 72 are complete. What is the completeness?

    Show answer
    72 ÷ 80 = 90%.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    🧮 Wage, hours and overtime calculations

    Correct pay calculations are central to compliance. Convert monthly wages to an hourly rate before computing overtime. A common method is monthly wage ÷ 26 working days ÷ 8 hours. Where the Factories Act applies, overtime is generally paid at twice the ordinary rate of wages. Other laws and some states differ, so confirm what applies to the site.

    Daily and weekly hour limits also matter. Asking workers to do long overtime to cover a gap may breach the rules, so rosters must be planned within limits.

    Check each payroll before it is paid: wage rate against the notified minimum, overtime against the log, deductions against what is allowed.

    ItemMethod
    Hourly rateMonthly wage ÷ 26 ÷ 8
    Overtime rate (illustrative)2 × hourly rate
    Overtime payOvertime hours × overtime rate
    CheckRate at or above notified minimum
    Overtime pay = Overtime hours × Hourly rate × 2 | Hourly rate = Monthly wage ÷ 26 ÷ 8
    🧪
    Worked example: A worker earns ₹20,800 a month: hourly rate = 20,800 ÷ 208 = ₹100. For 10 overtime hours, pay = 10 × 100 × 2 = ₹2,000. For 20 workers this is ₹40,000 for the month.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying overtime at the normal rate
    • Using a different divisor each month
    • Letting overtime exceed legal limits to cover a gap

    ✍️ Practice

    A worker earns ₹23,400 a month. What is the pay for 8 overtime hours at twice the hourly rate?

    Show answer
    Hourly = 23,400 ÷ 208 = ₹112.50. Pay = 8 × 112.50 × 2 = ₹1,800.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🛡️ Expert: misclassification, joint liability and audit readiness

    At expert level you manage risk across the whole arrangement. Misclassification means calling an employee a contractor to avoid dues. Sham contracting is a labor arrangement set up only to avoid the law. Some rules also restrict using contract labor for core activities of the client's business, so the client's work type has to be checked.

    The principal employer can often be held responsible if the contractor fails to pay wages or dues, so clients ask for proof of payment such as PF and ESI challans and wage registers. A strong specialist provides these proactively.

    Be audit-ready: keep records for the period the law requires, run quarterly internal audits, track changes in law, and keep a log of inspections and actions. Estimate your exposure so management understands the cost of non-compliance.

    RiskControl
    MisclassificationReview role and engagement type before onboarding
    Contractor fails to pay duesCollect proof of payment every month
    Inspector visitRecords ready, mock audits
    Law changeSubscribe to updates, review quarterly
    Exposure = Monthly shortfall per worker × Workers × Months (before penalties and interest)
    🧪
    Worked example: A shortfall of ₹520 per worker a month across 200 workers for 6 months gives 520 × 200 × 6 = ₹6,24,000 of back pay before any penalty or interest. Fixing the pay rate today stops it growing.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Ignoring client-side duties and exposure
    • Not collecting proof of statutory payments
    • Reviewing the law only after a notice

    ✍️ Practice

    A shortfall of ₹300 per worker a month across 120 workers for 4 months. What is the back-pay exposure?

    Show answer
    300 × 120 × 4 = ₹1,44,000, before penalties and interest.

    Fast roster fulfillment

    Delivering a full roster of ready-to-work people on the client's deadline, using a bench and a tight daily plan.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    ⚡ What fast roster fulfillment is and why it matters

    Roster fulfillment means supplying the number of workers a client needs for each shift and day. Fast roster fulfillment is doing this on a short deadline, such as 200 workers by Monday. It depends on having people ready before the request arrives and on a tight plan for the days in between.

    Why it matters: clients judge a staffing partner by how quickly it responds when they are under pressure. A fast and reliable fill wins repeat business and protects against penalties for late starts.

    TermMeaning
    RosterThe list of workers required by shift and day
    RequiredWorkers the client needs
    JoinedWorkers who have started
    PendingRequired minus joined
    BenchPre-screened workers ready to start soon
    Talent poolA wider list of past and prospective workers
    Daily trackerA live sheet of progress against the target
    Roster fulfillment % = Joined ÷ Required
    🧪
    Worked example: A client requires 200 workers and 170 have joined. Fulfillment = 170 ÷ 200 = 85%, with 30 pending.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Starting to search only after the request arrives
    • Not keeping a bench
    • Reporting numbers only at the end

    ✍️ Practice

    A client requires 150 workers and 138 have joined. What is the fulfillment percentage?

    Show answer
    138 ÷ 150 = 92%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of demand and sources of supply

    Demand arrives in different forms. Planned ramp-ups give weeks of notice. Seasonal peaks are predictable but large. Urgent replacements are small but need an answer within hours. One-off events need a team for a short window.

    Supply also has different sources, each with its own speed and cost: the bench (fastest), referrals from current workers, walk-in drives, redeploying workers from lower-priority clients and partner suppliers. A good plan decides in advance which source handles which type of demand.

    Demand typeTypical noticeBest supply source
    Planned ramp-upWeeksDrives and referrals
    Seasonal peakWeeks, predictableBench plus drives
    Urgent replacementHoursBench
    One-off eventDaysBench and partners
    Bench coverage = Bench size ÷ Expected demand
    🧪
    Worked example: The bench has 54 ready workers and the expected demand for the next peak is 120. Bench coverage = 54 ÷ 120 = 45%. The other 55% must come from drives and referrals.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating all demand as urgent
    • Having no bench for urgent replacements
    • Relying on a single source

    ✍️ Practice

    The bench has 45 ready workers and demand is 150. What is the bench coverage?

    Show answer
    45 ÷ 150 = 30%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🗓️ The fulfillment plan, step by step

    Imagine a client needs 200 workers by Monday. A tight plan runs backward from that day. Steps: (1) clarify the request: numbers, roles, shifts, location, pay, documents; (2) activate the bench and confirm availability; (3) open drives and referral calls; (4) screen and verify documents in batches; (5) run an induction session before the start day; (6) confirm transport and reporting time with each worker; (7) track daily and report to the client.

    Not everyone who is confirmed will turn up. Divide the target by the expected show-up rate to find how many candidates must be confirmed.

    DayAction
    T minus 5Clarify request, activate bench
    T minus 4 to 3Drives, referrals, screening
    T minus 2Documents, offers, batch induction
    T minus 1Confirm transport and reporting time
    TJoining day, attendance check
    Candidates to confirm = Required ÷ Show-up rate
    🧪
    Worked example: The client needs 200 workers on Monday and the expected show-up rate is 85%. Candidates to confirm = 200 ÷ 0.85 = 235.3, so confirm about 236 people.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Confirming only the exact number needed
    • Not confirming transport
    • Doing induction on the joining day, which delays work

    ✍️ Practice

    The client needs 120 workers and the expected show-up is 80%. How many should be confirmed?

    Show answer
    120 ÷ 0.8 = 150.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    📈 Tracking progress: run rate and forecast

    A daily tracker answers one question: will we finish on time? The run rate is joined workers divided by days elapsed. Divide the remaining requirement by the run rate to forecast how many more days are needed.

    If the forecast shows a miss, act early: add a drive, call the bench, bring in a partner supplier. Waiting until the last day leaves no room to fix it. Report the forecast to the client with the plan, not only the number.

    MeasureFormula
    Run rateJoined ÷ Days elapsed
    RemainingRequired − Joined
    Days to completeRemaining ÷ Run rate
    On track?Days elapsed + Days to complete ≤ Deadline
    Run rate = Joined ÷ Days | Days to complete = Remaining ÷ Run rate
    🧪
    Worked example: 200 workers are needed in 10 days. After 4 days 90 have joined: run rate = 90 ÷ 4 = 22.5 a day. Remaining = 110, so days to complete = 110 ÷ 22.5 = 4.9, about 5. Total = 9 days, so the plan is on track.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Looking only at total joined
    • Not forecasting
    • Telling the client only on the deadline day

    ✍️ Practice

    300 workers are needed in 12 days. After 5 days 125 have joined. What is the run rate, the days still needed and is it on track?

    Show answer
    Run rate = 125 ÷ 5 = 25 a day. Remaining = 175, so 7 days. 5 + 7 = 12 days, which just meets the deadline with no margin.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: surge capacity, partner suppliers and the cost of speed

    At expert level you build surge capacity ahead of time: a maintained bench, partner suppliers on standby with agreed rates and standard documents, and a ready induction kit. When a large urgent request arrives, you can scale in days rather than weeks.

    Speed has a cost. Urgent drives, extra recruiters and partner suppliers often cost more per hire. The decision is whether the premium is smaller than the cost of delay, which may include SLA penalties and lost client trust.

    Protect quality when rushing. Fast hires have a higher risk of early attrition, so keep verification and induction in place even under pressure, and track 30-day retention for rush hires separately.

    DecisionQuestion to ask
    Pay for speed?Is the premium less than the delay penalty?
    Use a partner supplier?Do they meet document and compliance standards?
    Relax screening?What is the early attrition risk?
    Build more bench?What is the cost of idle bench against missed demand?
    Net benefit of speed = Delay penalty avoided − (Premium per hire × Urgent hires)
    🧪
    Worked example: An urgent drive costs ₹2,200 per hire against ₹1,500 normally, a premium of ₹700. For 80 urgent hires the extra cost is ₹56,000. A 3-day delay would cost ₹25,000 a day in penalties, which is ₹75,000. Net benefit = 75,000 − 56,000 = ₹19,000, so paying for speed is justified.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Paying for speed without comparing the penalty
    • Using partner suppliers who skip compliance checks
    • Not tracking retention of rush hires

    ✍️ Practice

    The premium is ₹600 per hire for 60 urgent hires. A 2-day delay costs ₹20,000 a day. What is the net benefit of paying for speed?

    Show answer
    Extra cost = 600 × 60 = ₹36,000. Penalty avoided = 2 × 20,000 = ₹40,000. Net benefit = ₹4,000, which favours speed but only narrowly.

    Tools & Quality

    The tools, workflows and quality checks for each skill.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: High-volume blue-collar / white-collar temp hiring

    A volume hirer lives in a candidate tracker and a few simple checklists. The daily work is to open channels, move candidates stage by stage and read the funnel numbers every evening. Each tool below supports one step of the pipeline.

    Tool or techniqueUsed forExample
    Candidate trackerOne row per candidate, one column per stageMark 'Offered' and 'Joined' dates to see drop-off
    Funnel calculatorFinding leads needed from conversion rates300 joins at 12% overall needs 2,500 leads
    Screening checklistFast and uniform suitability checkAvailability, fitness, trade test, documents
    Document checklistIdentity, address, age and bank proofNo offer letter until all four are verified
    Referral and walk-in drive planCheap blue-collar sourcingVenue, date, screeners and transport planned
    Duplicate check sheetCatching repeat or fake candidates across drivesMatch by ID number and mobile number

    Working procedure

    1. Confirm the requisition with the client: numbers, roles, shift, site, pay and start date.
    2. Work backward from the target joins to leads needed using stage conversion rates.
    3. Pick channels for each group and set a cost per hire limit for each.

    🏗️ Project: Fill 250 workers in 10 days

    Skill: Fast roster fulfillment

    A client needs 250 workers in 10 days for a new shift. After 4 days, 88 have joined. The bench holds 60 ready workers and expected show-up is 80%. An urgent push would cost ₹650 extra per hire for 70 hires, while each day of delay costs ₹20,000 in penalties for an expected 3-day delay.

    ItemValue
    Required250 workers
    Deadline10 days
    Joined after 4 days88
    Bench60
    Penalty per day of delay₹20,000

    Your tasks

    1. Find the run rate, remaining workers and days to complete, and say if it is on track.
    2. Find the run rate needed for the remaining 6 days.
    3. Find the bench coverage and how many candidates to confirm for 250 at 80% show-up.

    Check your work

    Show worked solution
    Task 1: Run rate = 88 ÷ 4 = 22 a day. Remaining = 250 − 88 = 162. Days to complete = 162 ÷ 22 = 7.4, so 8 days. Total = 4 + 8 = 12 days, more than 10, so it is not on track.     Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: High-volume blue-collar / white-collar temp hiring

    Why must a drive start with more leads than the target joins?

    Show model answer
    Every stage loses candidates. Leads needed is the target divided by the product of all stage conversions. If the overall conversion is 12%, 100 joins need about 834 leads.

    How do blue-collar and white-collar hiring differ?

    Show model answer
    Blue-collar uses walk-ins, referrals and local contacts, and screens for availability, fitness and trade skill. White-collar uses portals and referrals, and screens by test and interview. Blue-collar risk is early drop-off; white-collar risk is notice-period delay.

    Joins fall short in the third week of a drive. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Check the funnel to find the weakest stage first. If offered-to-joined is low, call the offered candidates and fix pay or transport doubts. If leads are low, open another channel. Report the plan to the client early.

    A new channel gives cheap hires but half leave in a month. What do you decide?

    Show model answer
    Cheap cost per hire is misleading when retention is poor. Add the replacement cost to the true cost and compare channels on cost, offer-to-join and 30-day retention. Keep the channel only if the full cost is still lower.

    A drive needs 400 joins at 10% overall conversion. How many leads?

    Show model answer
    Leads = 400 ÷ 0.10 = 4,000. If the cost per lead is known, multiply to budget the sourcing spend.

    💬 Interview Questions: Contingent labor law compliance

    Who is responsible for compliance, contractor or principal employer?

    Show model answer
    Both. The contractor pays wages, makes contributions and keeps records. The principal employer must check the contractor's licence and can often be held responsible if dues are unpaid. Rules vary, so confirm current law.

    What is misclassification and why is it risky?

    Show model answer
    It means calling an employee a contractor to avoid dues. It leads to back pay, penalties and loss of licence. Review the role and engagement type before onboarding.

    An inspector arrives tomorrow and files are incomplete. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Do a quick audit of the sampled sites, list missing documents and collect what is available today. Do not backdate records. Prepare an honest status note with a fix date, and run mock audits afterwards.

    A client wants lower bill rates by skipping statutory contributions. How do you respond?

    Show model answer
    Decline politely and show the numbers. Statutory cost of about 13% is a legal cost, and skipping it creates exposure that is far larger. Offer savings elsewhere, such as lower margin or longer contracts.

    A worker earns ₹24,960 a month. Pay for 5 overtime hours at twice the rate?

    Show model answer
    Hourly = 24,960 ÷ 26 ÷ 8 = ₹120. Pay = 5 × 120 × 2 = ₹1,200. Confirm that twice the rate applies to the site.

    💬 Interview Questions: Fast roster fulfillment

    What is a bench and why does it matter?

    Show model answer
    A bench is a list of pre-screened workers who can start at short notice. It is the fastest supply for urgent demand and replacements. Without it every request starts from zero.

    How do you decide how many people to confirm?

    Show model answer
    Divide the required number by the expected show-up rate and round up. At 85% show-up, 200 workers need about 236 confirmed. Update the rate with actual data from each site.

    On day 5 you are behind plan for a Monday deadline. What now?

    Show model answer
    Recompute the run rate and the days needed. Call the bench, add a drive and ask a partner supplier to stand by. Tell the client the forecast with the recovery plan the same day.

    Should you relax screening to meet a deadline?

    Show model answer
    No. Fast hires already carry higher early attrition risk. Add screeners or batches, but keep identity checks and induction. Track 30-day retention of rush hires separately.

    180 workers are needed and show-up is 90%. How many to confirm?

    Show model answer
    Confirm = 180 ÷ 0.9 = 200 people.
