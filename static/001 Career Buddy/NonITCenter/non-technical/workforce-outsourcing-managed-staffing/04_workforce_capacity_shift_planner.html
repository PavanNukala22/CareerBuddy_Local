    Course Overview

    Workforce Capacity & Shift Planner: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches you to plan the people side of a service operation: how many staff are needed each interval, how to turn that into shifts and days off, how much extra cover to hold for absence, and how to forecast the work that will arrive. It is built around four linked skills and one shared model.

    The role matters because every service promise depends on having the right number of people at the right time. Too few people means missed service levels, penalties and tired teams. Too many means idle cost. A good planner saves lakhs of rupees a month and protects both customers and staff.

    The course has four skill tabs, each with five levels of content: tools and workflow, how quality is tested, good practices, a hands-on project, troubleshooting and interview practice. A final assessment and a summary page with formulas and a self-check close the course.

    Who this course is for

    • Workforce planners, schedulers and real-time analysts in contact centres, BPOs, helpdesks and shared-service teams.
    • Team leaders and operations managers who build rosters and defend headcount asks.
    • Analysts and fresh graduates who want an entry into workforce management and capacity planning.
    • Finance and operations partners who need to read staffing models and challenge their assumptions.

    How the course is organised

    • Skill tabs: four skills in a fixed chain, from Erlang staffing to rosters, absence buffers and forecasting.
    • Tools and workflow: the spreadsheets, tools and step-by-step procedure used in daily planning work.
    • Testing and quality: checks, pass standards and one quality KPI for each skill, with a worked example.
    • Good practices and projects: do and avoid pairs, then a small project with data, tasks and a verified solution.
    • Troubleshooting and interviews: symptom, cause and fix tables, plus five interview questions per skill with model answers.
    • Summary: recap, a formula sheet, an I can checklist and next learning steps.
    ⏱️
    Time needed: About 10 to 12 hours, including the four projects

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Calculate workload in Erlangs from volume, AHT and interval length.
    2. Determine the number of agents needed per interval to meet a service level target using an Erlang C table.
    3. Evaluate how AHT, volume and target changes move staffing and cost.
    4. Build a weekly roster from shift templates that matches the interval requirement.
    5. Check a roster for rest gaps, weekly offs and fairness of nights and weekends.
    6. Compare overtime and part-time cover for a recurring gap and recommend the cheaper option.
    7. Calculate roster size from required heads and shrinkage, and size a floater pool statistically.
    8. Compute MAPE, bias and seasonal indices to judge and adjust a forecast.
    9. Convert a volume forecast into required FTE and roster size.
    10. Present a staffing plan with stated assumptions, risks and a monthly cost.

    Module 2

    Erlang staffing models

    Using Erlang formulas to work out how many people are needed to answer work within a target time, interval by interval.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    📞 What Erlang models are and why they matter

    An Erlang is a unit of workload. One Erlang means one person busy for one full hour. If contacts arrive and each takes some time to handle, the total busy time per interval, measured in hours of work per hour, is the workload in Erlangs. Erlang models, named after the Danish engineer A. K. Erlang, turn that workload into the number of staff needed to meet a service target.

    Why they matter: staffing by simple averages fails because work arrives randomly. Even when average capacity equals average workload, queues build up during random bursts. Erlang formulas account for this randomness, so they give a realistic answer to the question 'how many people do I need so that most work is picked up within the target time?'

    They are used for contact centres, helpdesks, front desks, dispatch and any service where work arrives randomly and waits in a queue until someone is free.

    TermMeaning
    Workload (Erlangs)Total handling time per interval ÷ interval length
    AHTAverage handling time per item
    IntervalThe time block planned, often 15 or 30 minutes
    Service levelShare of work answered within a target time, such as 80% in 20 seconds
    ASAAverage speed of answer
    OccupancyShare of agent time spent on work
    AbandonmentCustomers who give up while waiting
    Workload (Erlangs) = Volume × AHT (seconds) ÷ Interval length (seconds)
    🧪
    Worked example: 120 contacts arrive in a 30-minute interval and AHT is 240 seconds. Workload = 120 × 240 ÷ 1,800 = 16 Erlangs. This means 16 agents would be busy all the time, which is the minimum, not the number to roster.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Rostering exactly the workload number of agents
    • Using a daily average instead of interval volumes
    • Mixing seconds and minutes in the formula

    ✍️ Practice

    90 contacts arrive in a 30-minute interval and AHT is 300 seconds. What is the workload in Erlangs?

    Show answer
    90 × 300 ÷ 1,800 = 15 Erlangs.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of Erlang models and key measures

    There are three models you will meet. Erlang B assumes there is no queue: if all agents are busy, the contact is blocked and lost. It is used for phone lines and trunks. Erlang C assumes contacts wait in a queue and nobody abandons. It is the standard for staffing. Erlang A extends C by letting some customers abandon after waiting, which is more realistic when queues are long.

    Occupancy is a key companion measure: workload divided by agents. A very high occupancy means agents never get a break between contacts and burn out. A very low occupancy means paid time is idle. Many operations target 80 to 88 percent for phone work.

    ModelAssumptionTypical use
    Erlang BNo queue; extra contacts are blockedPhone lines, trunks
    Erlang CQueue with no abandonmentStaffing agents
    Erlang AQueue with abandonmentLong queues, realistic planning
    Occupancy = Workload (Erlangs) ÷ Agents
    🧪
    Worked example: The workload is 16 Erlangs and 19 agents are on duty. Occupancy = 16 ÷ 19 = 84.2%. With 20 agents, occupancy would be 80%.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning for 100% occupancy
    • Using Erlang B for a queue
    • Ignoring occupancy and breaks

    ✍️ Practice

    The workload is 15 Erlangs and 18 agents are on duty. What is occupancy?

    Show answer
    15 ÷ 18 = 83.3%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🧮 Erlang C step by step

    To staff with Erlang C: (1) compute the workload A in Erlangs; (2) start with N agents slightly above A, because N must be greater than A or the queue grows without limit; (3) compute the probability that a contact has to wait, P(wait); (4) compute the service level using P(wait), N, A, the target time T and AHT; (5) if the service level is below target, add one agent and repeat.

    In practice a spreadsheet or workforce management tool does the arithmetic. The planner's job is to understand the inputs and check that the result makes sense. The table below shows the search for the 16 Erlang example with a target of 80 percent answered within 20 seconds and AHT of 240 seconds.

    Agents NP(wait)Service levelOccupancy
    1853.1%55.0%88.9%
    1937.4%70.9%84.2%
    2025.6%81.7%80.0%
    2117.1%88.7%76.2%
    P(wait) = [Aᴺ/N! × N/(N−A)] ÷ [Σ(k=0..N−1) Aᵏ/k! + Aᴺ/N! × N/(N−A)] | SL = 1 − P(wait) × e^(−(N−A)×T/AHT)
    🧪
    Worked example: For a workload of 16 Erlangs, 19 agents give 70.9% service level, which misses the 80% target. 20 agents give 81.7%, which meets it. So the plan is 20 agents for this interval, at 80% occupancy and an average speed of answer of about 15 seconds.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Stopping at the first N above A
    • Using a daily target instead of an interval target
    • Forgetting that 20 seconds and 240 seconds must be in the same unit

    ✍️ Practice

    The workload is 10 Erlangs with AHT of 240 seconds. The target is 80% in 20 seconds. Service level is 77.8% at 13 agents and 87.5% at 14 agents. How many agents are needed and what is occupancy?

    Show answer
    14 agents are needed because 13 misses the target. Occupancy = 10 ÷ 14 = 71.4%.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    📈 Scenarios and KPIs: what moves the staffing number

    Three inputs drive the answer: volume, AHT and the service target. A small change in AHT changes the staffing number noticeably, so process improvements and coaching that shorten handling time have a direct cost effect.

    Core KPIs to track every interval are service level, ASA, occupancy, abandonment rate and agents on duty against agents required. Review them against the plan weekly. Large gaps point to a forecast error, an adherence problem or a wrong AHT in the model.

    ChangeEffect on workloadEffect on agents
    AHT falls from 240 s to 210 s16 to 14 Erlangs20 to 18 agents
    Volume rises 25%16 to 20 Erlangs20 to 25 agents
    Tighter target (90/20)Same workloadMore agents
    Agents saved = Old requirement − New requirement | Monthly saving = Agents saved × Cost per agent
    🧪
    Worked example: AHT falls from 240 to 210 seconds, so workload falls from 16 to 14 Erlangs. At 14 Erlangs, 17 agents give 73.8% (below target) and 18 agents give 84.2%, so 18 agents are needed against 20 before. At ₹25,000 a month per agent, the saving is 2 × ₹25,000 = ₹50,000 a month for this interval pattern.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Assuming a straight-line relationship between workload and agents
    • Not updating AHT after process changes
    • Reading only the daily average service level

    ✍️ Practice

    Workload rises from 16 to 20 Erlangs and the requirement rises from 20 to 25 agents. At ₹25,000 per agent per month, what is the extra monthly cost?

    Show answer
    5 extra agents × ₹25,000 = ₹1,25,000 a month.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: pooling, limits of Erlang C and improving the model

    Pooling is the most important idea at expert level. Larger groups are more efficient than small ones, because random peaks in one queue are absorbed by idle agents who would otherwise serve another queue. Combining two teams of 8 Erlangs each, each needing 11 agents, gives one team of 16 Erlangs needing 20 agents. That is a saving of 2 agents with the same service level.

    Know the limits of Erlang C. It assumes contacts arrive randomly in a steady way, that nobody abandons, that all agents have the same skills and that handling time is consistent. Real operations have abandonment, multi-skilled agents, call-backs and sudden spikes. Experts adjust with Erlang A, simulation or a buffer on top of the Erlang result, and they reforecast intraday.

    Leading the process means keeping the model honest: compare predicted and actual service level, correct AHT regularly and document assumptions.

    LimitBetter approach
    No abandonmentErlang A or simulation
    Multi-skilled agentsSkill-based routing models
    Spikes and burstsIntraday reforecast and flexible cover
    Stale AHTMonthly AHT refresh
    Pooling gain = (Agents for separate teams) − (Agents for pooled team)
    🧪
    Worked example: Two teams each carry 8 Erlangs and each needs 11 agents for 80% in 20 seconds, 22 in total. Pooled, 16 Erlangs need 20 agents. Pooling gain = 22 − 20 = 2 agents, or ₹50,000 a month at ₹25,000 per agent.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Splitting queues by habit rather than need
    • Trusting Erlang C results for highly bursty work
    • Never comparing the forecast service level with actual

    ✍️ Practice

    Two teams each carry 6 Erlangs and need 9 agents each. Pooled, 12 Erlangs need 16 agents. What is the pooling gain?

    Show answer
    Separate = 9 + 9 = 18. Pooled = 16. Gain = 2 agents.
    Module 3

    Roster / shift scheduling

    Turning the staffing requirement into shifts and days off that cover demand, follow the rules and feel fair.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗓️ What rostering is and why it matters

    A roster is the plan that says who works which shift on which day. The staffing requirement tells you how many people are needed at each time. Rostering turns that requirement into real shifts, days off and rest periods, while respecting working-time rules and being fair to workers.

    Why it matters: a good roster puts the right number of people at the right times, controls overtime cost and keeps workers rested and willing to stay. A poor roster gives overstaffing at quiet times, understaffing at busy times and unhappy people.

    TermMeaning
    ShiftA defined block of working time
    RosterThe plan of shifts by person and day
    CoverageStaff scheduled against staff required
    Weekly offThe rest day each worker is entitled to
    FTEFull-time equivalent, a unit of staff capacity
    Shift templateA standard shift pattern, such as 6 to 2
    FTE needed = Required hours per week ÷ Hours per FTE per week
    🧪
    Worked example: A site needs 1,200 hours of work a week and one full-time worker contributes 48 hours. FTE needed = 1,200 ÷ 48 = 25. This is before allowing for absence.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Rostering to the budget instead of the demand
    • Forgetting weekly offs when counting capacity
    • Building the roster before the forecast is ready

    ✍️ Practice

    A site needs 1,440 hours of work a week and one full-time worker contributes 48 hours. How many FTE are needed?

    Show answer
    1,440 ÷ 48 = 30 FTE.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of shift patterns

    Patterns differ in how they trade fairness, flexibility and cost. Fixed shifts give workers a stable life but few want nights. Rotating shifts share nights and weekends but can disturb sleep if rotated badly; forward rotation (morning, then evening, then night) is easier on the body. Split shifts cover two peaks with a gap but are unpopular. Compressed weeks pack hours into fewer, longer days. Part-time and on-call workers add flexibility for peaks.

    PatternStrengthWatch-out
    FixedStable routineHard to staff nights
    RotatingShares unpopular shiftsFatigue if rotation is poor
    SplitCovers two peaksLong day, unpopular
    Compressed weekFewer commute daysFatigue in long days
    Part-time or on callFlexible for peaksLess reliable, less trained
    Coverage ratio = Staff scheduled ÷ Staff required
    🧪
    Worked example: An interval requires 20 staff and 23 are scheduled. Coverage = 23 ÷ 20 = 115%, which means 3 extra people. Another interval requires 18 and has 16 scheduled: 16 ÷ 18 = 88.9%, which is 2 short.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using the same pattern for every role
    • Rotating backward (night to morning) with short rest
    • Splitting shifts without extra pay or notice

    ✍️ Practice

    An interval requires 18 staff and 16 are scheduled. What is the coverage ratio, and how many are short?

    Show answer
    16 ÷ 18 = 88.9%. Short by 2.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🔧 Building a roster, step by step

    Steps: (1) get the interval requirement from the staffing model; (2) define shift templates that match the demand curve; (3) calculate how many workers each template needs; (4) assign people to templates; (5) check rules: weekly off, maximum hours, rest between shifts and leave already approved; (6) check fairness of nights and weekends; (7) publish early, so workers can plan; (8) manage swaps and changes with clear rules.

    A quick capacity check is the shift-slot method. Count the shift slots needed in a week (shifts per day times people per shift times days) and divide by the number of shifts each worker can do in a week.

    StepOutput
    Requirement by intervalStaffing numbers
    Shift templatesStart and end times
    Assign peopleDraft roster
    Rule checkCompliance flags
    PublishRoster at least a week ahead
    Workers needed = Shift slots per week ÷ Shifts per worker per week
    🧪
    Worked example: A 24 by 7 site runs 3 shifts a day with 10 people per shift: 3 × 10 × 7 = 210 shift slots a week. Each worker does 6 shifts, so workers needed = 210 ÷ 6 = 35, before an absence buffer.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Publishing the roster at the last minute
    • Skipping the rules check
    • Not allowing for approved leave

    ✍️ Practice

    A site runs 3 shifts a day with 12 people per shift for 7 days. Each worker does 6 shifts a week. How many workers are needed?

    Show answer
    3 × 12 × 7 = 252 slots. 252 ÷ 6 = 42 workers.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    💰 Fairness, overtime and cost trade-offs

    Fairness is measured: count each person's nights, weekends and public-holiday shifts and compare them. Large gaps lead to complaints and attrition.

    Cost trade-offs also need numbers. When there is a gap in coverage, a manager can ask people to work overtime or add part-time cover. Overtime is paid at a higher rate, often double where the law or policy says so, so it is usually more expensive than a part-timer for regular gaps. Overtime also adds fatigue.

    OptionRateHoursCost
    Overtime (2× ₹120)₹240120₹28,800
    Part-timer₹130120₹15,600
    Saving with part-timer₹13,200
    Overtime cost = Gap hours × Base rate × 2 (illustrative) | Part-timer cost = Gap hours × Part-time rate
    🧪
    Worked example: A recurring gap of 120 hours a month. Overtime at twice the base rate of ₹120 costs 120 × 240 = ₹28,800. A part-timer at ₹130 an hour costs 120 × 130 = ₹15,600. Saving = ₹13,200 a month, and workers are less tired.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using overtime for permanent gaps
    • Not tracking overtime per person
    • Ignoring fatigue and error costs

    ✍️ Practice

    A gap of 160 hours a month. Base rate ₹100 with overtime at twice that. A part-timer costs ₹115 an hour. What is the saving from using the part-timer?

    Show answer
    Overtime = 160 × 200 = ₹32,000. Part-timer = 160 × 115 = ₹18,400. Saving = ₹13,600.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🛡️ Expert: legal limits, fatigue and optimisation

    Working-time rules limit daily and weekly hours, require weekly rest and often set minimum rest between shifts and restrictions on night work. These rules differ by state and by industry and change over time, so confirm current requirements for each site before finalising a roster.

    Fatigue is a business risk. Quick turnarounds, long runs of nights and extended overtime increase errors and accidents. A common check is the rest gap between the end of one shift and the start of the next.

    At expert level, rosters are built with optimisation tools that balance coverage, cost, rules and preferences, with options such as shift bidding, self-scheduling and structured swaps. Review the results against actual demand each week and feed the lessons back into the next cycle.

    RiskControl
    Short rest between shiftsAutomatic rest-gap check
    Long overtime runsCap per person per week
    Unfair nightsFairness report each cycle
    Last-minute changesSwap rules and notice period
    Rest gap = Next shift start − Previous shift end
    🧪
    Worked example: A worker finishes a night shift at 06:00 and is rostered to start at 14:00 the same day. Rest gap = 8 hours. If policy requires at least 11 hours, this roster line is a violation and must be changed.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Checking rules only after publishing
    • Ignoring fatigue because coverage looks fine
    • Letting swaps bypass the rules check

    ✍️ Practice

    A worker finishes at 22:00 and starts the next shift at 07:00. The policy requires at least 10 hours of rest. Is it a violation?

    Show answer
    Rest gap = 9 hours. It is a violation, 1 hour short.
    Module 4

    Absenteeism buffering

    Adding the right amount of extra capacity in advance so absence does not leave the floor short.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🛡️ What absenteeism buffering is and why it matters

    Absenteeism buffering means planning extra capacity in advance to cover people who will not be at work. Absence is predictable in aggregate even though you cannot know who will be absent on a given day. If 8 percent of rostered people are typically absent, rostering exactly the requirement guarantees a shortfall.

    Why it matters: shortages cause missed targets, penalties, overtime and stress for those present. The buffer protects service. But a buffer that is too big wastes money on idle people, so the aim is the right size.

    TermMeaning
    Absenteeism rateShare of scheduled days lost to absence
    ShrinkageAll time that scheduled staff are not available for productive work
    Planned shrinkageLeave, training, meetings
    Unplanned shrinkageSickness, no-shows, lateness
    BufferExtra capacity rostered to cover shrinkage
    FloaterA cross-trained worker moved to where gaps appear
    Absenteeism rate = Absent days ÷ Scheduled days
    🧪
    Worked example: A team of 25 is scheduled for 26 days: 25 × 26 = 650 scheduled days. 52 days were lost to absence. Rate = 52 ÷ 650 = 8%.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Planning to the requirement with no buffer
    • Measuring absence only monthly, not by day
    • Treating all absence as the same

    ✍️ Practice

    A team of 30 is scheduled for 26 days and 39 days were lost to absence. What is the absenteeism rate?

    Show answer
    30 × 26 = 780 scheduled days. 39 ÷ 780 = 5%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of shrinkage and buffering methods

    Shrinkage has two parts. Planned shrinkage can be scheduled around: annual leave, training, team meetings and coaching. Unplanned shrinkage cannot: sickness, no-shows, lateness and emergencies. Total shrinkage adds them, and it should always be measured over the same base of scheduled time.

    There are several ways to buffer. Over-rostering adds extra people. A floater or standby pool covers gaps on the day. Cross-training lets people cover different tasks. Flexible hours and overtime help in short bursts. Most operations combine two or three methods.

    MethodStrengthWeakness
    Over-rosteringSimple, always presentIdle cost on good days
    Floater poolMoves to where neededNeeds cross-training
    Cross-trainingWide coverTraining time
    OvertimeFast, no hiringCostly, tiring
    Total shrinkage = Planned shrinkage + Unplanned shrinkage
    🧪
    Worked example: Planned shrinkage is 10 percent (leave 6, training 4) and unplanned is 8 percent. Total shrinkage = 10 + 8 = 18%.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Ignoring planned shrinkage
    • Not separating planned from unplanned
    • Using last year's numbers without updating

    ✍️ Practice

    Planned shrinkage is 9% and unplanned is 6%. What is the total?

    Show answer
    9 + 6 = 15%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🧮 Calculating the buffer, step by step

    Steps: (1) state the requirement as the number of people who must be at work; (2) measure total shrinkage from your own data, by shift and day of week; (3) calculate the roster size; (4) round up to a whole person; (5) set the floater pool for the unplanned part; (6) review monthly.

    The formula divides by one minus shrinkage rather than multiplying by one plus shrinkage. This is because shrinkage is a share of the rostered people, not a share of the requirement. Adding 15 percent to 100 gives 115, which is too low; 100 ÷ 0.85 gives 118.

    RequiredShrinkageRoster size
    10015%118
    8012%91
    12020%150
    Roster size = Required ÷ (1 − Shrinkage), rounded up
    🧪
    Worked example: 100 people are required on a shift and shrinkage is 15%. Roster size = 100 ÷ 0.85 = 117.65, so 118 people. The buffer is 18 people.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Multiplying by 1.15 instead of dividing by 0.85
    • Not rounding up
    • Using one shrinkage number for every day

    ✍️ Practice

    80 people are required and shrinkage is 12%. How many should be rostered?

    Show answer
    80 ÷ 0.88 = 90.9, so 91 people.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    💰 Cost of buffer against cost of shortage

    A buffer costs money every day, whether or not people are absent. A shortage costs money only on the days it happens, but it can cost more when it does, through lost output, penalties and overtime. The decision compares the two.

    The comparison should use real costs: the monthly cost of each extra person, and the cost of each missing person-day including lost output and penalties.

    ItemCalculationAmount
    Buffer cost18 extra × ₹18,000₹3,24,000
    Shortage cost12 missing × 26 days × ₹1,200₹3,74,400
    Buffer saves₹50,400
    Buffer cost = Extra people × Monthly cost | Shortage cost = Missing per day × Days × Cost per missing person-day
    🧪
    Worked example: A buffer of 18 extra people at ₹18,000 a month costs ₹3,24,000. Without it, an average of 12 people would be missing each day at ₹1,200 per person-day over 26 days, which is ₹3,74,400. The buffer saves ₹50,400 a month.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Comparing only the buffer cost
    • Using only the penalty and ignoring lost output
    • Not testing the numbers each quarter

    ✍️ Practice

    A buffer of 10 extra people costs ₹18,000 each a month. Without it, 8 people would be missing each day, costing ₹1,000 per person-day over 26 days. Which is cheaper and by how much?

    Show answer
    Buffer = ₹1,80,000. Shortage = 8 × 26 × 1,000 = ₹2,08,000. The buffer is cheaper by ₹28,000.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: statistical buffers and pooling

    Absence varies from day to day. A buffer sized to the average will be short about half the days. A statistical buffer covers the average plus a safety margin. With a 95 percent cover target and roughly normal, independent absences, the buffer is the mean plus 1.645 times the standard deviation.

    Pooling makes the buffer cheaper. Independent absences partly cancel each other out when groups are combined, so the standard deviation of the combined group grows only with the square root of the sum of variances. A shared floater pool for several lines needs fewer heads than a separate buffer for each line.

    These calculations assume absences are independent and roughly normal. Check for clusters, such as an illness outbreak or post-holiday absence, and keep a contingency plan for them.

    ApproachBuffer
    Mean onlyShort about half the days
    Mean + 1.645 SDCovers about 95% of days
    Pooled across linesFewer total heads
    Buffer (95%) = Mean + 1.645 × SD | Pooled SD = √(SD₁² + SD₂² + …)
    🧪
    Worked example: Four lines each average 12 absent with SD 3. Separate buffers: 12 + 1.645 × 3 = 16.9, so 17 each, 68 in total. Pooled: mean 48 and SD = √(4 × 9) = 6, so 48 + 1.645 × 6 = 57.9, about 58. Pooling saves 10 heads.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Sizing to the mean
    • Assuming absences are independent when they cluster
    • Pooling across lines without cross-training

    ✍️ Practice

    Three lines each average 10 absent with SD 2. What are the total buffers separately and pooled at 95%, and what is the saving?

    Show answer
    Separate: 10 + 1.645 × 2 = 13.29, so 14 each, 42 in total. Pooled: mean 30, SD = √12 = 3.46, so 30 + 5.70 = 35.7, about 36. Saving = 6 heads.
    Module 5

    Demand-forecasting data analysis

    Using past data and known events to predict how much work will arrive, so capacity can be planned before it is needed.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    📊 What demand forecasting is and why it matters

    Demand forecasting predicts how much work will arrive in a future period, for example contacts per interval, orders per day or shifts needed per week. It is the first step in capacity planning, because the staffing and roster plans are only as good as the forecast beneath them.

    A forecast is built from history and known events. Data patterns have parts: a baseline level, a trend (rising or falling over time), seasonality (repeating patterns by hour, weekday or month) and noise (random variation that cannot be predicted). Good forecasting separates these parts.

    TermMeaning
    DemandWork arriving, such as contacts or shifts requested
    TrendLong-term rise or fall
    SeasonalityRepeating pattern by hour, day or month
    NoiseUnpredictable random variation
    HorizonHow far ahead the forecast looks
    GranularityTime unit: interval, day, week
    Forecast errorDifference between forecast and actual
    Moving average forecast = Sum of last n periods ÷ n
    🧪
    Worked example: Weekly volumes for the last 4 weeks were 400, 420, 380 and 400. The 4-week moving average = (400 + 420 + 380 + 400) ÷ 4 = 400. The forecast for next week is 400.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Forecasting without cleaning outliers
    • Using too coarse a time unit
    • Ignoring known events

    ✍️ Practice

    Show answer

    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of forecasting methods

    Methods range from simple to complex. The naive method uses the last value. Moving averages smooth noise. Weighted averages give recent data more weight. Exponential smoothing updates the forecast each period using a smoothing factor between 0 and 1. Seasonal indices adjust for repeating patterns. Regression and machine-learning models use many drivers, such as promotions, weather or client plans. Qualitative input, such as the client's own plan, is also valuable and often beats statistics for one-off events.

    The best method is the simplest one that is accurate enough. Test methods on past data before relying on them.

    MethodBest forWeakness
    NaiveStable demandCopies noise
    Moving averageSmoothing noiseSlow to react
    Exponential smoothingResponsive and simpleNeeds a factor choice
    Seasonal indexRepeating weekly or daily patternsNeeds enough history
    Regression or MLMany driversNeeds data and skill
    New forecast = α × Latest actual + (1 − α) × Previous forecast
    🧪
    Worked example: With α = 0.3, the previous forecast was 400 and the actual was 440. New forecast = 0.3 × 440 + 0.7 × 400 = 132 + 280 = 412.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Setting α without testing
    • Using a complex model when a simple one works
    • Ignoring client plans

    ✍️ Practice

    With α = 0.2, the previous forecast was 500 and the actual was 450. What is the new forecast?

    Show answer
    0.2 × 450 + 0.8 × 500 = 90 + 400 = 490.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🔧 The forecasting process and seasonal indices

    Steps: (1) gather history at the right granularity; (2) clean the data, removing errors and flagging one-off events; (3) look for trend and seasonality; (4) choose a method and test it on past data; (5) produce the forecast; (6) adjust for known events such as promotions or festivals; (7) review with the client; (8) track accuracy and improve.

    A seasonal index measures how a period compares with the average. A weekday index is that day's average volume divided by the overall daily average. An index above 1 means a busier-than-average day. To forecast a specific day, multiply the forecast average by the day's index.

    DayAverage volumeIndex
    Monday1251.25
    Wednesday1001.00
    Friday800.80
    Seasonal index = Period average ÷ Overall average | Day forecast = Average forecast × Index
    🧪
    Worked example: Monday averages 125 against an overall daily average of 100, so the index is 1.25. If next week's average is forecast at 110 a day, the Monday forecast = 110 × 1.25 = 137.5.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using too little history for seasonality
    • Not cleaning one-off spikes
    • Forecasting daily totals and assuming a flat profile

    ✍️ Practice

    Friday averages 80 against an overall daily average of 100. Next week's average is forecast at 120. What is the Friday forecast?

    Show answer
    Index = 80 ÷ 100 = 0.80. Forecast = 120 × 0.80 = 96.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    🎯 Forecast accuracy: MAPE and bias

    A forecast is useful only if you know how accurate it is. Mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) measures the average size of errors as a percentage of actual. Bias shows whether the forecast is consistently too high or too low. A forecast with low MAPE but strong bias still leads to systematic over or under staffing.

    Track both by interval type and by week, and look at the largest misses. Investigate causes: was it a one-off event, a data problem or a model problem?

    WeekActualForecastAbs error %
    110011010.0%
    21201154.2%
    39010011.1%
    41101009.1%
    MAPE = Average of (|Actual − Forecast| ÷ Actual) | Bias = Σ(Forecast − Actual) ÷ Σ Actual
    🧪
    Worked example: For the four weeks above, errors are 10.0%, 4.2%, 11.1% and 9.1%, so MAPE = 34.4 ÷ 4 = 8.6%. Bias = (10 − 5 + 10 − 10) ÷ 420 = 5 ÷ 420 = +1.2%, a slight over-forecast.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Looking only at MAPE and not bias
    • Averaging errors with signs, so they cancel
    • Not investigating the biggest misses

    ✍️ Practice

    Actuals are 200, 180 and 220 and forecasts are 190, 190 and 200. What is MAPE?

    Show answer
    Errors: 10 ÷ 200 = 5.0%; 10 ÷ 180 = 5.6%; 20 ÷ 220 = 9.1%. MAPE = 19.6 ÷ 3 = 6.5%.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: from forecast to heads, and planning under uncertainty

    The forecast must be converted into staffing. Required FTE equals workload hours divided by the productive hours each FTE provides at the target occupancy. Then shrinkage is added to get the number to roster.

    Plan for uncertainty. A forecast is a central estimate with a range around it. If MAPE is about 8 percent, a forecast of 3,000 contacts could realistically be 2,760 to 3,240. Experts plan the base on the forecast and hold flexible capacity, such as part-timers, floaters and overtime rules, for the range.

    Lead the process by agreeing a forecast calendar with the client, sharing accuracy reports and building in event feedback: promotions, festivals and system changes should reach the planner before they arrive.

    StepCalculation
    Workload hoursVolume × AHT in hours
    Productive FTEWorkload ÷ (Productive hours × Occupancy)
    Roster sizeProductive FTE ÷ (1 − Shrinkage)
    Required FTE = (Volume × AHT hours) ÷ (Productive hours per FTE × Target occupancy)
    🧪
    Worked example: 3,000 contacts a day at 6 minutes (0.1 hour) AHT give 300 workload hours. With 6.5 productive hours per FTE and 85% occupancy, required FTE = 300 ÷ (6.5 × 0.85) = 54.3, so 55. With 15% shrinkage, roster = 55 ÷ 0.85 = 64.7, so 65.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Forgetting occupancy
    • Not adding shrinkage
    • Planning only to the central forecast with no flexible capacity

    ✍️ Practice

    2,400 contacts a day with AHT of 9 minutes (0.15 hour), 6.5 productive hours per FTE and 80% occupancy. How many FTE are required?

    Show answer
    Workload = 2,400 × 0.15 = 360 hours. FTE = 360 ÷ (6.5 × 0.8) = 360 ÷ 5.2 = 69.2, so 70.

    💬 Interview Questions: Erlang staffing models

    What does one Erlang mean?

    Show model answer
    One Erlang is one person busy for one full hour. Workload in Erlangs is handling time in an interval divided by the interval length. It is the minimum, not the number to roster.

    Why is the Erlang C number higher than the workload?

    Show model answer
    Work arrives randomly, so queues form in bursts even when average capacity matches workload. Extra agents absorb bursts so most contacts are answered in the target time.

    Your manager asks to cut staff by 10 percent because the average service level is 88 percent. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    I check interval level results, not the daily average. Peak intervals may miss while quiet ones are over-served. I run a what-if to show the service level and occupancy after the cut, then recommend shifting hours rather than cutting.

    A client wants 90 in 20 instead of 80 in 20. How do you respond?

    Show model answer
    I run both targets on the same intervals and present the extra agents and monthly cost. The client then decides with the price in view.

    40 contacts arrive in a 15-minute interval with AHT of 180 seconds, and 12 agents are on duty. What are workload and occupancy?

    Show model answer
    Workload = 40 × 180 ÷ 900 = 8 Erlangs. Occupancy = 8 ÷ 12 = 66.7%, which is low, so the interval may be overstaffed.

    💬 Interview Questions: Roster / shift scheduling

    What is FTE and why do planners use it?

    Show model answer
    FTE is full-time equivalent, a unit of capacity. A person working half the hours counts as 0.5. Planners use FTE to compare required hours with available hours across different contracts.

    Why is forward rotation preferred?

    Show model answer
    Moving from morning to evening to night lets the body clock shift later, which is easier than moving earlier. It also gives longer rest between shift changes.

    Two people ask to swap shifts the day before. How do you handle it?

    Show model answer
    I check that both still meet rest gap and weekly off rules and that coverage stays above the threshold. If it passes, I approve and log it. If not, I decline and offer another option.

    Overtime is requested every month for the same night gap. What do you recommend?

    Show model answer
    A recurring gap is a staffing problem. I compare overtime cost with a part-timer or a new hire and also count the fatigue risk. Usually I recommend fixed cover.

    A site needs 1,008 hours of work a week and one FTE gives 48 hours. How many FTE are needed?

    Show model answer
    1,008 ÷ 48 = 21 FTE, before adding cover for absence.

    💬 Interview Questions: Absenteeism buffering

    What is the difference between absenteeism and shrinkage?

    Show model answer
    Absenteeism is lost days due to absence. Shrinkage is all time scheduled staff are not available for work, including leave, training, meetings and unplanned absence.

    Why divide by one minus shrinkage?

    Show model answer
    Shrinkage is a share of rostered people, not of the requirement. If 15 percent of the roster is away, only 85 percent are present, so required ÷ 0.85 is correct.

    Absence spikes after long weekends. How do you plan for it?

    Show model answer
    I measure absence by weekday and period, raise the buffer for those days, and pre-agree standby and overtime rules. I also review leave approvals around the long weekend.

    Management wants to remove the buffer to save cost. What is your answer?

    Show model answer
    I compare buffer cost with shortage cost using real numbers. If shortage is costlier, I show the numbers. I may propose a smaller pooled buffer as a compromise.

    60 people are required and shrinkage is 10 percent. What is the roster size?

    Show model answer
    60 ÷ 0.90 = 66.7, rounded up to 67. The buffer is 7 people.

    💬 Interview Questions: Demand-forecasting data analysis

    What are the parts of a demand pattern?

    Show model answer
    Baseline level, trend, seasonality and noise. Good forecasting separates them so noise is not treated as a pattern.

    Why track bias as well as MAPE?

    Show model answer
    MAPE shows the size of errors but not their direction. Bias shows if forecasts are consistently high or low, which leads to systematic over or under staffing.

    The client plans a promotion next week but gave no volume estimate. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    I ask for the dates, channels and past promotion results. I use the volume uplift from a similar past event, set a range, and hold flexible capacity such as part-timers for the high side.

    Your forecast is accurate on average but peaks are missed. What is the cause and fix?

    Show model answer
    Daily or weekly totals hide the intraday profile. I build interval seasonal indices and forecast at interval level, then compare errors for peak intervals.

    With α = 0.5, the previous forecast was 600 and the actual was 640. What is the new forecast?

    Show model answer
    0.5 × 640 + 0.5 × 600 = 320 + 300 = 620.

    📚 What you covered

    Erlang staffing models

    • Workload in Erlangs = Volume × AHT ÷ Interval seconds.
    • Staff above the workload until the service target is met.
    • Check occupancy and refresh AHT often.
    • Pooling queues reduces agents needed.

    Roster / shift scheduling

    • Workers needed = Shift slots ÷ Shifts per worker.
    • Check rest gap, weekly off and fairness before publishing.
    • Use part-time cover for recurring gaps.
    • Rules differ by state; confirm current rules.

    Absenteeism buffering

    • Roster = Required ÷ (1 − Shrinkage), rounded up.
    • Separate planned and unplanned shrinkage.
    • Compare buffer and shortage cost.
    • Pooled floaters need fewer heads.

    Demand-forecasting data analysis

    • Clean history and use seasonal indices.
    • Track MAPE and bias together.
    • Convert volume to FTE with occupancy.
    • Plan a range, not a single number.
