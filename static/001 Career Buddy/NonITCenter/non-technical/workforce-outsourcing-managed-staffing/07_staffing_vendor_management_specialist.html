    Module 1

    Course Overview

    Staffing Vendor Management Specialist: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a vendor management specialist runs a managed staffing programme where a primary vendor supplies workers through secondary suppliers. It covers tracking supplier performance, tiering suppliers by service level, auditing compliance and negotiating markup.

    The role matters because the client holds the prime responsible for every worker. A specialist who measures suppliers fairly, rewards good ones, checks compliance with evidence and keeps prices fair protects service, cost and legal standing at once.

    The course has five parts. A short overview and prerequisites come first. Then each of the four skills has tools, testing, practices, a project, troubleshooting and interview questions. A summary with formulas and a checklist closes the course.

    Who this course is for

    • Staffing coordinators who want to move into vendor management.
    • Procurement and HR executives who manage contract labour suppliers.
    • Recruitment operations analysts working with a managed service provider.
    • Vendor managers at primes or clients who want a structured toolkit.

    How the course is organised

    • Overview and objectives: what you will be able to do.
    • Prerequisites: knowledge, tools and a three-question self-check.
    • Skill tools and workflow: the spreadsheets, reports and step-by-step method for each skill.
    • Testing and quality: checks and a KPI formula for each skill.
    • Good practices and a project: dos and avoids, then a case with verified working.
    • Troubleshooting, interviews and summary: common problems, model answers, formulas and next steps.
    ⏱️
    Time needed: About 10 to 12 hours

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Calculate supplier fill rate, on-time submission rate and submissions per placement.
    2. Build a weighted scorecard and apply a rolling three-month average.
    3. Design tier cut-offs, SLAs and volume shares for a supplier panel.
    4. Calculate expected fills, SLA attainment and supplier share against a cap.
    5. Plan risk-based audits using likelihood times impact.
    6. Classify findings and compute an audit score and rating.
    7. Calculate sample error rate and audit coverage.
    8. Break a markup into statutory cost, overhead and margin.
    9. Compute the break-even markup and judge whether an offer is safe.
    10. Convert markup changes into annual rupee savings for approvers.

    📋 Prerequisites

    What you should already know

    • Percentages, ratios and simple weighted averages.
    • Basic idea of staffing, bill rate and pay rate.
    • Awareness of statutory contributions such as PF and ESI (illustrative, confirm current rules).
    • Indian number style with lakh and crore.
    • Basic reading of tables and reports.

    What you need to have ready

    • A spreadsheet such as Excel or Google Sheets.
    • A calculator.
    • A sample supplier tracker or scorecard to practise on.

    Quick readiness check

    A supplier fills 45 of 50 roles. What is the fill rate?

    Show answer
    45 ÷ 50 = 90%.

    Pay rate is ₹200 and bill rate is ₹232. What is the markup?

    Show answer
    (232 − 200) ÷ 200 = 16%.

    Weights are 60 and 40 with scores 80 and 90. What is the weighted score?

    Show answer
    0.60 × 80 + 0.40 × 90 = 48 + 36 = 84.
    Module 2

    Secondary supplier performance tracking

    Measuring the smaller suppliers who deliver workers through the primary vendor, using the same yardstick every month.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    📋 What secondary supplier tracking is and why it matters

    In a managed staffing programme, the primary vendor (or prime) often does not supply every worker itself. It passes some roles to secondary suppliers, also called second-tier or sub-vendors: smaller agencies, local labor contractors and niche specialists. Secondary supplier performance tracking is the regular measurement of how well these suppliers deliver.

    Why it matters: the client holds the primary vendor responsible for everything, including work done by secondary suppliers. A weak secondary supplier harms fill rate, quality and compliance, and it can only be fixed if the problem is visible. Tracking gives the vendor management specialist the facts to reward good suppliers, coach average ones and replace poor ones.

    TermMeaning
    Primary vendor (prime)The main supplier under contract with the client
    Secondary supplierA sub-vendor supplying through the prime
    AllocationRoles given to a supplier to fill
    SubmissionA candidate sent for a role
    FillA role staffed by a worker who starts
    Scorecard periodThe time window measured, usually a month
    Supplier fill rate = Roles filled ÷ Roles allocated
    🧪
    Worked example: A secondary supplier is allocated 80 roles in a month and fills 60. Fill rate = 60 ÷ 80 = 75%. If the prime's target is 85%, this supplier is 10 points short.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Tracking only the prime vendor's total numbers
    • Measuring suppliers on different yardsticks
    • Looking at results only when there is a complaint

    ✍️ Practice

    A secondary supplier is allocated 50 roles and fills 42. What is the fill rate?

    Show answer
    42 ÷ 50 = 84%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of metrics and where the data comes from

    Metrics fall into five groups. Delivery covers fill rate and speed. Quality covers how many submitted candidates are accepted and how long placed workers stay. Compliance covers documents, statutory payments and audit results. Commercial covers rate adherence and invoice accuracy. Responsiveness covers how quickly the supplier replies and acts.

    Data should come from systems, not memory: the vendor management system for requisitions and submissions, attendance records for presence, client feedback for quality and audit reports for compliance. One source per metric keeps arguments about numbers short.

    GroupExample metricSource
    DeliveryFill rate, time to submitVMS
    QualityAcceptance rate, 30-day retentionVMS and attendance
    ComplianceAudit score, document validityAudit file
    CommercialRate adherence, invoice accuracyFinance
    ResponsivenessReply time to requestsTicket log
    Submissions per placement = Candidates submitted ÷ Placements made
    🧪
    Worked example: A supplier submits 150 candidates and 30 are placed. Submissions per placement = 150 ÷ 30 = 5. A lower number means the supplier sends better-matched candidates. A supplier needing 12 per placement wastes the client's interview time.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using several data sources for the same metric
    • Counting submissions without quality
    • Ignoring commercial metrics

    ✍️ Practice

    A supplier submits 120 candidates and 40 are placed. What is the submissions-per-placement ratio?

    Show answer
    120 ÷ 40 = 3 submissions per placement.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🔧 The tracking process, step by step

    Steps: (1) define each metric with its formula, source and period, and share it with suppliers; (2) collect data on a fixed date each month; (3) check for errors and clear disputed records; (4) calculate each metric and convert it to a score; (5) combine scores into a scorecard; (6) hold a short review call with each supplier; (7) agree actions with owners and dates; (8) track whether the actions worked next month.

    A key delivery metric is on-time submission: the share of requisitions on which the supplier sent a qualified candidate within the agreed time. It is a leading indicator, because slow submission today becomes unfilled roles next week.

    StepOutput
    Define metricsWritten definitions
    Collect dataMonthly data file
    Clean dataAgreed records
    ScoreSupplier scorecard
    Review callAgreed actions
    Follow upAction tracker
    On-time submission rate = Requisitions answered within the time limit ÷ Requisitions released
    🧪
    Worked example: A supplier is released 100 requisitions and sends a qualified candidate within 48 hours on 82 of them. On-time submission = 82 ÷ 100 = 82%. The supplier misses most on Fridays, which is raised in the review call.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Changing metric definitions without notice
    • Skipping the data clean-up
    • Holding a review with no agreed actions

    ✍️ Practice

    A supplier is released 90 requisitions and answers 72 in time. What is the on-time submission rate?

    Show answer
    72 ÷ 90 = 80%.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    📊 Building a weighted scorecard

    A weighted scorecard combines metrics into one score using weights that reflect what matters most to the client. Each metric is first converted to a 0 to 100 score. Multiply by its weight and add the results. Weights must total 100 percent.

    Use the total for ranking, and the individual scores for coaching. A supplier with a good total and one very poor metric still needs an action plan for that metric. Share the method with suppliers before using it.

    MetricWeightScoreContribution
    Delivery358028.0
    Quality259022.5
    Compliance2510025.0
    Commercial157010.5
    Total10086.0
    Scorecard total = Σ (Metric score × Weight ÷ 100)
    🧪
    Worked example: Using the table, 0.35 × 80 + 0.25 × 90 + 0.25 × 100 + 0.15 × 70 = 28 + 22.5 + 25 + 10.5 = 86.0. The supplier's weakest area is commercial, so the action plan focuses on rate adherence.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Weights that do not total 100
    • Looking at the total only
    • Changing weights during the year

    ✍️ Practice

    Weights are 40, 30 and 30, and scores are 75, 85 and 100. What is the scorecard total?

    Show answer
    0.40 × 75 + 0.30 × 85 + 0.30 × 100 = 30 + 25.5 + 30 = 85.5.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: small samples, trends and development

    Experts read scorecards with care. A supplier allocated only 5 roles can show 80 percent by filling 4, and one miss moves the result by 20 points. Small volumes give noisy results, so compare with a minimum volume or use a rolling average over several months.

    A rolling three-month average smooths noise and shows the real direction. Pair it with the latest month: if the latest is well above the average, the supplier may be improving, and if it is well below, trouble may be starting.

    Lead development, not only measurement. Share best practice from top suppliers, give struggling suppliers a clear plan with a 90-day review, and recognise improvement publicly. Watch for gaming, such as filling roles with unsuitable people to lift the fill rate.

    IssueApproach
    Small volumeMinimum volume or rolling average
    One-month spikesRolling three-month average
    GamingCheck quality alongside fill rate
    Weak supplier90-day improvement plan
    Rolling average = (Month 1 + Month 2 + Month 3) ÷ 3
    🧪
    Worked example: A supplier's monthly fill rates are 70%, 80% and 90%. The rolling average = (70 + 80 + 90) ÷ 3 = 80%. The latest month alone, 90%, looks strong, but the supplier is only now recovering, so the average is a safer basis for volume decisions.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Basing decisions on one month
    • Ignoring small-volume noise
    • Never recognising improvement

    ✍️ Practice

    A supplier's monthly fill rates are 60%, 66% and 72%. What is the rolling average?

    Show answer
    (60 + 66 + 72) ÷ 3 = 66%.
    Module 3

    SLA tiering

    Grouping suppliers into tiers with different service levels, access and volume, so effort and work go where performance is.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🏅 What SLA tiering is and why it matters

    SLA tiering groups suppliers into tiers, such as Gold, Silver and Bronze, based on performance. Each tier has its own service-level expectations, share of volume and level of management attention.

    Why it matters: treating every supplier the same wastes effort and rewards weak performance. Tiering pushes volume to suppliers that deliver, gives weaker suppliers a clear path to improve and lets the specialist spend time where it matters most.

    TermMeaning
    TierA performance group of suppliers
    Tier criteriaThe scores or conditions for each tier
    SLAService level expected of the supplier
    Volume sharePart of total roles given to a tier
    PromotionMoving up a tier
    DemotionMoving down a tier
    ProbationA trial period with extra monitoring
    Volume allocated = Total roles × Tier volume share
    🧪
    Worked example: In a month 1,000 roles are released. Gold suppliers receive 60%, Silver 25% and Bronze 15%. Gold gets 600 roles, Silver 250 and Bronze 150.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Keeping every supplier on equal volume
    • Tiers with no clear criteria
    • Never moving suppliers between tiers

    ✍️ Practice

    800 roles are released and Gold suppliers get 55%. How many roles do Gold suppliers receive?

    Show answer
    800 × 55% = 440 roles.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of tiering models

    Performance tiering ranks suppliers by score and is the most common. Category tiering groups them by the type of workers supplied, such as industrial, clerical or technical. Geographic tiering groups them by region. Strategic tiering labels suppliers as strategic, preferred or transactional, based on importance as well as performance.

    Many programmes combine models, for example performance tiers within each category. Keep the model simple enough that suppliers can understand it.

    ModelBased onBest for
    PerformanceScoreRewarding delivery
    CategoryWorker typeSpecialist skills
    GeographicRegionLocal supply
    StrategicImportance and performanceLong-term partners
    Tier distribution = Suppliers in the tier ÷ Total suppliers
    🧪
    Worked example: A programme has 20 suppliers: 4 Gold, 9 Silver and 7 Bronze. Gold share = 4 ÷ 20 = 20%, Silver = 45% and Bronze = 35%. A healthy pyramid has a small top and a manageable bottom.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Too many tiers
    • Mixing very different worker types in one tier
    • Keeping all suppliers in the middle tier

    ✍️ Practice

    A programme has 25 suppliers and 10 are Silver. What share is Silver?

    Show answer
    10 ÷ 25 = 40%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🔧 Setting up the tiers, step by step

    Steps: (1) choose the score that decides tiers, usually the scorecard total; (2) set cut-offs, for example Gold at 88 and above, Silver 75 to 87 and Bronze below 75; (3) set SLAs for each tier; (4) agree volume shares; (5) assign each supplier; (6) tell suppliers where they stand and why; (7) review at fixed times; (8) apply promotion and demotion rules.

    Higher tiers usually carry tougher SLAs in return for more volume. A Gold supplier might be expected to fill 95 percent and submit within 24 hours, while a Bronze supplier might be held to 85 percent and 72 hours.

    TierScoreFill SLASubmit within
    Gold88+95%24 hours
    Silver75 to 8790%48 hours
    BronzeBelow 7585%72 hours
    Expected fills = Roles allocated × Tier fill SLA
    🧪
    Worked example: Gold suppliers are allocated 600 roles with a 95% SLA, so expected fills = 600 × 0.95 = 570. A supplier scoring 91 is Gold, one scoring 80 is Silver and one scoring 72 is Bronze.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Setting the same SLA for all tiers
    • Not explaining the tier to the supplier
    • Changing cut-offs without notice

    ✍️ Practice

    Silver suppliers are allocated 250 roles with a 90% SLA. What are the expected fills?

    Show answer
    250 × 0.90 = 225.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    💰 The economics of tiering

    Tiering concentrates volume, and volume buys better prices. Higher-tier suppliers are often willing to accept a lower markup in return for steady work. Moving labor cost from a high-markup supplier to a lower-markup one directly saves money.

    Always check that the saving does not cost quality. The tier score includes quality, which helps. Also check capacity: a Gold supplier cannot take unlimited volume.

    TierTypical markup (illustrative)
    Gold14%
    Silver16%
    Bronze18%
    Saving = Base labor cost moved × (Old markup − New markup)
    🧪
    Worked example: ₹1 crore of base labor cost moves from a Bronze supplier at 18% markup to a Gold supplier at 14%. Saving = 1 crore × 4% = ₹4,00,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Moving volume without checking capacity
    • Chasing price over quality
    • Not recording the saving

    ✍️ Practice

    ₹80 lakh of base labor cost moves from a 17% markup supplier to a 14% markup supplier. What is the saving?

    Show answer
    80 lakh × 3% = ₹2,40,000.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: promotion rules, concentration and fairness

    Rules for promotion and demotion should prevent flip-flopping. A common approach is to require two consecutive periods above or below a cut-off before moving a supplier, and to give a demoted supplier a recovery window before volume is cut.

    Watch concentration risk. If one Gold supplier takes too large a share, a problem on its side can stop the programme. Set a cap on the share any single supplier can hold and keep a back-up supplier qualified.

    Fairness matters too. Small suppliers with low volume can look weak because of noise, so apply minimum-volume rules, and make exceptions for strategic reasons visible and documented.

    RiskControl
    Flip-floppingTwo-period rule
    ConcentrationCap on supplier share
    Small-supplier noiseMinimum volume
    FavouritismDocumented exceptions
    Supplier share = Supplier volume ÷ Total volume
    🧪
    Worked example: A Gold supplier fills 380 of 1,000 roles, a 38% share. The cap is 35%, so 30 roles must move to other suppliers. The specialist moves the next 30 roles to the second Gold supplier.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • No cap on any one supplier
    • Demoting after one bad month
    • Hiding exceptions

    ✍️ Practice

    A supplier fills 420 of 1,200 roles and the cap is 30%. How many roles are over the cap?

    Show answer
    Share = 35%. 30% of 1,200 = 360. Excess = 420 − 360 = 60 roles.
    Module 4

    Supplier audit compliance

    Checking, with evidence, that suppliers follow the law and the contract, and making sure problems are fixed.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🔎 What supplier audit compliance is and why it matters

    A supplier audit is a structured check of whether a supplier is meeting its legal and contractual duties: licences, wages, statutory contributions, working conditions, records and safety. Audit compliance is the programme that plans audits, records findings and makes sure corrections happen.

    Why it matters: the client and the primary vendor can be held responsible when a supplier breaks labour rules. An audit finds problems before an inspector, a worker complaint or a news story does. It also shows clients that the vendor controls its supply chain.

    TermMeaning
    Audit scopeWhat the audit covers
    EvidenceRecords and observations that support a finding
    FindingA recorded gap against a requirement
    Major non-conformanceA serious gap with legal or safety risk
    Minor non-conformanceA smaller gap with low risk
    ObservationA comment that is not yet a breach
    Corrective actionA fix with an owner and a date
    Audit compliance score = Items compliant ÷ Items checked
    🧪
    Worked example: An audit checks 60 items and 51 are compliant. Score = 51 ÷ 60 = 85%. The 9 gaps are classified, assigned and dated.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Auditing without evidence
    • Treating every gap as equally serious
    • No follow-up on corrective actions

    ✍️ Practice

    An audit checks 80 items and 68 are compliant. What is the score?

    Show answer
    68 ÷ 80 = 85%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of supplier audits

    A desk audit reviews documents sent by the supplier. A site audit visits the supplier's office or the work site. A worker-interview audit speaks privately to workers about pay, hours and treatment. A payment audit traces wages and statutory contributions from payroll to proof of payment. Audits can be scheduled or unannounced.

    A mix works best. Documents can be falsified, so site checks and interviews are needed to confirm what is on paper. Coverage should also be tracked: the share of suppliers audited within the year.

    TypeWhat it checksStrength
    Desk auditDocumentsFast, wide
    Site auditPremises and recordsDirect view
    Worker interviewPay, hours, treatmentFinds hidden issues
    Payment auditWages and contributionsHard evidence of payment
    Unannounced auditReal conditionsHard to prepare for
    Audit coverage = Suppliers audited ÷ Total suppliers
    🧪
    Worked example: A programme has 20 suppliers and 15 were audited this year. Coverage = 15 ÷ 20 = 75%. The five not audited are the smallest, so risk-based planning decides whether they come first next year.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Relying only on documents
    • Announcing every audit in advance
    • Auditing the same suppliers every year

    ✍️ Practice

    A programme has 30 suppliers and 24 were audited. What is the coverage?

    Show answer
    24 ÷ 30 = 80%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🔧 Running an audit, step by step

    Steps: (1) plan the audit and set its scope using a risk view; (2) send the document list; (3) review documents before visiting; (4) visit the site and walk through the process; (5) interview workers privately; (6) sample payroll and payment proof; (7) classify findings; (8) hold a closing meeting and share the draft findings; (9) agree corrective actions with dates; (10) follow up and verify closure.

    Sampling needs care. Choose files at random and also pick files that look unusual. The error rate in the sample is a quick measure of how clean the records are.

    StepOutput
    PlanScope and risk view
    Document reviewPre-visit notes
    Site visit and interviewsObservations
    Payroll samplingError rate
    Classify findingsFindings list
    Closing meetingAgreed actions
    Follow-upVerified closure
    Error rate = Files with errors ÷ Files sampled
    🧪
    Worked example: The auditor samples 30 worker files and finds problems in 6, such as missing wage slips or incomplete PF records. Error rate = 6 ÷ 30 = 20%. The supplier must fix all 6 and explain why they occurred.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Sampling only clean-looking files
    • Skipping worker interviews
    • Not verifying that fixes were made

    ✍️ Practice

    The auditor samples 25 files and finds errors in 4. What is the error rate?

    Show answer
    4 ÷ 25 = 16%.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    📊 Classifying and scoring findings

    Not all findings are equal. A serious breach, such as unpaid wages or unsafe conditions, matters far more than a missing signature. Classifying findings as major, minor or observation shows what to fix first.

    A simple scoring method starts at 100 and deducts points: 5 for each major and 1 for each minor. A rating then follows: 90 and above passes, 75 to 89 is conditional with a corrective plan and below 75 fails and may trigger suspension of new volume. Agree the rules in the supplier contract.

    ResultScoreConsequence
    Pass90 and aboveContinue as normal
    Conditional75 to 89Corrective plan with dates
    FailBelow 75Hold new volume and re-audit
    Audit score = 100 − (Major findings × 5 + Minor findings × 1)
    🧪
    Worked example: An audit records 2 major and 6 minor findings. Score = 100 − (2 × 5 + 6 × 1) = 100 − 16 = 84. This is a conditional rating, so the supplier must fix the findings by agreed dates and is re-audited.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating all findings alike
    • Scoring rules not in the contract
    • Not re-auditing a conditional supplier

    ✍️ Practice

    An audit records 1 major and 9 minor findings. What is the score and rating?

    Show answer
    100 − (5 + 9) = 86, which is conditional.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: risk-based planning, repeats and consequences

    Experts do not audit everyone equally. A risk score multiplies the likelihood of a problem by its impact, each on a 1 to 5 scale. Suppliers with high scores, such as those with many workers, a poor history or complaints, are audited first and more often.

    Watch repeat findings. A finding that returns after being closed means the fix was cosmetic. Repeat findings should escalate: a senior review, then volume holds, and ultimately removal from the programme.

    Keep the audit independent and fair. Auditors should not be paid by the supplier, and the same rules should apply to every supplier. Know the legal angle: in many cases the principal employer can be held responsible for unpaid wages and dues, so payment audits matter.

    Risk factorIncreases score when
    LikelihoodPoor history, many workers, complaints
    ImpactLarge headcount, safety-critical site
    Repeat findingsSame finding returns
    EscalationVolume hold then removal
    Risk score = Likelihood (1 to 5) × Impact (1 to 5)
    🧪
    Worked example: Supplier A has likelihood 4 and impact 5, so its risk score is 20. Supplier B has likelihood 2 and impact 3, giving 6. Supplier A is audited first and without notice.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Auditing all suppliers equally
    • Closing repeat findings without root cause
    • Allowing audits to be paid for by the supplier

    ✍️ Practice

    Supplier C has likelihood 3 and impact 4. Supplier D has likelihood 5 and impact 2. Which is audited first?

    Show answer
    C = 12 and D = 10. C is audited first.
    Module 5

    Markup rate negotiations

    Agreeing the supplier's margin on top of worker cost, fairly, with facts, and without pushing the supplier into non-compliance.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    💬 What markup is and why it matters

    Markup is the amount a supplier adds to the cost of a worker to arrive at the bill rate. It covers statutory costs the supplier must pay, its own overhead and its margin. Markup is usually shown as a percentage of the pay rate.

    Why it matters: markup is often the biggest negotiable part of the price. A small change in percentage becomes a large amount over a year. But a markup set too low can push a supplier to cut corners on wages or contributions, which becomes a compliance risk for the client and the vendor.

    TermMeaning
    Pay rateWhat the worker earns per hour or month
    Statutory costEmployer contributions and levies required by law
    OverheadSupplier's running cost
    MarginSupplier's profit
    Markup %(Bill rate − Pay rate) ÷ Pay rate
    Bill rateThe rate charged to the client
    Rate cardAgreed pay and bill rates by role
    Markup % = (Bill rate − Pay rate) ÷ Pay rate
    🧪
    Worked example: A worker's pay rate is ₹300 and the bill rate is ₹354. Markup = (354 − 300) ÷ 300 = 18%.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Confusing markup with margin
    • Negotiating a percentage without seeing the cost build-up
    • Looking only at the percentage and not the rupee effect

    ✍️ Practice

    The pay rate is ₹250 and the bill rate is ₹290. What is the markup?

    Show answer
    (290 − 250) ÷ 250 = 16%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of markup structures and what is inside a markup

    A flat percentage is the simplest. Tiered percentages fall as volume grows. A fixed fee per head ignores the pay rate. Cost-plus with an open cost breakdown shows statutory cost, overhead and margin separately. A capped markup sets a ceiling regardless of pay changes.

    Inside any markup there are three parts: statutory cost, overhead and margin. Knowing the parts is the key to a fair negotiation, because statutory cost is not negotiable while overhead and margin are.

    StructureHow it worksWatch-out
    Flat %One percentage for allMay hide cost differences
    Tiered %Falls with volumeNeeds volume tracking
    Fixed fee per headSame amount per workerWeak link to cost
    Cost-plus, open bookShows each partMore work to audit
    CappedCeiling on markupCheck statutory changes
    Markup % = Statutory % + Overhead % + Margin %
    🧪
    Worked example: An 18% markup breaks down into statutory cost 13%, overhead 3% and margin 2% (illustrative). On a ₹300 pay rate that is ₹39 for statutory cost, ₹9 for overhead and ₹6 for margin, a total of ₹54 and a bill rate of ₹354.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating statutory cost as negotiable
    • Not asking for the breakdown
    • Mixing up the parts

    ✍️ Practice

    Statutory cost is 12.5%, overhead 2.5% and margin 2%. What is the markup?

    Show answer
    12.5 + 2.5 + 2 = 17%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🔧 The negotiation process, step by step

    Steps: (1) prepare: gather market benchmarks, the supplier's cost build-up, volume history and performance scores; (2) set three numbers: the ideal, the target and the walk-away; (3) open with facts, not demands; (4) trade: offer volume, longer terms or faster payment in return for rate; (5) agree in writing with the rate card; (6) review at fixed intervals.

    Always convert percentages into annual rupees. A reduction of two points on a large base is a large number, and it makes the trade-offs clear to the people who approve the deal.

    StepOutput
    PrepareBenchmarks and cost build-up
    Set rangeIdeal, target, walk-away
    OpenFact-based position
    TradeVolume or terms for rate
    AgreeSigned rate card
    ReviewScheduled rate review
    Annual saving = Annual base labor cost × (Old markup − New markup)
    🧪
    Worked example: Annual base labor cost with a supplier is ₹5 crore. Negotiating markup from 18% to 16% saves 2 points. Annual saving = 5 crore × 2% = ₹10,00,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Negotiating without data
    • Giving volume without getting rate
    • Verbal agreements

    ✍️ Practice

    Annual base labor cost is ₹3 crore and markup falls from 17% to 15%. What is the annual saving?

    Show answer
    3 crore × 2% = ₹6,00,000.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    🧮 Checking the cost build-up and the supplier's margin

    Before agreeing a markup, test it. Compute the statutory cost from the worker's wage, using current rates. Subtract it from the markup to see what is left for overhead and margin. If the rest is large compared with similar suppliers, there is room to negotiate. If it is tiny, the supplier may be unable to comply.

    A useful check is the supplier's gross margin as a share of the bill rate: bill rate less pay rate less statutory cost, divided by bill rate.

    ItemAmount per hour
    Bill rate₹354.00
    Pay rate₹300.00
    Statutory cost (13.25%)₹39.75
    Left for overhead and margin₹14.25
    Gross margin % = (Bill rate − Pay rate − Statutory cost) ÷ Bill rate
    🧪
    Worked example: Bill rate ₹354, pay rate ₹300 and statutory cost of 13.25% of pay, which is ₹39.75. Left = 354 − 300 − 39.75 = ₹14.25. As a share of the bill rate, that is 14.25 ÷ 354 = 4.0%. Overhead must still come out of this ₹14.25.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Using old statutory rates
    • Ignoring that overhead comes out of the remainder
    • Squeezing the supplier to near zero

    ✍️ Practice

    Pay ₹250, bill ₹295 and statutory cost 13% of pay. What is the gross margin as a share of the bill rate?

    Show answer
    Statutory = ₹32.50. Left = 295 − 250 − 32.5 = ₹12.50. 12.50 ÷ 295 = 4.2%.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: leverage, indexing and the break-even floor

    Strong negotiators know their alternatives. If other suppliers can deliver at a lower markup with similar quality, you have leverage. If the supplier is hard to replace, leverage is weak. Prepare your best alternative before the meeting.

    Index prices to wages. If the legal minimum wage rises, the pay rate rises, and the bill rate must follow. Agree the pass-through formula in advance so changes do not trigger arguments. Volume tiers can reward growth with a lower markup.

    Know the floor. The break-even markup is the statutory percentage plus overhead. A markup below it means the supplier loses money on every worker and is tempted to cut wages, delay contributions or reduce safety. A fair deal keeps the supplier above the floor.

    LeverUse
    AlternativesStrengthen your position
    Volume tiersReward growth
    Wage indexingPass-through of legal changes
    Break-even floorAvoid compliance risk
    Break-even markup = Statutory % + Overhead %
    🧪
    Worked example: Statutory cost is 13.25% and overhead is 3%, so the break-even markup is 16.25%. If the buyer pushes the markup to 15%, the supplier loses 1.25 points on every worker. The specialist stops at a level above 16.25%.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Pushing the markup below break-even
    • No pass-through formula for wage changes
    • Negotiating without an alternative

    ✍️ Practice

    Statutory cost is 12.5% and overhead is 2.5%. The buyer proposes 14%. By how many points is it below break-even?

    Show answer
    Break-even = 15%. 15 − 14 = 1 point below.

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Secondary supplier performance tracking

    Why track secondary suppliers when the client contracts only with the prime?

    Show model answer
    The client holds the prime responsible for all workers, including those from secondary suppliers. A weak sub-vendor hurts fill rate, quality and compliance. Tracking makes the problem visible so the prime can coach or replace that supplier.

    Why is a high fill rate alone not a good sign?

    Show model answer
    A supplier can raise fill rate by sending unsuitable people who leave early. Fill rate must be read with acceptance rate, submissions per placement and retention. That is how gaming is spotted.

    A supplier says its low score is unfair. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Share the written definitions and the raw records. Trace a sample of disputed records together, correct any real errors and re-issue the score. If the data is right, move to an action plan.

    A supplier with 5 roles shows 80% fill rate and a large supplier shows 78%. Who is better?

    Show model answer
    Not clear yet. The small supplier filled 4 of 5 and one miss would move it 20 points. I would apply a minimum volume rule or compare rolling averages over several months before ranking them.

    A supplier is allocated 120 roles and fills 96. The target is 85%. What is the gap?

    Show model answer
    Fill rate = 96 ÷ 120 = 80%. The gap is 85 − 80 = 5 points. To reach 85% it needed 0.85 × 120 = 102 fills, so it is 6 fills short.

    💬 Interview Questions: SLA tiering

    Why have tiers instead of treating all suppliers equally?

    Show model answer
    Equal treatment rewards weak performance and wastes management time. Tiers send volume to suppliers who deliver and give weaker ones a clear path up. Effort goes where it matters most.

    Why use a two-period rule for promotion and demotion?

    Show model answer
    One month can be noise or luck. Two consecutive periods show a real trend. It stops flip-flopping and is fairer to suppliers.

    A Gold supplier now holds 45% of volume and has one bad week. What is the risk and your action?

    Show model answer
    Concentration risk: a problem on its side can stop the programme. I would apply the cap, move the excess to another qualified supplier and keep a back-up ready.

    A preferred supplier scores 74 but a manager wants it in Silver. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    Apply the published rule, which says Bronze. If there is a real strategic reason, record it as a documented exception with an approver and a review date. Hidden exceptions destroy trust in the tiers.

    Silver gets 360 roles at a 90% SLA and fills 310. What is attainment?

    Show model answer
    Expected fills = 360 × 0.90 = 324. Attainment = 310 ÷ 324 = 95.7%. Silver is 14 fills short, so I would ask for a recovery plan.

    💬 Interview Questions: Supplier audit compliance

    Why does the client care about a supplier's compliance?

    Show model answer
    In many cases the principal employer can be held responsible for unpaid wages and dues (illustrative, confirm current rules). Audits find problems before an inspector or worker complaint does and show control of the supply chain.

    Why combine desk, site and interview audits?

    Show model answer
    Documents can be falsified. Site visits confirm records and conditions, and private interviews find hidden issues on pay, hours and treatment. Payment traces give hard proof.

    A supplier fixes a major finding but it returns in the next audit. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    I treat it as a repeat finding. I ask for root cause, escalate to senior review and, if it continues, hold new volume. Repeats mean the earlier fix was cosmetic.

    You have time to audit only five of twelve suppliers. How do you choose?

    Show model answer
    I use a risk score of likelihood times impact, each 1 to 5. Suppliers with large headcount, poor history or complaints come first. I also keep a few random picks so no supplier feels immune.

    An audit records 2 major and 9 minor findings. What is the score and rating?

    Show model answer
    Score = 100 − (2 × 5 + 9 × 1) = 100 − 19 = 81. That is conditional, so the supplier needs a corrective plan with dates and a re-audit.

    💬 Interview Questions: Markup rate negotiations

    What are the three parts of a markup, and which are negotiable?

    Show model answer
    Statutory cost, overhead and margin. Statutory cost is set by law, so it is not negotiable. Overhead and margin can be discussed. Knowing the split keeps the talk fair and fact based.

    Why is pushing markup as low as possible a bad strategy?

    Show model answer
    Below the break-even floor the supplier loses money on every worker. It may then cut wages, delay contributions or reduce safety, which becomes a compliance risk for the client and the vendor.

    The supplier will not move on rate. What else can you trade?

    Show model answer
    Longer contract terms, higher volume, faster payment or tier status. I ask for rate in return for each of these, and I write the trade into the rate card.

    Minimum wage is raised and the supplier asks for a new rate. How do you handle it?

    Show model answer
    I use the pass-through formula in the contract. Pay rate rises, statutory cost is recalculated and the bill rate follows. If there is no clause, I add one so the next change is not an argument.

    Annual base cost is ₹2.5 crore. Markup falls from 16% to 14.5%. What is the saving?

    Show model answer
    Points saved = 16 − 14.5 = 1.5. Saving = ₹2,50,00,000 × 1.5% = ₹3,75,000 a year.

    • Never push markup below the break-even floor.
    • Present every offer in annual rupees and agree a wage pass-through.
