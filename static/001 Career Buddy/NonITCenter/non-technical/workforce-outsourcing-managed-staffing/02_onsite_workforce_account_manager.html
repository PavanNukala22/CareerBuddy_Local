    Course Overview

    Onsite Workforce Account Manager: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches the daily work of an Onsite Workforce Account Manager: the person who stands between an outsourced workforce and the client's plant, warehouse or service floor. You learn to see problems early, keep the right number of people on every shift, fill shifts reliably and keep the client's expectations in line with what the contract promises.

    The role matters because the client judges the staffing company by what happens on the floor. Late starts, short shifts and missed targets become complaints, SLA credits and lost renewals. A manager who is present, numerate and organised protects output, worker safety and the contract.

    The course has four skills: direct floor oversight, daily headcount maintenance, shift-fill rate optimization and client operational alignment. Each skill has levels from basics to expert, with tools, testing, practice, a project, troubleshooting and interview questions. A summary and assessment close the course.

    Who this course is for

    • Supervisors and team leaders moving into account management roles.
    • Freshers joining a staffing or facility services company as account executives.
    • Operations coordinators who handle rosters, attendance and client reports.
    • Managers of outsourced teams who want a cleaner approach to fill rate and client reviews.

    How the course is organised

    • Overview and prerequisites explain who the course is for and what you should know.
    • Core lessons cover each skill from Level 1 to Level 4 with formulas and worked examples.
    • Tools and workflow show the documents, trackers and daily steps for each skill.
    • Testing and quality describe how to check your own work with a KPI formula.
    • Practice and project give do and avoid lists, a project with data, and a full solution.
    • Troubleshooting, interview questions, summary and assessment close the course.
    ⏱️
    Time needed: About 10 to 12 hours

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Calculate span of control, productivity attainment and issue closure rate for a site.
    2. Plan and run a floor walk using a checklist, an issue log and an escalation path.
    3. Apply the 5 Whys method to a repeat issue and name the process fix.
    4. Calculate attendance, absenteeism and the remaining gap after backfill.
    5. Determine the roster size needed for a required headcount using the buffer formula.
    6. Calculate shift-fill rate, confirmation rate and the effect of a standby pool.
    7. Compare the cost of a fill lever with the SLA credit it avoids.
    8. Build a scorecard with target, actual, status and cause notes for a client review.
    9. Explain how to handle a change request and calculate its cost.
    10. Design an escalation approach for conflicting client instructions.

    Module 2

    Direct floor oversight

    Being visibly on the client's work floor so problems are caught early and outsourced workers stay productive and safe.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    👀 What floor oversight is and why it matters

    Direct floor oversight means the account manager is physically present where the work happens: walking the lines, watching the process, talking to workers and supervisors, and fixing issues before they reach the client's management. It is the opposite of managing from a desk and reading reports the next day.

    Why it matters: most problems on a staffing contract show up first on the floor, such as late starts, missing safety equipment, confusion about tasks or friction with the client's supervisors. A manager who is present sees these within minutes. One who is not finds out when the client complains.

    Oversight is support, not policing. The goal is to keep output, quality and safety on track and to make workers feel that someone is looking after them.

    TermMeaning
    Floor walkA planned round of the work area to observe and talk
    Span of controlNumber of workers per supervisor
    SupervisorThe person who directs workers day to day
    IncidentAn event that disrupts work, safety or quality
    Near missAn event that could have caused harm but did not
    SOPStandard operating procedure for a task
    EscalationPassing an issue to a higher level when it cannot be solved locally
    Span of control = Workers ÷ Supervisors
    🧪
    Worked example: A site has 120 outsourced workers and 6 supervisors. Span of control = 120 ÷ 6 = 20 workers per supervisor. If the contract expects no more than 25 per supervisor, the site is within limit.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Managing from the office and relying on next-day reports
    • Treating oversight as inspection instead of support
    • Visiting only when something has gone wrong

    ✍️ Practice

    A site has 90 workers and 5 supervisors. What is the span of control?

    Show answer
    90 ÷ 5 = 18 workers per supervisor.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of oversight activities

    Floor oversight is made of several different activities, and each has its own purpose. Scheduled floor walks give regular coverage. Spot checks are unannounced and test whether standards hold when nobody expects a visit. Shift handover checks make sure information passes cleanly between shifts. Incident response is the reactive side: being there when something goes wrong. Quality and safety observations focus on specific standards.

    A good account manager mixes these on purpose. Relying only on scheduled walks lets people prepare. Relying only on incident response means always being late.

    ActivityPurposeTypical frequency
    Scheduled floor walkRegular visibility and issue spottingEvery shift
    Spot checkTest standards without warningSeveral per week
    Shift handover checkSmooth information transferDaily
    Incident responseContain and fix problemsAs needed
    Safety observationCheck PPE and safe practiceDaily
    Floor-walk coverage = Walks completed ÷ Walks planned
    🧪
    Worked example: The plan is 24 floor walks a week and 21 were completed. Coverage = 21 ÷ 24 = 87.5%. The 3 missed walks all fell on the night shift, so night supervision needs attention.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Doing only day-shift walks
    • Following the same route at the same time every day
    • Counting a walk as done when no issue was recorded

    ✍️ Practice

    The plan is 30 walks and 27 were completed. What is the coverage?

    Show answer
    27 ÷ 30 = 90%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🚶 Running a floor walk, step by step

    A floor walk is more useful when it follows a routine. Steps: (1) prepare by checking today's roster, open issues and any client requests; (2) walk the area in a planned route and vary the route over time; (3) observe attendance, safety equipment, pace, quality and behaviour; (4) talk to workers and supervisors and ask open questions; (5) record every issue in the issue log with owner and due time; (6) close the loop by telling people what was done.

    Tools and documents: a floor-walk checklist, the issue log, the SOPs for the tasks being done and the escalation contact list. The issue log is the most important tool because it turns observations into tracked actions.

    StepWhat to doRecord
    PrepareReview roster and open issuesNotes
    ObserveSafety, pace, quality, behaviourChecklist
    TalkAsk workers what is blocking themIssue log
    ActFix on the spot or assign ownerOwner and due time
    CloseTell people the outcomeClosure date
    Issue closure rate = Issues closed on time ÷ Issues logged
    🧪
    Worked example: During a month, 40 issues were logged and 34 were closed within the agreed time. Closure rate = 34 ÷ 40 = 85%. The 6 late ones were all waiting on client maintenance, which is raised in the weekly review.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Noticing a problem but not logging it
    • Logging issues without an owner or due time
    • Not telling workers what happened to their feedback

    ✍️ Practice

    In a month, 25 issues were logged and 20 were closed on time. What is the closure rate?

    Show answer
    20 ÷ 25 = 80%.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    📈 Scenarios, productivity and KPIs

    Floor presence should show up in the numbers. Three groups of KPIs are common: productivity (output per labor hour), quality (defects or rework) and safety (incidents and near misses). Reading them together shows the real picture. High output with rising defects, for example, means workers are rushing.

    A typical scenario: output is below target on one line. A walk reveals that workers wait 20 minutes each shift for material. The fix is a material hand-off agreement with the client, not pressure on workers. Numbers tell you where to look. The floor tells you why.

    KPIFormulaReading it
    ProductivityOutput ÷ labor hoursUnits per hour
    Productivity attainmentActual ÷ target productivityHow close to goal
    Defect rateDefects ÷ units producedQuality level
    Incident rateIncidents ÷ workersSafety trend
    Productivity = Output ÷ Labor hours | Attainment = Actual ÷ Target
    🧪
    Worked example: A line produced 4,800 units using 600 labor hours: 4,800 ÷ 600 = 8 units per hour. The target is 9, so attainment = 8 ÷ 9 = 88.9%. A floor walk shows a 20-minute wait for material each shift.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Pushing workers harder when the cause is waiting time
    • Looking at productivity without looking at quality
    • Not comparing the same shift and line

    ✍️ Practice

    A line made 5,400 units in 600 labor hours. The target is 10 units per hour. What is attainment?

    Show answer
    5,400 ÷ 600 = 9 units per hour. 9 ÷ 10 = 90%.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🧠 Expert: coaching, root cause and boundaries

    At expert level the manager coaches supervisors and workers, finds root causes and stays inside role boundaries. The 5 Whys method asks why repeatedly until the real cause appears. A line is slow because workers wait; they wait because material arrives late; it arrives late because the order is released late; the order is late because of a shift handover gap. The fix lives at the handover.

    Boundaries matter. On the client's site, the client's supervisors usually direct day-to-day work, while the account manager manages the staffing company's people and the contract. Correcting workers in front of client staff or contradicting a client supervisor causes friction. Raise it privately and agree the fix.

    Track repeat issues. A repeat means the previous fix did not address the cause.

    SituationBetter response
    Same problem keeps coming backRun 5 Whys and fix the cause
    Client supervisor gives conflicting instructionsAgree one instruction privately, then brief workers
    Worker repeatedly lateCheck transport and shift timing before disciplining
    Safety breachStop work, fix, record, report
    Repeat issue rate = Repeat issues ÷ Total issues
    🧪
    Worked example: Out of 40 issues logged in a month, 10 were repeats. Repeat rate = 10 ÷ 40 = 25%. The target is 10% or less, so the manager runs a 5 Whys review on the top three repeating issues.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Fixing symptoms and not causes
    • Correcting workers publicly in front of the client
    • Ignoring repeat issues because each was closed

    ✍️ Practice

    Out of 50 issues, 6 were repeats. What is the repeat rate, and is it within a 10% limit?

    Show answer
    6 ÷ 50 = 12%. It is above the 10% limit, so a root-cause review is needed.
    Module 3

    Daily headcount maintenance

    Knowing exactly who is present, who is missing and who will replace them before the shift needs them.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🔢 What daily headcount maintenance is and why it matters

    Headcount maintenance is the daily job of making sure the number of workers present matches the number the client needs for each shift. It involves comparing the roster with actual attendance, finding gaps and replacing missing workers quickly.

    Why it matters: the client plans output around headcount. Every missing worker can mean lost production, overtime for others or a missed service level. Doing this every day, at the start of every shift, keeps small gaps from becoming big failures.

    TermMeaning
    RosterThe planned list of workers for a shift
    Required headcountWorkers the client needs on the shift
    PresentWorkers who actually arrived
    AbsenteeA rostered worker who did not attend
    BackfillA replacement for an absent worker
    StandbyTrained workers kept ready to replace absentees
    No-showAbsent without notice
    Attendance % = Present ÷ Rostered × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: 200 workers were rostered and 184 came. Attendance = 184 ÷ 200 = 92%. That leaves 16 absent workers to backfill.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Checking headcount only at the end of the shift
    • Relying on supervisors' verbal counts without matching a record
    • Not telling the client about a gap until output suffers

    ✍️ Practice

    150 workers were rostered and 141 were present. What is the attendance percentage?

    Show answer
    141 ÷ 150 = 94%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of absence and headcount categories

    Absence comes in different forms and each needs a different response. Planned absence, such as approved leave, can be covered in advance. Unplanned absence, such as sickness or no-shows, needs a fast backfill. Late arrivals and early exits reduce productive hours even if the worker is counted present.

    Headcount also has categories. Rostered is who was planned. Present is who arrived. Productive is who is actually working and fully trained. A new trainee may be present but not yet fully productive, so counting them as full capacity gives a false picture.

    TypeExampleResponse
    Planned absenceApproved leavePlan cover in the roster
    Unplanned absenceSickness, no-showCall standby immediately
    Late arrivalTransport delayTrack pattern, fix cause
    Early exitLeft before shift endRecord and review
    AttritionWorker resignedStart replacement hiring
    Absenteeism rate = Absent ÷ Rostered
    🧪
    Worked example: 200 workers were rostered and 16 were absent. Absenteeism = 16 ÷ 200 = 8%. If 5 of the 16 were approved leave, the unplanned rate is 11 ÷ 200 = 5.5%.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Treating all absence the same
    • Counting trainees as full capacity
    • Not separating planned and unplanned absence

    ✍️ Practice

    120 workers were rostered and 9 were absent. What is the absenteeism rate?

    Show answer
    9 ÷ 120 = 7.5%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🌅 The daily headcount routine, step by step

    A reliable routine is what makes headcount dependable. Steps: (1) the evening before, confirm tomorrow's roster with workers; (2) at shift start, run a roll call or biometric check; (3) match actual attendance with the roster; (4) calculate the gap against the required headcount; (5) call standby workers or move floaters to cover the gap; (6) report the final headcount and any remaining gap to the client; (7) at shift end, reconcile the day's attendance in the tracker.

    Speed matters. The first 30 minutes of the shift decide whether the gap is covered before it affects output. Many managers set a cut-off time, such as 30 minutes after shift start, by which backfill must be on the floor.

    TimeActionOutput
    Evening beforeConfirm rosterConfirmed list
    Shift startRoll call and attendance matchPresent count
    First 30 minBackfill from standby or floatersGap closed or reported
    Mid-shiftUpdate clientHeadcount message
    Shift endReconcile trackerDaily report
    Gap = Required − Present | Remaining gap = Gap − Backfill
    🧪
    Worked example: A shift requires 200 workers and 184 are present, so the gap is 16. The standby pool supplies 10, leaving 6. Three floaters move from another line, leaving 3. The manager reports a remaining gap of 3 to the client with a plan.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Waiting for the supervisor to raise the gap
    • Moving floaters without telling the client
    • Not recording the final headcount

    ✍️ Practice

    A shift requires 120 workers and 108 are present. Standby supplies 7. What gap remains?

    Show answer
    Gap = 120 − 108 = 12. After standby, 12 − 7 = 5 remain.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    🧮 Backfill planning and buffer maths

    You cannot fill every gap on the day, so good managers roster extra workers in advance. This is a buffer. The size of the buffer depends on the typical absenteeism rate. If 8% of rostered workers are usually absent, rostering exactly the required number will always leave you short.

    The formula is: Workers to roster = Required productive headcount ÷ (1 − absenteeism rate), rounded up. This is better than adding a flat percentage, because it makes sure the workers who actually turn up equal the requirement.

    Update the absenteeism rate from your own data each month. It changes with season, pay cycle and local events.

    RequiredAbsenteeismRoster needed
    2008%218
    1506%160
    30010%334
    Workers to roster = Required ÷ (1 − Absenteeism rate), rounded up
    🧪
    Worked example: The client needs 200 productive workers and absenteeism is 8%. 200 ÷ 0.92 = 217.4, so roster 218. With 8% absent (about 17), around 200 will be present.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Adding a flat 10% without using real data
    • Not rounding up
    • Using last year's absenteeism rate without updating it

    ✍️ Practice

    The client needs 150 productive workers and absenteeism is 6%. How many should be rostered?

    Show answer
    150 ÷ 0.94 = 159.6, so roster 160.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🛡️ Expert: forecasting, compliance and the cost of getting it wrong

    At expert level the manager forecasts absence instead of reacting. Absence is not random: it rises the day after a holiday, at month-end and pay day, during bad weather and around local festivals. A calendar of these events lets you roster extra workers on risky days.

    Compliance limits the fixes. Maximum daily and weekly hours, overtime limits and rest periods cannot be broken just to cover a gap. Asking the same workers to stay longer is not a free solution.

    Understaffing and overstaffing both cost money. Understaffing risks lost output and SLA credits. Overstaffing means paying for idle hours. The aim is the right buffer, not the largest one. Also guard data integrity: a buddy punch makes the headcount look right while the floor is short.

    RiskControl
    Predictable spikes in absenceEvent calendar and extra roster
    Overtime limit breachedUse standby, not extended hours
    OverstaffingReview buffer monthly
    Buddy punchingSpot checks of floor vs biometric records
    Daily overstaffing cost = Extra workers × Hours × Cost per hour
    🧪
    Worked example: On a day with low absence, 12 extra workers were on the roster: 12 × 8 hours × ₹250 = ₹24,000 a day. Over 26 working days that is ₹6,24,000. The manager reviews the buffer rule so it adapts to the calendar.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Keeping a fixed high buffer all year
    • Breaking overtime rules to cover gaps
    • Trusting biometric data without floor checks

    ✍️ Practice

    8 extra workers worked 8 hours at ₹220 per hour. What is the daily cost of the overstaffing?

    Show answer
    8 × 8 × 220 = ₹14,080 a day.
    Module 4

    Shift-fill rate optimization

    Raising the share of required shifts that are actually staffed, without breaking cost, quality or compliance limits.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    📊 What shift-fill rate is and why it matters

    Shift-fill rate is the percentage of required shifts that are actually staffed. A shift here means one worker slot on one shift. If the client needs 500 shift slots in a week and 460 are staffed, the fill rate is 92%.

    Why it matters: fill rate is often the headline measure in the contract. A low fill rate means unmet client demand, lost output and, in many contracts, financial penalties. Shift-fill is also a leading signal of trouble in hiring, retention and attendance.

    TermMeaning
    Shift slotOne worker position on one shift
    FillA slot staffed by a qualified worker
    UnfilledA required slot with nobody assigned or present
    Lead timeTime between the request and the shift start
    Standby poolTrained workers ready to fill late gaps
    Cross-trainedAble to work on more than one task or line
    Shift-fill rate = Shifts filled ÷ Shifts required
    🧪
    Worked example: A client requires 500 shifts in a week and 460 are filled. Fill rate = 460 ÷ 500 = 92%. 40 shifts are unfilled.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Counting a rostered worker as filled even when they did not turn up
    • Mixing fill rate with attendance rate
    • Measuring only at week end

    ✍️ Practice

    400 shifts were required and 372 were filled. What is the fill rate?

    Show answer
    372 ÷ 400 = 93%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Why shifts go unfilled, and the ways to fill them

    Unfilled shifts have causes: not enough workers in the pool, requests that arrive too late, skills that do not match the task, transport or distance problems, and pay or conditions that make shifts unattractive. Naming the cause tells you which fix will work.

    There are also different channels for filling a shift: the core pool of regular workers, a standby pool, referrals from current workers, surge supply from a supplier and redeploying workers from lower-priority lines. Each has a speed and cost.

    CauseExampleLikely fix
    Supply shortagePool too smallRecruit more
    Late noticeRequest arrives 2 hours before shiftAgree minimum notice with client
    Skill mismatchNo forklift-trained workersCross-train
    TransportDistance, no busArrange pickup or transport allowance
    PayRate below nearby sitesReview rate with client
    Share of unfilled shifts by cause = Cause count ÷ Total unfilled
    🧪
    Worked example: Over a week 40 shifts were unfilled: 16 for transport, 12 for late notice, 8 for skill mismatch and 4 for other reasons. Transport share = 16 ÷ 40 = 40%, so a pickup route has the biggest effect.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Hiring more people when the cause is transport
    • Not recording the reason for each unfilled shift
    • Treating all unfilled shifts as supplier failure

    ✍️ Practice

    In a week 30 shifts were unfilled and 12 were due to late notice. What share is that?

    Show answer
    12 ÷ 30 = 40%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🗓️ The fill process, step by step

    A repeatable process lifts fill rate. Steps: (1) forecast demand using client plans and history; (2) publish the roster early; (3) confirm each worker for tomorrow's shift by call or message; (4) raise an early alert for any unconfirmed shifts about 24 hours ahead; (5) activate standby for what remains; (6) track fill daily and review causes weekly.

    Confirmation is the step most often skipped, and it is the cheapest. A worker who has confirmed is far more likely to appear. Tools: a fill tracker, a standby list with contact details and a simple message group for quick reach.

    StepTimingTool
    ForecastWeeklyClient plan and history
    Publish roster3 to 5 days aheadRoster
    ConfirmEvening beforeCalls or messages
    Early alert24 hours aheadFill tracker
    Activate standbyShift morningStandby list
    Confirmation rate = Confirmed ÷ Rostered
    🧪
    Worked example: For tomorrow, 100 shifts are rostered and 88 are confirmed by 6 pm, a confirmation rate of 88%. The team calls the 12 unconfirmed workers and 8 confirm, giving 96. The remaining 4 come from standby, so all 100 are covered.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Rostering without confirming
    • Waiting until shift morning to chase
    • Not keeping standby contact details current

    ✍️ Practice

    150 shifts are rostered and 135 are confirmed. What is the confirmation rate?

    Show answer
    135 ÷ 150 = 90%.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    🧮 Levers and calculations: sizing the standby pool

    The standby pool is the strongest lever. Too small and gaps stay open. Too large and you pay for idle workers. A simple rule is to size it from the average daily gap multiplied by a safety factor, for example 1.5, to cover peak days.

    Calculate the effect before acting. If 40 shifts are unfilled in a week and a standby pool is expected to fill 25, the new fill rate is (460 + 25) ÷ 500 = 97%.

    Combine levers: earlier confirmation reduces gaps, cross-training widens who can fill them, and a transport route removes the biggest cause.

    LeverEffectWatch-out
    Standby poolFills late gapsIdle cost
    Early confirmationReduces no-showsNeeds effort daily
    Cross-trainingWidens who can fillTraining time
    Transport routeRemoves distance causeRoute cost
    Standby size = Average daily gap × Safety factor | New fill rate = (Filled + Standby fills) ÷ Required
    🧪
    Worked example: An average daily gap of 10 with a factor of 1.5 gives a standby pool of 15. Over a week, with 460 of 500 filled and standby covering 25 more, the new fill rate = 485 ÷ 500 = 97%.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Sizing the pool by guess
    • Counting the pool as fully effective on day one
    • Using only one lever

    ✍️ Practice

    300 shifts were required and 276 were filled. The standby pool will fill 15 more. What is the new fill rate?

    Show answer
    (276 + 15) ÷ 300 = 291 ÷ 300 = 97%.
    Level 4 · Expert

    ⚖️ Expert: balancing fill, cost, quality and compliance

    Perfect fill is not always the right goal. Pushing from 96% to 100% can cost far more than it earns. The expert compares the cost of each extra percentage point against the benefit, which may be avoided penalties or protected client trust.

    Quality and compliance set limits. A filled shift with an unqualified worker is not a real fill. Fatigue from long hours raises errors and breaches working-time rules. Cheap fill that damages quality or safety is a poor trade.

    Where the contract has SLA credits, the maths is direct: compare the credit avoided with the cost of the extra standby capacity.

    Trade-offQuestion to ask
    Extra standby costDoes it avoid more in credits than it costs?
    Overtime to fillDoes it stay inside the legal limit?
    Unqualified fillWill quality or safety suffer?
    Over-fillAre we paying for idle hours?
    Net benefit = SLA credit avoided − Extra cost
    🧪
    Worked example: The SLA credit is ₹40,000 for each percentage point below the 98% target. Fill is 96%, so the credit is 2 × ₹40,000 = ₹80,000. An extra standby team costing ₹50,000 a month lifts fill to 98%. Net benefit = ₹80,000 − ₹50,000 = ₹30,000.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Chasing 100% regardless of cost
    • Counting an unqualified worker as a fill
    • Ignoring working-time limits to cover shifts

    ✍️ Practice

    The SLA credit is ₹30,000 per point below a 98% target. Fill is 95%. Extra standby costs ₹60,000 and lifts fill to 98%. What is the net benefit?

    Show answer
    3 points × ₹30,000 = ₹90,000 credit avoided. ₹90,000 − ₹60,000 = ₹30,000.
    Module 5

    Client operational alignment

    Making sure the outsourced team works to the client's targets, rules and rhythm, and stays aligned as things change.

    Level 1 · FoundationsLevel 2 · Core conceptsLevel 3 · IntermediateLevel 4 · Expert
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🤝 What alignment is and why it matters

    Client operational alignment means the account manager and the outsourced team work to the same goals, rules and routines as the client's own operation. The client should feel the outsourced team is part of their operation, not a separate unit.

    Why it matters: misalignment causes missed targets, rework and blame. Aligned teams share priorities, report the same numbers and solve problems together. Alignment is also what protects contract renewal.

    TermMeaning
    SLAService level agreement: promises in the contract
    KPIA measured performance indicator
    SOPStandard operating procedure
    QBRQuarterly business review
    Escalation matrixWho to contact for each level of issue
    Change requestA formal request to alter scope, volume or terms
    SLA compliance = KPIs met ÷ KPIs measured
    🧪
    Worked example: The SLA has 10 KPIs and the site met 8 of them last month. SLA compliance = 8 ÷ 10 = 80%. The two missed KPIs become the focus of the next review.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Working to internal targets that the client does not know
    • Reporting different numbers from the client's own
    • Learning the client's rules only after a breach

    ✍️ Practice

    The SLA has 12 KPIs and the site met 9. What is the SLA compliance?

    Show answer
    9 ÷ 12 = 75%.
    Level 1 · Foundations

    🗂️ Types of stakeholders, meetings and documents

    Alignment depends on knowing who matters and how you talk to them. Stakeholders include the client's sponsor, operations manager, shift supervisors, HR, safety officer and finance. Each cares about different things: the sponsor about results and cost, supervisors about daily output, the safety officer about compliance.

    Rhythm comes from regular meetings: a short daily huddle, a weekly review and a monthly or quarterly business review. Key documents are the SLA, SOPs, the scorecard and the escalation matrix.

    ItemPurposeFrequency
    Daily huddlePlan the shift, share issuesDaily, 10 minutes
    Weekly reviewReview KPIs and actionsWeekly
    Monthly review or QBRPerformance, trends, improvement plansMonthly or quarterly
    Escalation matrixRoute issues to the right personAlways available
    Meeting adherence = Meetings held ÷ Meetings planned
    🧪
    Worked example: 26 daily huddles were planned this month and 24 were held. Adherence = 24 ÷ 26 = 92.3%. The two missed ones were on days with a shift-change disruption.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Talking only to the operations manager
    • Skipping huddles when busy
    • No written escalation path

    ✍️ Practice

    20 huddles were planned and 18 were held. What is the adherence?

    Show answer
    18 ÷ 20 = 90%.
    Level 2 · Core concepts

    🧭 Setting up alignment: onboarding, goals and reporting

    Alignment is set up in the first weeks. Steps: (1) attend the client's site induction and learn its rules; (2) agree the KPIs, targets and how each is calculated; (3) agree the reporting format and timing; (4) set the meeting rhythm; (5) agree how changes and escalations are handled; (6) brief the outsourced team on all of this.

    The most important step is agreeing definitions. If the client counts output at packing and you count it at assembly, the two reports will never match. Write down each KPI's formula and data source and have the client sign off.

    Setup stepOutput
    Site inductionRules understood
    KPI agreementTargets and formulas signed off
    Reporting formatStandard daily and weekly report
    Meeting rhythmCalendar of huddles and reviews
    Escalation pathContact matrix
    Target achievement = Actual ÷ Target
    🧪
    Worked example: The daily output target is 9,000 units and the actual was 8,550. Achievement = 8,550 ÷ 9,000 = 95%. The report also shows the cause, a 40-minute material delay.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Starting work before KPIs are agreed
    • Differences in how output is counted
    • Skipping the induction

    ✍️ Practice

    The target is 5,000 units and the actual was 4,650. What is the achievement?

    Show answer
    4,650 ÷ 5,000 = 93%.
    Level 3 · Intermediate

    📑 Reporting and reviews: scorecards, trends and quality

    Reports should be short, consistent and honest. A good scorecard shows the KPI, the target, the actual, the status (red, amber or green) and a trend over time. A single bad day matters less than a trend. Always add a short note on cause and action for any red item.

    Quality is part of alignment. Defect rate is a standard measure: defects divided by units produced. Present it alongside output so the client sees that volume is not coming at the cost of quality.

    KPITargetActualStatus
    Output9,0008,550Amber
    Defect rate1.5% or less2.0%Red
    Fill rate98%97%Amber
    Attendance95%94%Amber
    Defect rate = Defects ÷ Units produced
    🧪
    Worked example: A line produced 8,550 units with 171 defects. Defect rate = 171 ÷ 8,550 = 2.0%. The target is 1.5% or less, so it is red. The note explains that new workers on the line caused most defects and a coaching plan is in place.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Reports with numbers but no cause or action
    • Showing only today instead of the trend
    • Hiding bad numbers until the review

    ✍️ Practice

    A line produced 6,000 units with 90 defects. What is the defect rate?

    Show answer
    90 ÷ 6,000 = 1.5%.
    Level 4 · Expert

    🔄 Expert: change requests, scope creep and escalation

    Over time the client's needs change: more volume, new tasks, extra shifts. If these are handled informally they become scope creep, where the team does more work than the contract covers with no extra billing and no extra planning. The expert uses a change request: a short written agreement on what changes, for how long and at what price.

    Escalation should be early and factual. When priorities conflict, for example a supervisor and the operations manager asking for different things, the account manager brings both to one conversation and records the decision.

    Renewal readiness is built daily: consistent reporting, a visible record of improvements and good relationships at several levels of the client's organisation.

    SituationResponse
    Client asks for extra workersRaise a change request with scope, duration and rate
    Conflicting instructionsBring both parties together and record the decision
    Repeated KPI missesPresent a recovery plan, do not wait for the review
    Contract renewal approachingShow a trend of improvements and relationship map
    Change cost = Workers × Days × Hours per day × Bill rate
    🧪
    Worked example: The client asks for 20 extra workers for 24 working days, 8 hours a day, at a bill rate of ₹300. Change cost = 20 × 24 × 8 × 300 = ₹11,52,000. The work starts only after the client signs the change request.

    ⚠️ Common mistakes

    • Doing extra work on a verbal request
    • Escalating late or without facts
    • Waiting for the renewal meeting to show value

    ✍️ Practice

    The client asks for 15 extra workers for 12 days, 8 hours a day, at a bill rate of ₹280. What is the change cost?

    Show answer
    15 × 12 × 8 = 1,440 hours. 1,440 × 280 = ₹4,03,200.

    The tools, workflows and quality checks for each skill.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: Direct floor oversight

    Floor oversight runs on a few simple paper or spreadsheet tools that you carry on every walk. The aim is to turn what you see into a logged, owned and closed action. Most good managers keep the tools light so they never replace talking to people.

    Tool or techniqueUsed forExample
    Floor-walk checklistSame observation points on every roundTick PPE, attendance, pace, housekeeping, SOP use
    Issue logTurn observations into tracked actionsIssue 14: no gloves at Line B, owner Ravi, due 11:00

    5 Whys templateFind root cause of repeat issuesWhy late material? Late order release? Handover gap
    Near-miss registerLearn from events that caused no harmPallet slipped near walkway, no injury, logged

    Working procedure

    1. Review the roster, open issues and client requests before leaving the desk.
    2. Pick a route that differs from yesterday and cover every line and shift over the week.
    3. Observe safety, pace, quality and behaviour using the checklist.
    4. Ask workers one open question, such as what slows you down today.
    5. Log every issue at once with owner, due time and priority.
    6. Fix small items on the spot and escalate the rest by the escalation matrix.
    7. Close the loop by telling workers and supervisors what was done.
    8. At day end, review the log and update the closure and repeat counts.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: Daily headcount maintenance

    Headcount work runs on a roster, an attendance record and a standby list. You compare them every shift and close gaps inside the cut-off time. Clean data matters more than fancy software.

    Working procedure

    1. Confirm tomorrow's roster with workers the evening before.
    2. Run the roll call or biometric check at shift start.
    3. Match the attendance record to the roster and list absentees.
    4. Calculate the gap as required minus present.
    5. Call standby workers and move floaters before the cut-off time.
    6. Tell the client the final headcount and any remaining gap with a plan.
    7. Check the floor count against the biometric record for a sample of workers.
    8. Reconcile the day in the tracker and update absenteeism data.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: Shift-fill rate optimization

    Fill-rate work uses a forecast, a confirmation routine and a record of why each shift went unfilled. The tools are simple, but they must be updated daily. The reasons data tells you which lever to pull.

    1. Collect the client's demand plan and compare it with last month's actuals.

    5. Activate standby and cross-trained workers on the shift morning.
    6. Record a reason code for every unfilled shift.
    7. Review the reasons weekly and choose one lever to change.
    8. Compare the cost of each lever with the credit it avoids before acting.

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Direct floor oversight

    Why is floor oversight support and not policing?

    Show model answer
    The goal is to keep output, quality and safety on track and help workers do their job. If workers feel watched only to be blamed, they hide problems. When they see issues fixed, they report more, and you find problems earlier.

    What is the role boundary between you and the client supervisor?

    Show model answer
    The client supervisor directs day-to-day work. You manage your company's people and the contract. You do not contradict the supervisor in front of workers. You raise differences privately and agree one instruction.

    Output on Line B is falling and the supervisor wants to push workers harder. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    I would walk the line first and time what workers actually do. If they wait for material, pushing will not help. I would show the waiting data to the client and agree a material hand-off, then re-check the output after a week.

    A worker is late three times in a week. How do you respond?

    Show model answer
    I would first check transport and shift timing, since these are common causes. I would talk to the worker, record the reason and agree a plan. Discipline comes only if the cause is within the worker's control and the pattern continues.

    A site has 150 workers and 8 supervisors, with a limit of 20 per supervisor. Is it compliant?

    Show model answer
    Span = 150 ÷ 8 = 18.75 workers per supervisor. That is below 20, so it is within the limit. Some lines may still be above 20 even if the average is fine, so check each line.

    💬 Interview Questions: Daily headcount maintenance

    What is the difference between rostered, present and productive headcount?

    Show model answer
    Rostered is who was planned. Present is who arrived. Productive is who is working and fully trained. A trainee may be present but not productive, so counting them as full capacity gives a false picture.

    Why use required divided by one minus absenteeism for the buffer?

    Show model answer
    It sizes the roster so that the workers who actually turn up equal the requirement. A flat percentage may be too high or too low. The formula uses your own absenteeism data and is rounded up.

    The client calls at 7 am and says the line is short but your sheet is full. What do you do?

    Show model answer
    I would go to the floor and count, then compare faces with the biometric record. If buddy punching or early punches caused it, I would fix the record and cover the gap with standby. I would report the finding to the client the same day.

    Absence is high the day after a festival. How do you prepare?

    Show model answer
    I would check the absence calendar and last year's data, then roster extra workers and confirm each worker the evening before. I would line up standby and floaters. All of this has to stay inside working-time limits.

    The client needs 180 productive workers and absenteeism is 9 percent. How many do you roster?

    Show model answer
    180 ÷ (1 − 0.09) = 180 ÷ 0.91 = 197.8. Round up and roster 198. With 9 percent absent, about 18 will be missing, leaving about 180 present.

    💬 Interview Questions: Shift-fill rate optimization

    How is shift-fill rate different from attendance rate?

    Show model answer
    Fill rate compares shifts filled with shifts the client required. Attendance compares workers present with workers rostered. A site can have good attendance and poor fill if the roster was too small.

    Why record a reason code for each unfilled shift?

    Show model answer
    Without a reason you cannot choose the right fix. If transport is the largest cause, a pickup route helps more than recruitment. Reasons also show whether the problem is yours or the client's, such as late notice.

    Fill is 94 percent and the target is 98. A manager suggests hiring 30 more workers. What do you check first?

    Show model answer
    I would check the reason codes. If most gaps come from transport or late notice, extra hiring will not fix them. I would size the fix to the largest cause and compare cost with the credit avoided.

    The client wants 100 percent fill. How do you respond?

    Show model answer
    I would show the cost of each extra point against the benefit, using the SLA credit as the guide. Often a target such as 98 percent is the right balance. I would also point out that overtime and unqualified fills break rules.

    250 shifts were required, 230 filled, and standby will fill 10 more. What is the new fill rate?

    Show model answer
    (230 + 10) ÷ 250 = 240 ÷ 250 = 0.96, which is 96%. Before standby it was 230 ÷ 250 = 92%, so standby adds 4 points.

    💬 Interview Questions: Client operational alignment

    Why must KPI definitions be agreed in writing?

    Show model answer
    Different counting points or formulas make reports disagree even when the work is the same. A signed definition removes arguments and shows both sides the same truth. It also protects you if targets are disputed later.

    What is scope creep and how do you prevent it?

    Show model answer
    Scope creep is doing more work than the contract covers without extra billing or planning. I prevent it with a written change request that states what changes, for how long and at what price. Work starts after the client signs.

    Course Summary

    Onsite Workforce Account Manager: key points, formulas and next steps.

    📚 What you covered

    Direct floor oversight

    • Be present on the floor on every shift and vary your route.
    • Log every issue with an owner and due time, then close the loop.
    • Read productivity, quality and safety together.
    • Use 5 Whys on repeats and respect the client's role boundaries.

    Daily headcount maintenance

    • Match the roster to the attendance record at the start of each shift.
    • Close gaps with standby and floaters within the cut-off time.
    • Size the roster as required divided by one minus absenteeism, rounded up.
    • Forecast risky days and stay within working-time limits.

    Shift-fill rate optimization

    • Fill rate is shifts filled divided by shifts required.
    • Code the reason for every unfilled shift and fix the biggest cause.
    • Confirm workers the evening before and size standby from the daily gap.
    • Compare lever cost with the SLA credit avoided.

    Client operational alignment

    • Agree KPI definitions in writing and report the same numbers as the client.
    • Run a regular meeting rhythm and give a cause and action for every red item.
    • Use change requests for extra work and escalate early.
    • Build a record of improvements before the renewal.
