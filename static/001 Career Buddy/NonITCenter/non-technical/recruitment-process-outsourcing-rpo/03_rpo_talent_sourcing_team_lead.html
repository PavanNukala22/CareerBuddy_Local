    Module 01 · Foundations

    The Talent Sourcing Team Lead Role

    The person who makes sure the right candidates are found and engaged before recruiters need them: running the sourcing team in short, focused cycles, keeping workloads balanced, using the right channels, and proving it with pipeline numbers.

    🔎 What is a Talent Sourcing Team Lead?

    In an RPO engagement, sourcers find and first-engage candidates: searching databases and networks, writing outreach, and building talent pools. Recruiters then take interested candidates forward: screening, presenting them to hiring managers and managing the process through to offer.

    The Talent Sourcing Team Lead manages that sourcing team. They decide what the team works on each week, how work is shared out, where to look for talent, and how to tell whether it is working. It is a hands-on leadership role: part planner, part coach, part analyst.

    🏃
    Sprint planner
    Organizes sourcing into short cycles with clear goals and quick feedback.
    ⚖️
    Workload balancer
    Keeps work spread fairly so no sourcer is overloaded and none sits idle.
    🧭
    Channel strategist
    Chooses where to look for each type of role, and drops what does not work.
    📊
    Pipeline analyst
    Tracks the numbers that show whether there will be enough candidates next month.

    🔄 Where Sourcing Sits in the Hiring Flow

    Sourcing is the front end of the funnel. If it is weak, everything after it slows down, because recruiters have no one to screen and managers have no one to interview.

    Module 02 · Ways of working

    Agile Sprint Sourcing Management

    Sourcing priorities change weekly: roles open, close, get put on hold or become urgent. Working in short, time-boxed sprints gives the team focus without losing the ability to react.

    🎯 Why Agile for Sourcing?

    Agile ideas come from software teams, but they fit sourcing well, because sourcing work is also uncertain, iterative and dependent on feedback. You do not know whether a message, a channel or a search will work until you try it.

    ❌ Without sprints

    • Every open role feels equally urgent
    • Sourcers spread thin across too many roles
    • Problems found weeks later, at the end of a search
    • Little shared learning between team members

    ✅ With sprints

    • A short, clear list of what matters this cycle
    • Focused effort, with work limits per person
    • Fast feedback on what is working
    • A regular moment to learn and adjust
    📌
    You do not have to adopt every agile ceremony. Take what helps: short cycles, a visible board, a quick daily check-in, and a regular review. Drop anything that becomes admin for its own sake.

    ⏱️ The Sprint Structure

    Many sourcing teams use one- or two-week sprints. A one-week sprint suits fast-moving volume hiring, while two weeks gives more room for hard-to-fill roles. The diagram below shows a two-week example.

    Figure 2.1 · Planning, stand-ups, mid-point check, review and retrospective
    EventPurposeTypical length
    Sprint planningAgree which roles to focus on, targets for the sprint, and who works on what45 to 60 minutes
    Daily stand-upShare progress, raise blockers, ask for help10 to 15 minutes
    Mid-sprint checkSpot problems early and move work if needed15 to 30 minutes
    Sprint reviewShow results against the sprint goals, ideally with recruiters present30 minutes
    RetrospectiveDecide what to start, stop and continue30 to 45 minutes

    📋 The Backlog and Priorities

    The backlog is the list of all open roles and sourcing needs. Each sprint, you pull the highest-priority items into the sprint and leave the rest in the backlog. Without a priority rule, whoever shouts loudest wins.

    A simple scoring approach looks at four things for each requisition:

    FactorQuestion to askHigher priority when…
    Business impactWhat happens if this role stays empty?Revenue, operations or a launch depend on it
    UrgencyWhen must the person start?The deadline is near, or a notice period is long
    DifficultyHow scarce are suitable candidates?The role needs rare skills and a long lead time to find them
    ReadinessIs the role approved and the brief clear?Intake is done and the manager is available
    💡
    Start hard roles early. A hard-to-fill role that begins only when it becomes urgent will miss its date. Give scarce-skill roles a head start in the sprint plan, even if they are not yet at the top of anyone's list.

    🗂️ A Visual Board

    A simple board makes the work visible, shows bottlenecks, and gives the stand-up something to look at. It can be a physical wall, a board inside the ATS, or a project tool.

    Figure 2.2 · Cards move left to right. A column that fills up shows a bottleneck.

    Set a work-in-progress limit for each person or column, for example no more than five active roles per sourcer. Spreading attention across too many roles lowers quality on all of them.

    🔁 Reviews and Retrospectives

    The sprint review is about results: what was achieved against the sprint goals. The retrospective is about how the team works: what to change. Keep them separate.

    📊 Sprint review: questions

    • Did we reach the targets for each priority role?
    • How many qualified candidates did we hand over?
    • What did recruiters and hiring managers say about quality?
    • Which roles need a different approach?

    🔧 Retrospective: questions

    • What worked well that we should keep doing?
    • What slowed us down, and what is one fix?
    • Which outreach or search ideas should we try next?
    • Is the workload fair, and does anyone need help?
    ✅
    Pick one or two improvements per retro, assign owners, and check them at the next one. A long list of ideas that nobody follows up on teaches the team that retros do not matter.

    Module 03 · Resourcing

    Team Capacity Load Balancing

    A team can only do a certain amount of good sourcing in a sprint. Capacity planning compares the work that exists with the time the team really has, and shares it out so quality holds up and nobody burns out.

    🧮 What Capacity Means

    Capacity is the number of productive sourcing hours a person has in a sprint. It is always lower than their paid hours, because of meetings, training, leave, admin and the need to switch between tasks.

    formulaCapacity per person = Available working days × Productive hours per day × Focus factor

# Illustrative example for a two-week sprint:
#   10 working days, minus 0 days leave          = 10 days
#   6 productive hours per day (rest is meetings/admin)
#   Focus factor 0.85 (allowance for task switching and interruptions)
#   Capacity = 10 × 6 × 0.85 = 51 hours
    📌
    The numbers above are illustrations. Use your own team's data: ask sourcers to track how their time really splits for a couple of sprints, then set your own productive hours and focus factor.

    ⚖️ Estimating Demand

    Not all roles take the same effort. Giving each role an effort estimate, in hours or points, lets you compare demand with capacity. The easiest way is a small set of role categories, adjusted from your own history.

    Role categoryTypical effort per sprint (illustrative)Why
    Standard8 hoursPlentiful candidates, clear brief, many places to search
    Hard-to-fill20 hoursScarce skills, more searching and more outreach per response
    Niche or senior30 hoursSmall market, personalized outreach, longer relationship building

    Review these estimates every few sprints against what the team actually spent, and adjust.

    📊 Balancing the Load

    Compare total demand with total capacity, then look at how work sits across individuals. A team can be on target overall and still have one person drowning while another waits for work.

    worked example# Team of 4 sourcers, capacity 51 hours each
Team capacity   = 4 × 51                        = 204 hours

# Demand this sprint
6 standard roles   × 8   =  48
5 hard-to-fill     × 20  = 100
2 niche roles      × 30  =  60
Total demand     =                              208 hours

Utilization      = 208 ÷ 204 ≈ 102%   → slightly over capacity

    At about 102% there is no room for surprises. A reasonable target is to plan below full capacity, leaving room for urgent new roles and illness, for example 85% to 90%. Here, that points to a decision: move some roles into the next sprint, bring in extra help, or both.

    Figure 3.1 · Rebalancing without changing the total amount of work

    🧩 Matching Skills to Roles

    Equal hours is not the same as equal fit. Balance the load by hours first, then check that the right people have the right work.

    🎓
    Specialisms
    Some sourcers know technology markets, others finance or healthcare. Use it, but also build cross-training.
    🌍
    Languages and regions
    Match by language, local market knowledge and time zone where that matters.
    📈
    Experience level
    Give newer sourcers a mix of standard roles, with a buddy, before the hardest searches.
    🔄
    Rotation
    Rotate difficult or repetitive work so it does not always fall on the same people.
    ⚠️
    Avoid single points of failure. If only one person can source a certain skill set, a day of leave or a resignation stalls the whole search. Cross-train a second person on every critical specialism.

    🚨 Overload and Surge

    Warning signs of overload

    • Outreach becomes generic and response rates fall
    • Candidates are handed over with thin notes, so recruiters redo the work
    • Roles are touched rarely, and sit idle between searches
    • Stand-ups turn into lists of things not done
    • Rising sick days, irritability or quiet resignation

    Options when demand outruns capacity

    OptionGood forWatch out for
    Re-prioritize and deferShort peaks with some flexibility on lower-priority rolesMake sure the client agrees to the trade-off
    Borrow from a shared poolShort-term help from other teamsOnboarding time and context loss
    Add temporary or contract sourcersSustained surges, such as a campaign or new projectCost, training, and consistency of quality
    Use sourcing partners or agenciesNiche skills or locations outside the team's reachFees, brand control and data handling
    Improve efficiencyOngoing pressureTemplates, automation and better search strings reduce effort per role
    💡
    Raise capacity gaps with the delivery lead early, with numbers. "We are at 115% for the next two sprints and need X" is a decision a manager can make. "We are very busy" is not.

    Module 04 · Strategy

    Multi-channel Sourcing Strategy

    No single channel finds every kind of candidate. A multi-channel strategy matches the right places to the right roles, tests them against results, and keeps shifting effort toward what works.

    🗺️ The Channel Landscape

    Sourcing channels fall into a few families. Most strategies use a combination of them.

    ChannelWhat it isStrengthsLimits
    Job boards and career siteAdvertising roles so that active job seekers applyReach, volume, speed for many rolesMostly active candidates; screening effort can be high
    Direct search (professional networks)Finding and approaching passive candidates on professional networks and similar sitesTargets specific skills and experienceTime-intensive, lower response rates, tool costs
    ATS and CRM rediscoveryRe-contacting past applicants and silver-medal candidates already in your databaseCheap, fast, already known to the clientData goes stale, consent and retention limits apply
    Employee referralsExisting staff recommending people they knowOften strong quality and retentionCan limit diversity if used alone
    Talent communitiesOpt-in groups of people interested in the employerWarm audience, long-term pipelineNeeds regular, relevant content to stay alive
    Events and campusCareer fairs, hackathons, meet-ups, university relationshipsEarly talent and relationship buildingCost and time, uneven short-term results
    Communities and social platformsSkill-specific forums, groups and online communitiesReach into niche professional groupsNeed real participation, not only posting
    Internal mobilityMoving current employees into new rolesRetention, speed, known performanceNeeds a client process and manager support
    Partners and agenciesExternal specialist firms for hard casesNetwork in niche markets, extra capacityFees, brand and data control

    🎯 Choosing Channels by Role

    The right mix depends on the role. A simple matrix helps the team decide quickly where to start.

    Role typeStart withAdd
    High-volume roles (for example customer support, operations)Job boards, career site, rediscoveryReferrals, events, talent community
    Specialist or scarce skills (for example engineering, data)Direct search, rediscovery, communitiesReferrals, partners
    Senior or executiveDirect search, referrals, partnersNetwork mapping and market intelligence
    Early careersCampus, events, talent communityJob boards and social platforms
    Hard-to-reach diverse talentCommunities, partnerships with professional bodiesBroader job-post reach and inclusive language checks
    📌
    This is a starting point, not a rulebook. Adjust it from your own results and from conversations with hiring managers about where their best hires have come from.

    🧪 Building a Channel Mix

    Spread effort across channels on purpose, rather than defaulting to the easiest one. Many teams keep most of their effort on proven channels and set aside a small share for experiments.

    Figure 4.1 · Percentages are for illustration only. Reserve a share for trying new channels.

    📏 Measuring Channel Effectiveness

    Judge a channel by what it produces, not only how much activity it creates. Compare channels on several measures together.

    MeasureWhat it shows
    VolumeHow many qualified candidates the channel yields
    QualityShare of candidates who pass screening, interview and become hires
    SpeedHow long from first contact to a submitted candidate
    CostSpend plus sourcer time per qualified candidate or per hire
    DiversityHow well the channel reaches the client's representation goals
    RetentionHow long hires from that channel stay and how they perform
    ⚠️
    Do not judge by the "last click". The channel recorded as the source of a hire may not have been the one that found the person. Agree a clear rule for source tracking and apply it consistently, so numbers can be compared fairly.

    ✉️ Outreach That Gets Replies

    Whichever channel you use, the first message decides whether a conversation starts. Good outreach is short, specific and respectful of the candidate's time.

    outreach structureWhy you:      One specific detail showing you read their profile or work.
Why this:     What the role is, and one or two real reasons it may interest them.
Next step:    A small, easy ask, such as a short call or a reply to a question.
Exit:         Make it easy to say no or to be removed from future contact.

    ✅ Do

    • Personalize the first line
    • Be honest about the role, pay range and location where known
    • Follow up once or twice, politely, then stop
    • Test two versions and keep the better one

    ❌ Avoid

    • Long, generic messages sent to hundreds at once
    • Overstating the role or hiding the client's name without reason
    • Ignoring opt-out requests
    • Contacting people against local data protection and marketing rules
    🔐
    Respect privacy rules. Candidate data is personal data. Follow the client's and local regulations on consent, contact preferences and retention, and record opt-outs promptly.

    Module 05 · Measurement

    Pipelining KPIs

    A pipeline is the set of candidates moving toward a role. Pipeline KPIs tell you whether there will be enough qualified candidates, early enough, to hit the hiring plan, while there is still time to do something about it.

    🚰 What a Pipeline Is

    Think of a pipeline as the funnel from first contact to hire. At each stage some candidates drop out, so you must start with many more people than the hires you need.

    Figure 5.1 · Numbers are illustrative. Your funnel will differ by role, channel and client.

    📐 Core Pipeline Measures

    MeasureHow it is calculatedWhat it tells you
    Outreach response rateReplies ÷ messages sentQuality of targeting and messaging
    Interest rateInterested candidates ÷ repliesHow attractive the role and the message are
    Screen-to-submit rateSubmitted ÷ screened by recruitersWhether sourced candidates meet the brief
    Submit-to-interview rateInterviewed ÷ submittedWhether hiring managers find candidates relevant
    Pipeline coverageQualified candidates in progress ÷ hires neededWhether there are enough candidates to hit the plan
    Time to first submissionDays from role opening to the first qualified candidate submittedHow quickly the team gets going
    Source of hireHires by originating channelWhich channels deliver results
    Pool freshnessShare of talent pool contacted or updated recentlyWhether the database is usable or stale

    🧮 Working Backward From the Hires You Need

    Pipeline planning starts from the hiring target and works backward through the conversion rates at each stage. This tells you how many candidates you need at the top, and shows where the funnel is leaking.

    worked example# Illustrative conversion rates, per stage
Contacted → Replied and interested   20%
Replied   → Screened                 50%
Screened  → Submitted                40%
Submitted → Interviewed              50%
Interviewed → Offer                  25%
Offer     → Hire (accepted)          80%

# Working backward for ONE hire:
Offers needed         = 1    ÷ 0.80 = 1.25
Interviews needed     = 1.25 ÷ 0.25 = 5
Submissions needed    = 5    ÷ 0.50 = 10
Screens needed        = 10   ÷ 0.40 = 25
Replies needed        = 25   ÷ 0.50 = 50
Outreach needed      = 50   ÷ 0.20 = 250 contacts per hire

# For 4 hires of this kind: about 1,000 contacts, or 40 submissions
    💡
    Use the math to find the lever. If response rate rises from 20% to 25%, outreach per hire falls from 250 to 200. If submit-to-interview improves because managers like the candidates more, the saving carries through every stage above it.

    🔭 Leading and Lagging Indicators

    Some measures tell you what already happened. Others warn you about what is about to happen. A good dashboard has both.

    🔭 Leading indicators

    Move early, and give time to act.

    • Outreach volume and response rate
    • Qualified candidates in the pipeline
    • Pipeline coverage per role
    • Time to first submission

    🪞 Lagging indicators

    Confirm results, but arrive late.

    • Time to fill
    • Offer acceptance rate
    • Hires by source
    • Quality of hire

    If you only watch lagging measures, you learn about a missed hiring date after it has happened. Leading indicators let the sourcing lead intervene while there is still time.

    🎛️ Reading and Acting on the Numbers

    A KPI is useful only if it leads to a decision. Review the pipeline in every stand-up and sprint review, ask what has changed, and decide what to do.

    What you seeLikely causePossible action
    Low response rateWeak targeting or messageRefine search criteria, rewrite outreach, test variants
    Good interest, poor screen passBrief not clear, or candidates mis-matchedRe-run intake with the manager, tighten criteria
    Good submissions, few interviewsManager expectations differ from the briefCalibrate with sample profiles and quick feedback
    Low pipeline coverageNot enough activity, or market is thinAdd capacity, widen channels, adjust expectations
    Strong pipeline, offers declinedOffer or process problemReview pay, speed and candidate experience
    ⚠️
    Beware vanity metrics. Messages sent and profiles viewed are easy to count but say little about results. Use them to understand effort, and judge success by responses, qualified submissions and hires.
    🏁
    That completes the loop. The pipeline numbers feed the next sprint plan, which sets the load, which drives the channels. Take the assessment next to check how much has stuck.

    Module 06

    Assessment

    Test yourself on sprints, capacity, channels and pipeline KPIs. Multiple-choice questions, timed like a real screening test. Difficulty ranges from quick checks to genuinely tricky judgement calls.

    Test what you've learned
    A timed, exam-style mock test. Some questions are quick concept checks; others describe a realistic situation and ask what you would do.
    • Multiple-choice questions with a mix of concept and scenario items
    • Difficulty from Simple to Hard
    • Covers sprints, capacity, channels and pipeline KPIs

    🔒 Exam Rules & Security

    • The test runs in fullscreen with a countdown timer; it auto-submits when time is up.
    • No tab or window switching and no exiting fullscreen. After 3 warnings the test auto-submits.
    • Screenshots, copy, cut, print and right-click are disabled; refreshing or closing the page asks for confirmation.
    • Navigate freely, mark questions for review, then submit. Results and explanations appear only after submission.
    • Answers are graded on the LMS server when available, so they are never present in the page.
