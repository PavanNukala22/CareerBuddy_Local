    Module 01

    Course Overview

    International Talent Sourcer: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how an International Talent Sourcer finds and engages skilled people in other countries. It covers four skills: cross-border passive headhunting, multilingual sourcing, cross-country qualification equivalency assessment and diaspora targeting.

    The role matters because the best candidates for hard-to-fill roles are often abroad, not looking for a job and writing their profiles in another language. A good sourcer builds pipelines that are fast, lawful and fair. A weak one wastes money, breaks privacy rules or sends a candidate who cannot legally or professionally do the job.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures are illustrative and use US dollars. Privacy, employment and recognition rules differ by country and change, so confirm them with legal and immigration advisers before acting.

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Explain passive candidates and plan a cross-border outreach campaign.
    2. Calculate reply rate, funnel conversion, sourcing yield and the messages needed for a target.
    3. Prioritise leads with a simple score and respect privacy and non-solicitation limits.

    🎯 Fundamentals: What passive headhunting is and why it matters

    A passive candidate is a skilled person who is not actively applying for jobs but might move for the right role. Passive headhunting means finding and approaching these people directly, often in another country. It is used for senior, scarce or specialist roles where job adverts do not reach the best people.

    It matters because the strongest people are usually employed and busy. A careful, respectful approach builds trust and a long-term talent pool. A careless one harms the employer's reputation and may break privacy or contract rules.

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Sending one long copied message to everyone
    • Ignoring privacy rules when collecting profile data
    • Approaching people covered by a non-solicitation agreement
    Hands-on Practice250 messages bring 30 replies. What is the reply rate?
    30 ÷ 250 × 100 = 12 percent.

    🧭 Core Concepts: Channels and the sourcing funnel

    Passive candidates are found through professional networks, conferences and speaker lists, publications and patents, open-source projects and portfolios, alumni groups, referrals and competitor mapping. Each channel suits different roles.

    Think of the process as a funnel. Messages lead to replies, replies to calls, calls to interviews, interviews to offers and offers to hires. Measure each step so you can see where people drop out.

    ChannelBest forWatch-out
    Professional networksMost white-collar rolesMessage limits and privacy rules
    Conferences and speaker listsSenior and technical rolesSlow but high quality
    Publications and patentsResearch and engineeringContact data may be hard to find
    Open-source and portfoliosDevelopers and designersCheck licence and privacy
    Referrals and alumniTrust-based rolesNeeds a referral process
    formulaSourcing yield = Hires ÷ Messages sent × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: From 400 messages: 60 replies, 24 calls, 9 interviews, 3 offers and 2 hires. Reply rate is 15 percent. Overall yield = 2 ÷ 400 × 100 = 0.5 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Judging a channel on messages sent, not hires
    • Not tracking each funnel step
    • Using one channel for every role
    Hands-on Practice500 messages, 75 replies, 30 calls, 12 interviews and 4 hires. What is the sourcing yield?
    4 ÷ 500 × 100 = 0.8 percent.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running a cross-border campaign step by step

    Follow eight steps. Meet the hiring manager and agree the brief: skills, level, location, salary range and relocation support. Build the talent map by country and employer. Search and shortlist. Write short, personal messages with a clear reason for the approach. Send in a planned sequence of two or three touches. Screen replies by phone. Present the relocation and visa position early. Hand over to the recruiter and log everything.

    Respect privacy law. You normally need a lawful basis to store personal data, must tell people where you got it within the legal time (often one month under some rules), and must delete or stop on request. Confirm the exact rules with your legal team.

    StepOwnerOutput
    BriefSourcer and hiring managerAgreed role profile and range
    Talent mapSourcerTarget list by country and employer
    Outreach sequenceSourcerTwo or three personalised touches
    Screening callSourcerInterest, availability, mobility, pay
    Relocation and visa checkMobility teamFeasibility note

    formulaMessages needed = Target interviews ÷ (Reply rate × Reply-to-interview rate)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The target is 9 interviews. The reply rate is 15 percent and 15 percent of replies reach interview. Interviews per message = 0.15 × 0.15 = 0.0225. Messages needed = 9 ÷ 0.0225 = 400.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Not agreeing the salary range before outreach
    • No sequence of follow-ups
    • Not telling candidates how their data was found
    Hands-on PracticeThe target is 6 interviews, the reply rate is 12 percent and 25 percent of replies reach interview. How many messages are needed?
    0.12 × 0.25 = 0.03. 6 ÷ 0.03 = 200 messages.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Campaign KPIs and scenarios

    Track reply rate, cost per hire, time to fill, offer acceptance rate and source mix. Compare by country and by channel, and look at the quality of the people hired, such as retention after 12 months.

    Scenario: replies are good but offers are refused. Check the pay range, relocation help and visa timing first. These often matter more than the message.

    🧪

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Leaving staff time out of the cost
    • Looking at reply rate only
    • Not tracking why offers fail
    Hands-on PracticeA campaign costs $30,000 and produces 5 hires. What is the cost per hire?
    30,000 ÷ 5 = $6,000.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: lead scoring, law and ethics

    Experts score leads so effort goes where success is likely. A simple score multiplies skill match, likelihood to move and reachability, each from 1 to 5. They also respect limits: data protection laws, non-solicitation clauses, anti-discrimination rules and honest messaging about the role.

    They test messages against each other, keep a do-not-contact list, protect candidate data and avoid damaging the market. A reputation for respect brings referrals.

    RiskControl
    Privacy breachLawful basis, notice and deletion process
    Breaking a non-solicitCheck clauses before approaching
    DiscriminationSkills-based criteria only
    Misleading messageAccurate role and pay information
    Data lossSecure tracker with access control
    formulaLead score = Skill match × Likelihood to move × Reachability (each 1 to 5)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Candidate A scores 5 × 2 × 4 = 40. Candidate B scores 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. B is approached first because the chance of a move and of being reached is higher.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Chasing only the best skills match
    • No do-not-contact list
    • Saving personal data without a plan to delete it
    Hands-on PracticeCandidate C scores 3, 5 and 3. Candidate D scores 5, 3 and 2. Who comes first?
    C = 3 × 5 × 3 = 45. D = 5 × 3 × 2 = 30. C comes first.

    🌍 Fundamentals: What multilingual sourcing is and why it matters

    Multilingual sourcing means finding candidates by searching and writing in the languages and on the platforms they use. Many skilled people write their profiles in their own language, with local job titles, and some do not appear in English searches at all.

    It matters because a sourcer who searches only in English misses large parts of the market. Messages in the person's language also get more replies and show respect.

    TermMeaning
    Native keywordTerm used by local professionals for a skill or title
    LocalisationAdapting content to local language and custom, not just translating
    TransliterationWriting a word in another script
    Local job boardRegional site used for hiring
    CEFR levelA1 to C2 scale for language ability
    Language coverageShare of needed languages the team can search
    formulaLanguage coverage = Languages covered ÷ Languages needed × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: A project needs 8 languages and the team covers 5. Coverage = 5 ÷ 8 × 100 = 62.5 percent. The gap is three languages.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Assuming English is enough
    • Relying on a direct translation of titles
    • Not stating the language level needed for the job
    Hands-on PracticeA project needs 4 languages and the team covers 3. What is the coverage?
    3 ÷ 4 × 100 = 75 percent.

    🧩 Core Concepts: Markets, sources and language levels

    Markets differ in the platforms they use, how formal messages should be and how profiles are written. Some markets prefer regional professional networks or local job boards. Some use scripts such as Arabic, Cyrillic or Chinese characters.

    Language level matters for the role. A job that needs daily client contact needs a higher level than a job that uses the language rarely. Describe the level using a standard scale.

    Market featureEffect on sourcingAction
    Different scriptEnglish keywords miss profilesAdd native-script keywords
    Local networks and boardsProfiles are elsewhereAdd local platforms
    Formal addressWrong tone lowers repliesUse correct formal form
    Local job titlesDirect translation failsMap titles with a native speaker
    Spelling variantsSearch misses variantsList all variants
    formulaKeyword variants = Skill terms × Languages × Spellings
    🧪
    Practical Example: A search uses 4 skill terms, 3 languages and 2 spelling variants. Variants = 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 search terms.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using one spelling only
    • Skipping the local platform
    • Using informal tone with senior people
    Hands-on PracticeA search uses 5 skill terms, 4 languages and 2 spellings. How many variants are there?
    5 × 4 × 2 = 40.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Building and running a multilingual search

    Follow seven steps. Define the skills and levels for each market. Map job titles with a native speaker. Build the keyword list. Run searches on global and local platforms. Review profiles with someone who reads the language. Localise the outreach message, including form of address and tone. Test two message versions and log results.

    Machine translation helps with a first draft but must be checked by a fluent person. A wrong word can change the meaning of a job title or sound rude.

    StepOwnerOutput
    Market and skills definitionSourcerSkill and level list per market
    Title mappingSourcer and native speakerLocal title table
    Keyword listSourcerSearch terms per language
    Search and reviewSourcerShortlist with notes
    Localised outreachNative speakerApproved message text
    Testing and logSourcerReply data by version
    formulaTranslation accuracy = Correct terms ÷ Terms checked × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: A reviewer checks 40 translated terms and 36 are correct. Accuracy = 36 ÷ 40 × 100 = 90 percent. The four errors are fixed before searching.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Sending machine-translated messages unchecked
    • Using the home-market job title
    • No test of message versions
    Hands-on PracticeA reviewer checks 25 terms and 22 are correct. What is the accuracy?
    22 ÷ 25 × 100 = 88 percent.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: KPIs and scenarios by language market

    Track reply rate and qualified candidate rate by language, time to first shortlist and cost per market. Compare localised and unlocalised messages to see whether localisation pays.

    Scenario: replies in one market are low. Check tone, platform choice, timing and whether the keywords match local titles.

    🧪
    Practical Example: Before localisation the reply rate in one market was 6 percent. After it, the rate was 9 percent. Lift = (9 − 6) ÷ 6 × 100 = 50 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Measuring only the total across all languages
    • Changing many things at once
    • No baseline
    Hands-on PracticeThe old reply rate was 10 percent and the new rate is 13 percent. What is the lift?
    (13 − 10) ÷ 10 × 100 = 30 percent.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: culture, fairness and pipeline planning

    Experts handle regional dialects, formal and informal address, and cultural norms about cold messages. They avoid screening people by accent, name or nationality. They check that the language level in a job advert is a real requirement.

    They plan the pipeline by market. Expected qualified candidates in a market equal leads multiplied by reply rate and qualified rate. Summing markets shows whether the plan meets the target.

    RiskControl
    MistranslationNative speaker review
    Name or accent biasSkills-based criteria
    Wrong toneTone guide per market
    Privacy rules differLocal legal check
    Unchecked AI outputHuman review of all outreach
    formulaExpected qualified = Σ (Leads × Reply rate × Qualified rate)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Market A has 300 leads, a 10 percent reply rate and 40 percent qualified: 12. Market B has 200 leads, 15 percent and 50 percent: 15. Total = 27 qualified candidates.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Setting an unnecessary language requirement
    • Using one tone guide for all markets
    • No plan by market
    Hands-on PracticeMarket 1: 400 leads, 8 percent reply, 50 percent qualified. Market 2: 100 leads, 20 percent reply, 40 percent qualified. What is the total?
    16 + 8 = 24 qualified candidates.

    🎓 Fundamentals: What qualification equivalency is and why it matters

    Qualification equivalency assessment compares a qualification earned in one country with the standard of another. Employers and authorities need it to know whether a degree, diploma or certificate is real, at the right level and in the right field.

    It matters because job titles and degree names mean different things across countries. A wrong decision hires someone who cannot practise or rejects a strong candidate by mistake.

    TermMeaning
    Credential evaluationComparing a foreign qualification with a local standard
    Recognition bodyOrganisation that advises on foreign qualifications, for example national information centres in the ENIC-NARIC network
    AccreditationOfficial approval of an institution or programme
    TranscriptRecord of courses and grades
    Apostille or legalisationCertification of a document for use abroad
    CreditUnit of study workload
    formulaECTS credits ≈ US semester credits × 2 (typical conversion; confirm with the evaluator)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A degree of 120 US semester credits converts to about 240 ECTS credits, which fits a four-year bachelor programme.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Assuming the same degree name means the same level
    • Accepting copies without checking the issuer
    • Ignoring accreditation
    Hands-on PracticeA programme has 30 US semester credits. What is the approximate ECTS value?
    30 × 2 = 60 ECTS, about one year of full-time study.

    ⚖️ Core Concepts: Academic and professional recognition

    Academic recognition says a foreign degree is equal to a local one for study or general employment. Professional recognition is different: for regulated professions such as medicine, nursing, law, teaching and many engineering fields, a licence from a regulator is usually needed, and equivalency alone is not enough.

    Where a gap exists, the regulator may ask for exams, bridging courses or supervised practice. The sourcer must know which case applies before presenting a candidate.

    TypeDecided byResult
    Academic recognitionRecognition body or the employerLevel and field comparison
    Professional licenceRegulator or professional bodyRight to practise
    Partial recognitionRegulatorBridging course or exam
    No recognitionRegulatorRetrain or work in an unregulated role
    formulaDegree length gap = Required years − Candidate years
    🧪
    Practical Example: The local standard needs 4 years and the candidate studied for 3. Gap = 1 year, so a bridging year or an extra assessment may be needed.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Presenting a candidate for a regulated role before checking the licence
    • Not asking the regulator what is missing
    • Treating equivalency as a licence
    Hands-on PracticeThe local standard needs 5 years and the candidate studied for 4. What is the gap?
    5 − 4 = 1 year.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Assessing equivalency step by step

    Follow seven steps. Collect certificates and transcripts. Verify them with the issuing institution. Check that the institution is accredited. Compare level, field, duration and credits. Decide, using an evaluator or recognition body where needed. Record the reasoning. Tell the hiring manager and the candidate what is accepted and what is missing.

    A weighted score keeps the decision consistent. Choose weights for level, field, duration and accreditation and keep them the same for all candidates.

    StepOwnerOutput
    Collect documentsCoordinatorCertificates and transcripts
    Verify with the issuerCoordinatorVerification record
    Check accreditationSourcerAccreditation evidence
    Compare and scoreEvaluatorEquivalency score
    Decision and notesEvaluatorWritten outcome
    CommunicateSourcerClear message to manager and candidate
    formulaEquivalency score = (Level × 0.40) + (Field × 0.30) + (Duration × 0.20) + (Accreditation × 0.10)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A candidate scores Level 90, Field 80, Duration 70 and Accreditation 100. Score = 36 + 24 + 14 + 10 = 84.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Changing weights for a favoured candidate
    • Skipping issuer verification
    • No written reasoning
    Hands-on PracticeA candidate scores Level 80, Field 90, Duration 60 and Accreditation 100. What is the score?
    32 + 27 + 12 + 10 = 81.

    Track the verification pass rate, turnaround time, cost per evaluation, the share of candidates cleared and the rework rate. Study any failed verification for patterns, such as a particular provider or a forged document type.

    Scenario: a candidate's degree is from an institution that cannot be verified. Pause, ask the candidate for another route to verify and tell the hiring manager the status honestly.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: regulated fields, fraud and cost of error

    Experts handle partial equivalence, different grading scales, distance degrees, honorary titles, name changes and diploma mills. They know that a title such as engineer is protected in some countries. They work with regulators and keep decision records that can be audited.

    They also estimate the cost of mistakes. Expected rework cost equals cases multiplied by the failure probability and the cost per failure.

    RiskControl

    🧪

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using a single check for all countries
    • Keeping certificates in email
    • Not costing failures
    Hands-on PracticeThere are 120 cases, a 5 percent failure probability and $400 per failure. What is the expected cost?
    120 × 5% × 400 = $2,400.

    🌐 Fundamentals: What diaspora targeting is and why it matters

    A diaspora is a group of people who live outside their country of origin but keep ties to it. Diaspora targeting means reaching skilled professionals in these groups for roles in or linked to that country. Some want to return, some want to help from abroad and many bring skills, language and cultural knowledge.

    It matters because the motivation is different from ordinary job hunting. Family, identity and a wish to contribute can matter more than pay. Employers who understand this can build strong, loyal teams.

    🧭 Core Concepts: Segments and channels

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: ethics, law and return on investment

    Experts handle sensitive issues. Do not use protected characteristics such as ethnicity or religion as hiring criteria, even if the aim seems positive. Use skills, language, education and genuine links to the country. Consider the effect on the source country, especially in sectors such as health where international codes ask employers to recruit responsibly.

    Measure return on investment. Net benefit equals the value of the hire minus the total cost. Return on investment equals net benefit divided by cost.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Sourcers collect names, contact details, work histories and sometimes qualifications and nationality. Data protection laws in many countries limit how this data can be collected, used and kept, and give people rights to know, correct and delete it.

    Security here means having a lawful basis for processing, telling people how their data was found, protecting it, deleting it when asked or when no longer needed, and avoiding discrimination and scams. Confirm the rules for each country with your privacy team.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    No lawful basis or noticeBreach of privacy lawRecord the basis and send a notice with the source
    Candidate data leakHarm to people and reputationSecure tracker with access control
    Discriminatory screeningLegal claims and unfair outcomesSkills-based criteria only
    Fake candidates or fake employersFraud and wasted effortVerify identity and employer details
    Contacting restricted peopleBreaches of non-solicit termsDo-not-contact list checked before outreach
    Data kept too longBreaks retention rulesDeletion schedule and process

    🛡️ Security for: Cross-border passive headhunting

    • Check the do-not-contact list and any non-solicit limits before outreach.
    • Use only data that is lawful to collect and tell people where you found it.
    • Keep outreach records in a secure tracker, not personal chats.

    🛡️ Security for: Multilingual sourcing

    • Machine translation tools may send text to outside servers, so do not paste personal data into untrusted tools.
    • Do not screen by accent, name or language beyond the real job need.
    • Keep the keyword and message logs free of personal data.

    🛡️ Security for: Cross-country qualification equivalency assessment

    • Verify certificates with the issuer and never open unknown files from unverified senders.
    • Store certificates securely and limit access.
    • Watch for forged certificates and fake institutions.

    🛡️ Security for: Diaspora targeting

    • Do not use or infer protected characteristics such as ethnicity or religion for targeting.
    • Get consent before adding people to mailing lists.
    • Recruit responsibly in sectors where the source country has shortages.

    🧪 Measuring security

    formulaDeletion request closure rate = Requests closed on time ÷ Requests received × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: 30 deletion requests are received and 28 are closed within the legal time. Rate = 28 ÷ 30 × 100 = 93.3 percent. The two late cases are reviewed and a reminder is added to the process.

    Security checklist

    • A lawful basis and notice exist for every record.
    • A do-not-contact list is checked before outreach.
    • Screening uses skills only.
    • Candidate data is stored in a secure tracker.
    • Deletion requests are closed on time.
    • Certificates are verified with the issuer.

    Module 12

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Cross-border passive headhunting

    IQ1.What is a passive candidate?
    A skilled person who is not actively applying but may move for the right role. They are reached by direct, respectful outreach.
    IQ2.Why do you measure each step of the funnel?
    Each step shows a different problem. Low replies point to the message, low interviews to screening or fit, and low acceptance to the offer or relocation.
    IQ3.A manager wants you to contact people from a competitor who just left under a non-solicit agreement. What do you do?
    Stop and check the clause with legal. If the clause restricts the approach, do not contact. Offer other candidates instead.
    IQ4.Your reply rate falls from 15 to 8 percent. What do you check?
    Check the message, timing, subject line, target list quality and platform limits. Test a new version and compare results.
    IQ5.Target is 6 interviews, reply rate 12 percent, 25 percent of replies reach interview. How many messages?
    0.12 × 0.25 = 0.03. 6 ÷ 0.03 = 200 messages.

    💬 Interview Questions: Multilingual sourcing

    IQ1.Why search in local languages?
    Many professionals write profiles in their own language and platform. Native keywords find people English searches miss.
    IQ2.What is the difference between translation and localisation?
    Translation converts words. Localisation also adapts tone, form of address, examples and customs so the message feels natural.
    IQ3.A machine-translated message gets almost no replies. What do you do?
    Have a native speaker review it, fix wording and tone, then test the new version against the old.
    IQ4.How do you avoid bias in language screening?
    Set the real language level needed for the job, use a standard scale and judge skills, not accent or name.
    IQ5.Old reply rate 10 percent, new 13 percent. What is the lift?
    (13 − 10) ÷ 10 = 30 percent.

    💬 Interview Questions: Cross-country qualification equivalency assessment

    IQ1.Why do degree names mislead?
    The same name can mean a different level or length in different countries. Compare level, field, duration and accreditation.
    IQ2.What is the difference between equivalency and a licence?
    Equivalency compares a degree. A licence gives legal permission to practise a regulated profession and comes from a regulator.
    IQ3.A candidate's university cannot be verified. What do you do?
    Pause the case, ask the candidate for other routes to verify, tell the manager honestly and do not present an unverified qualification as confirmed.
    IQ4.A manager wants the weights changed to favour a candidate. How do you respond?
    Explain that fixed weights keep decisions fair. If the criteria need to change, change them for all candidates and record why.
    IQ5.30 US semester credits equal how many ECTS credits?
    About 60 ECTS, using the common factor of 2.

    💬 Interview Questions: Diaspora targeting

    IQ1.Why is diaspora hiring different from ordinary hiring?
    Motives often include family, identity and mission, not only pay. The offer and support must reflect that.
    IQ2.How do you target a diaspora lawfully?
    Use skills, language, education and links to the country, not protected characteristics, and use partners such as alumni groups.
    IQ3.A candidate accepts but the family cannot settle. What do you do?
    Review schooling, housing and spouse work options with the mobility team, offer support and check in at set times.
    IQ4.Is diaspora hiring always ethical?
    It can raise concerns where it drains scarce skills from a country. Follow responsible recruitment policies, especially in sectors such as health.
    IQ5.A campaign costs $21,000 and produces 70 qualified leads. What is the cost per lead?
    21,000 ÷ 70 = $300.
