    Course Overview

    Expatriate Tax & Shadow Payroll Consultant: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how an Expatriate Tax and Shadow Payroll Consultant keeps international assignees compliant and the employer's costs under control. It covers four skills: tax equalization schemes, double taxation treaty application, split payroll coordination and non-resident withholding compliance.

    The role matters because pay that crosses borders creates tax and social security duties in more than one country. Mistakes lead to penalties, double tax for the employee and unplanned cost for the company. Done well, employees are taxed fairly, filings are on time and the company knows its true cost.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. All rates, thresholds and figures are illustrative. Tax law, treaties and filing rules differ by country and change often, so this course teaches the method, not current law. Real cases need a qualified tax adviser and the current rules of each country.

    ⚖️ Fundamentals: What tax equalization is and why it matters

    Tax equalization is a scheme where the company makes sure an assignee pays about the same tax on their base pay as they would have paid at home. The employee bears a hypothetical tax, which is taken from pay. The company pays the real home and host taxes on the assignment pay. The result is that tax does not decide whether someone accepts or refuses an assignment.

    It matters because tax rates differ widely. Without a clear scheme, one assignee could lose money while another gains. Tax equalization is fair and predictable for the employee, but it moves tax risk to the company, so the company must model and control the cost.

    ApproachWho bears the tax differenceTypical use
    Tax equalizationCompany pays actual tax; employee pays hypothetical taxLong-term assignments
    Tax protectionEmployee pays at most home-country tax; keeps any benefit if host tax is lowerAssignments where employee choice matters
    Laissez-faireEmployee pays all taxShort or employee-led moves
    Ad hoc supportCase-by-case agreementSpecial cases
    formulaCompany tax cost = Actual home and host taxes − Hypothetical tax withheld
    🧪
    Practical Example: Actual home and host taxes on the assignment pay are $90,000. The hypothetical tax charged to the employee is $40,000. Company tax cost = 90,000 − 40,000 = $50,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Promising tax equalization without modelling the cost
    • Mixing up tax protection and tax equalization
    • Not writing down which income is covered
    Hands-on PracticeActual taxes are $75,000 and hypothetical tax is $35,000. What is the company tax cost?
    75,000 − 35,000 = $40,000.

    🧮 Core Concepts: Key terms and the hypothetical tax calculation

    Hypothetical tax is an estimate of the tax the employee would pay at home on their stay-at-home income. Stay-at-home income usually includes base salary and regular bonus and excludes assignment allowances. The calculation uses the home tax rules, the family status and a clear list of included items, all set in the policy.

    The hypothetical tax is withheld from pay across the year. At year end it is recalculated on the real stay-at-home income and a settlement is made. Policies also say how personal income such as rent or investments is treated, because that income usually stays the employee's own tax matter.

    TermMeaning
    Stay-at-home incomePay the employee would have earned at home
    Hypothetical taxEstimated home-style tax charged to the employee
    Assignment allowancesExtra pay for the assignment, paid and taxed by the company
    SettlementYear-end true-up between company and employee
    Tax returnFiling in home and host country
    Tax adviserQualified person who prepares returns and estimates
    formulaHypothetical tax = Stay-at-home income × Effective hypothetical rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: Stay-at-home income is $120,000 and the effective hypothetical rate is 22 percent. Hypothetical tax = 120,000 × 22% = $26,400 for the year.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Including assignment allowances in stay-at-home income
    • Using a marginal rate where the policy says effective rate
    • Not updating the rate when family status changes
    Hands-on PracticeStay-at-home income is $150,000 and the effective rate is 20 percent. What is the hypothetical tax?
    150,000 × 20% = $30,000.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running the scheme step by step

    Follow a yearly cycle. Confirm the policy and eligibility. Estimate the hypothetical tax and set the monthly withholding. Withhold from pay. Prepare and file the home and host tax returns through the tax adviser. Calculate the year-end settlement on actual stay-at-home income. Pay or collect the difference. Close the year with a summary for the employee and for finance.

    Keep communication simple. Send the employee a clear statement showing income, hypothetical tax, taxes paid by the company and the settlement.

    Estimate and withholdPayrollMonthly hypothetical tax deduction
    ReturnsTax adviserHome and host returns filed
    SettlementTax and payrollEmployee statement and payment
    Year-end summaryTax and financeTotal company tax cost
    formulaEmployee settlement = Final hypothetical tax − Hypothetical tax withheld
    🧪
    Practical Example: Final hypothetical tax is $30,000 and $26,400 was withheld. Settlement = 30,000 − 26,400 = $3,600 owed by the employee.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Waiting for the employee to ask about the settlement
    • Filing the host return before data is confirmed
    • No summary for finance
    Hands-on PracticeFinal hypothetical tax is $28,000 and $31,000 was withheld. What is the settlement?
    28,000 − 31,000 = −3,000, so the company owes the employee $3,000.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Cost modeling, gross-up and KPIs

    Some assignment pay is taxable but the company wants the employee to receive it net. The company then pays the tax too, which is the gross-up. The formula is net amount ÷ (1 − combined marginal rate). A combined rate includes the taxes on the extra income, such as income tax and employee social contributions.

    Track the tax cost ratio, which shows how much tax the company pays compared with gross pay. Also track estimate accuracy, on-time returns and settlement timeliness.

    KPIFormulaUse
    Tax cost ratioCompany-paid tax ÷ Gross assignment payCompare cost across assignments
    Estimate accuracy1 − |Actual − Estimate| ÷ EstimateCheck forecast quality
    On-time returnsReturns filed on time ÷ Returns dueCompliance
    Settlement timelinessSettled by target date ÷ SettlementsEmployee experience
    formulaTax cost ratio = Company-paid tax ÷ Gross pay × 100   |   Gross-up = Net ÷ (1 − Marginal rate)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Company-paid tax is $50,000 on gross pay of $200,000: 25 percent. A $5,000 net allowance with a combined marginal rate of 45 percent costs 5,000 ÷ 0.55 = $9,091 to deliver.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Multiplying the net amount by (1 + rate) instead of dividing by (1 − rate)
    • Using one rate for every country
    • Forgetting the gross-up in the budget
    Hands-on PracticeCompany-paid tax is $36,000 on gross pay of $180,000. What is the tax cost ratio?
    36,000 ÷ 180,000 = 20 percent.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: equity pay, part-year cases and risk

    Experts handle complex items. Equity awards that vest after work in several countries are usually shared between countries by days worked in each country during the vesting period, though local rules differ. Part-year residence in the arrival and departure years, bonuses paid after the assignment ends and state or local taxes also need careful rules.

    Control risk with policy caps, regular cost reports, adviser reviews and records that support every return. A good consultant also warns leaders when a tax change would raise assignment cost.

    CaseIssueControl

    formulaApportioned award = Total award × (Days worked in host during vesting ÷ Total vesting days)
    🧪
    Practical Example: An award of $90,000 vests over 730 days. The employee worked 365 days in the host country. Share sourced to the host = 90,000 × 365 ÷ 730 = $45,000. Rules vary, so the adviser confirms the method.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using the vesting date only
    • Ignoring days worked in a third country
    • No record of days
    Hands-on PracticeAn award of $120,000 vests over 1,095 days with 365 days in the host. What is the host share?
    120,000 × 365 ÷ 1,095 = $40,000.

    🌐 Fundamentals: What a double taxation treaty does and key terms

    Without relief, the same income can be taxed twice: once by the country where it is earned and again by the country where the person lives. A double taxation treaty is an agreement between two countries that decides who may tax which income, reduces some withholding rates and sets a way to give relief.

    Treaties matter for assignees because pay for work done abroad may be taxable in both countries. The treaty tells you which country has the first right and how the other gives credit or an exemption. The details of each treaty differ, so always read the actual text.

    TermMeaning
    ResidenceCountry where the person is treated as living for tax
    SourceCountry where income arises
    Credit methodHome country allows a credit for tax paid abroad
    Exemption methodHome country does not tax income taxed abroad
    Permanent establishmentFixed place or agent giving a company a taxable presence
    Competent authorityTax authority that resolves treaty disputes
    formulaCombined tax without relief = Home tax + Host tax
    🧪
    Practical Example: An employee earns $100,000. Home tax is $30,000 and host tax is $25,000. Without relief the combined tax is 30,000 + 25,000 = $55,000, which is 55 percent of the income.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Assuming a treaty exists between any two countries
    • Using the wrong version of a treaty
    • Forgetting that domestic law applies first
    Hands-on PracticeHome tax is $20,000 and host tax is $18,000. What is the combined tax without relief?
    20,000 + 18,000 = $38,000.

    📜 Core Concepts: Treaty articles used for employees and the 183-day rule

    Most treaties follow a common model. The residence article decides where a person is resident if two countries claim them. The employment article usually says pay is taxed where the person lives, unless the work is done in the other country. A short-stay exception can leave the pay taxable only in the home country when three conditions are all met. The elimination article sets the credit or exemption. Other articles cover non-discrimination and a mutual agreement procedure for disputes.

    The usual short-stay conditions are: the person is present in the other country for no more than 183 days in the stated period, the pay is not paid by or for an employer resident there, and it is not borne by a permanent establishment there. The stated period varies, for example a rolling twelve months or the tax year, so read the treaty.

    Article themeWhat it does
    ResidenceDecides which country treats the person as resident, with tie-breaker rules
    Employment incomeAllocates taxing rights, includes the short-stay exception
    Elimination of double taxationCredit or exemption relief
    Non-discriminationStops less favourable treatment based on nationality
    Mutual agreement procedureLets authorities settle disputes
    formulaShort-stay exemption applies if: days in host ≤ 183 AND employer not resident in host AND cost not borne by a host PE
    🧪
    Practical Example: An employee spends 150 days in the host country, is paid by a home employer and the cost is not borne by a host permanent establishment. All three conditions are met, so the pay is taxed only at home under this treaty rule.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Counting only full days when the treaty counts part days
    • Using the wrong measurement period
    • Ignoring the employer test
    Hands-on PracticeAn employee spends 190 days in the host country with the other conditions met. Does the short-stay exemption apply?
    No. 190 days is more than 183, so the exemption fails and the host country may tax the pay for work done there.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Applying a treaty step by step

    Work in order. Decide residence in each country under its own law. If both treat the person as resident, apply the tie-breaker order: permanent home available, centre of vital interests, habitual abode, nationality, then mutual agreement. Identify the type of income. Find the right article. Decide whether relief is by credit or exemption. Collect the proof, such as a residence certificate. Calculate relief and file the claim.

    For the credit method, the home country usually limits the credit to the home tax that relates to the foreign income. So the credit is the lower of the foreign tax paid and that share of home tax.

    StepOutput
    Residence in each countryPosition statement for each country
    Tie-breaker if dual residentTreaty residence decided
    Income classificationArticle to apply
    Relief methodCredit or exemption
    ProofResidence certificate, payslips, tax receipts
    ClaimReturn and treaty claim filed
    formulaCredit limit = Home tax × (Foreign income ÷ Worldwide income); Credit = lower of foreign tax paid and credit limit
    🧪
    Practical Example: Worldwide income is $150,000, foreign income $100,000 and home tax on worldwide income $45,000. Limit = 45,000 × 100 ÷ 150 = $30,000. Foreign tax paid is $25,000, so credit = $25,000. Net home tax = 45,000 − 25,000 = $20,000. Total tax = 25,000 + 20,000 = $45,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Giving a credit for the full foreign tax with no limit
    • Skipping the tie-breaker
    • No proof of foreign tax paid
    Hands-on PracticeWorldwide income $200,000, foreign income $80,000, home tax $60,000 and foreign tax paid $30,000. What is the credit?
    Limit = 60,000 × 80 ÷ 200 = $24,000. Credit = $24,000, because it is lower than $30,000. The excess $6,000 is not creditable.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Treaty KPIs and scenarios

    Track how many treaty claims are accepted, how fast residence certificates arrive, how much tax relief is obtained compared with the tax at risk and how many claims are queried. Review reasons for rejected claims every quarter.

    Scenario: an employee works four months in another country for a home employer. Check days, employer and permanent establishment risk first, then decide if the short-stay exemption applies. If not, plan the credit.

    🧪
    Practical Example: Tax at risk across the year is $500,000 and relief obtained is $440,000, so realisation is 88 percent. Of 45 claims filed, 40 were accepted: 88.9 percent. Both are below 90 percent, so rejected claims are reviewed.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Counting claims filed rather than relief obtained
    • No review of rejections
    • Waiting for the tax return before requesting the certificate
    Hands-on PracticeTax at risk is $300,000 and relief obtained is $270,000. What is the realisation rate?
    270,000 ÷ 300,000 = 90 percent.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: disputes, anti-abuse and exposure

    Experts handle dual-resident individuals, conflicting day counts, permanent establishment risk created by the employee's work, and treaty benefits denied under anti-abuse rules such as a principal purpose test. They also manage mutual agreement procedures, which can take a long time, and interactions with social security agreements.

    Value the risk. Tax at risk multiplied by the chance of denial gives an expected exposure that supports a decision on whether to seek an advance ruling, file a protective claim or reserve cost.

    • Treating all risk as zero
    • Not keeping travel records
    • Ignoring slow dispute timelines
    Hands-on PracticeTax at risk is $400,000 and the chance of denial is 10 percent. What is the expected exposure?

    🧾 Fundamentals: What split and shadow payroll are

    Split payroll means paying an assignee in two places, for example part of the pay through the home payroll and part through the host payroll. Companies do this so the person receives local currency for local costs and to meet local rules. A shadow payroll is a payroll run in the host country that records pay made from elsewhere, so the right host tax and social security can be calculated, reported and paid.

    It matters because the assignee must be taxed correctly in each country and the company needs one clear view of cost. A wrong split, late data or a missing filing can lead to penalties and unhappy employees.

    ModelHow it worksTypical use
    Split payrollPay is divided between home and host payrollsLong assignments with local costs
    Shadow payrollHost payroll records pay made abroad and calculates taxPay stays at home but host tax applies
    Home payroll onlyNo host payrollShort trips below local thresholds
    Host payroll onlyEmployee fully on local payrollLocalised employees
    formulaHost pay = Total pay × Host share percent
    🧪
    Practical Example: Monthly gross pay is $10,000 and the host share is 40 percent. Host pay = $4,000 and home pay = $6,000.
    ⚠️

    • Changing the split without notifying payroll in both countries
    • Not recording who owns each payroll
    Hands-on PracticeMonthly gross pay is $12,000 and the host share is 35 percent. What is the host pay?
    12,000 × 35% = $4,200.

    🗃️ Core Concepts: Roles, data and currency

    Several teams work together: HR holds the assignment details, payroll runs the calculations, tax advises on rates and finance funds the payments. Good data is the base: gross pay, allowances, bonus dates, assignment dates, tax codes, social security status and any certificate that confirms which country's social security applies.

    Pay may be in different currencies. Agree the source of the exchange rate and the date it applies, and keep it the same each month.

    Data itemSourceRisk if wrong
    Gross pay and allowancesHR systemWrong tax and net pay
    Start and end datesAssignment recordWrong period or penalties
    Tax codes and IDsTax adviser and employeeRejected filing

    Exchange rateAgreed sourceReconciliation gaps
    formulaConverted amount = Amount × Exchange rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: A host pay of €3,000 at a rate of 1 euro = 1.10 dollars is 3,000 × 1.10 = $3,300.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using a different exchange rate in each payroll
    • Missing the effective date of a change
    • Missing social security proof
    Hands-on PracticeA pay of £2,000 at a rate of 1 pound = 1.25 dollars equals how many dollars?
    2,000 × 1.25 = $2,500.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Setting up and running the payroll cycle

    Set up in order. Confirm tax and social security status in both countries. Register the employer in the host country if the rules require it. Agree the split and the payroll calendar. Collect data each month by a cut-off date. Calculate net pay and employer cost. Fund the payments. File returns and pay taxes by their deadlines. Reconcile both payrolls and keep a record.

    A clear calendar with cut-off dates for each country prevents most late changes.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Payroll KPIs and reconciliation

    Track payroll accuracy (assignee runs with no error), on-time filing, reconciliation difference and number of late changes. Reconcile by comparing total gross pay on the home report with the total on the host payroll. Explain every difference.

    Scenario: the host payroll total is lower than the home report. Possible causes are a missing allowance, an old exchange rate or a start date mismatch. Fix the cause and correct the record.

    Non-resident withholding compliance

    Getting tax right when paying people who live abroad.

    💼 Fundamentals: What non-resident withholding is

    When a company pays certain income to a non-resident, many countries require the payer to withhold tax from the payment and send it to the tax authority. The payer is the withholding agent. The rate may be the domestic statutory rate or a lower treaty rate if the payee proves entitlement.

    It matters because the payer is responsible if tax is not withheld. Errors cause penalties and interest. Typical payments include fees for services, director fees, some employment income of non-residents, royalties and dividends.

    TermMeaning
    Withholding agentPayer that must withhold and remit
    Non-residentPerson not tax resident in the paying country
    Statutory rateRate in domestic law
    Treaty rateLower rate allowed by a treaty
    RemittancePayment of withheld tax to the authority
    Withholding certificateProof given to the payee
    formulaWithholding = Gross payment × Withholding rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: A company pays a non-resident $50,000 and the rate is 20 percent. Withholding = $10,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Paying the full amount and planning to fix it later
    • Not checking residence
    • Using a treaty rate without proof
    Hands-on PracticeA payment of $80,000 has a 15 percent withholding rate. What is the withholding?
    80,000 × 15% = $12,000.

    📂 Core Concepts: Payment types, documents and net or gross terms

    Different payments have different treatment. Check the payment type, the payee's residence, the statutory rate and the treaty. Collect documents before paying, such as a tax residence certificate and a beneficial ownership or treaty claim form. Many systems use specific forms, for example a W-8 type form in the United States.

    Contracts sometimes say the payee must receive a fixed net amount. Then the payer must gross up. The gross amount is net divided by one minus the rate.

    PaymentTypical treatmentDocument
    Services performed abroadOften not taxed at source without a presenceContract and proof of place of work
    Services performed in the paying countryOften subject to withholdingContract and residence certificate
    Director feesOften withheld at statutory rateBoard record
    RoyaltiesStatutory or treaty rateTreaty claim form
    DividendsStatutory or treaty rateBeneficial owner declaration
    formulaNet payment = Gross − Withholding   |   Gross = Net ÷ (1 − Rate)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The contract guarantees a net of $40,000 and the rate is 20 percent. Gross = 40,000 ÷ 0.80 = $50,000. Withholding is $10,000 and the payee receives $40,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Multiplying the net by (1 + rate)
    • Paying before collecting documents
    • Not filing the certificate with the payee
    Hands-on PracticeThe net payment is $68,000 and the rate is 15 percent. What gross payment is needed?
    68,000 ÷ 0.85 = $80,000.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Withholding process step by step

    Use ten steps. Classify the payment. Decide residence. Find the statutory rate. Check the treaty. Collect documents. Withhold at the correct rate. Remit by the deadline. Issue the certificate to the payee. File the return. Keep records.

    A pre-payment checklist stops most errors. Nothing is paid until the checklist is complete.

    Document collectionProcurement and payeeCertificate and form

    Certificate and recordsTaxCertificate sent, file complete
    formulaTreaty relief = (Statutory rate − Treaty rate) × Gross payment
    🧪
    Practical Example: A gross payment of $80,000 has a statutory rate of 25 percent and a treaty rate of 10 percent. Relief = (25% − 10%) × 80,000 = $12,000 less withheld.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • No rate reference saved
    • Applying a treaty rate without a valid certificate
    • Missing the remittance date
    Hands-on PracticeA gross payment of $60,000 has a statutory rate of 30 percent and a treaty rate of 15 percent. What is the relief?
    (30% − 15%) × 60,000 = $9,000.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Compliance KPIs and late-payment cost

    Track the on-time remittance rate, the share of payments with valid documents before payment, certificates issued on time and penalty and interest paid. Review exceptions monthly.

    Late remittance often adds interest or a penalty. A simple estimate is unpaid tax multiplied by the monthly rate and the months late.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: edge cases and exposure

    Module 11

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Expatriate tax and shadow payroll work uses salary, bank details, tax identification numbers and family information. It also moves money between countries. This makes it a target for fraud and a high-risk area for privacy breaches and tax penalties.

    Security here means limiting who sees pay data, separating duties so one person cannot create and approve a payment, checking bank detail changes, and keeping records that will stand up to a tax audit. Confirm local rules with tax and legal advisers.

    🛡️ Security for: Tax equalization schemes

    • Keep equalization calculations in a restricted workbook with locked formulas.
    • Share the hypothetical tax figures only with the assignee and approved advisers.
    • Record who approved each policy exception.

    🛡️ Security for: Double taxation treaty application

    • Store treaty claims and residence certificates securely.
    • Keep evidence of the rate and article relied on for each treaty claim.
    • Check the rules on holding and transferring foreign tax data.

    🛡️ Security for: Split payroll coordination

    • Use two people for payroll set-up and approval.
    • Verify any change of bank details by calling a known contact.
    • Reconcile the shadow payroll to the main payroll every period.

    🛡️ Security for: Non-resident withholding compliance

    • Withholding returns go to the authorities on time through official channels only.
    • Keep proof of payment and filing receipts.
    • Watch for fake tax authority messages asking for payment.

    🧪 Measuring security

    formulaDual approval compliance = Payments with two approvals ÷ Payments tested × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: A test of 50 payments finds 48 with two approvals. Compliance = 48 ÷ 50 × 100 = 96 percent. The two exceptions are investigated and the system is set to block single-approval payments.

    Security checklist

    • Payroll files are encrypted and access-limited.
    • Payments need two approvals.
    • Bank detail changes are verified by call-back.
    • Rate tables are locked and changes logged.
    • Tax records are kept for the audit period.
    • Staff are trained to spot fake tax authority messages.

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Tax equalization schemes

    IQ1.What is the difference between tax equalization and tax protection?
    Under tax equalization the employee bears a hypothetical tax and the company pays the actual taxes, so the employee is neither better nor worse off than at home. Under tax protection the employee pays at most the home-country tax and keeps any gain if the host tax is lower.
    IQ2.What is stay-at-home income and why does it matter?
    It is the pay the employee would have earned at home, excluding assignment allowances. It sets the hypothetical tax, so a wrong definition changes the employee's cost and the company's cost.
    IQ3.An employee questions a settlement bill. What do you do?
    Walk through the statement line by line: income, hypothetical tax, amount withheld and balance. Check the inputs for errors and correct any. If the policy is unclear, update it so the next statement is clearer.
    IQ4.The tax cost ratio has risen from 25 to 35 percent. What do you check?
    Look for new allowances, higher host rates, missing gross-up, equity awards or bonuses, and exchange-rate effects. Update the forecast and report the drivers to finance.
    IQ5.A net allowance of $6,000 is taxed at a combined 40 percent. What does it cost?
    6,000 ÷ (1 − 0.40) = $10,000.

    💬 Interview Questions: Double taxation treaty application

    IQ1.What problem does a double taxation treaty solve?
    It stops the same income being fully taxed in two countries by allocating taxing rights, reducing some withholding rates and giving relief through credit or exemption.
    IQ2.What are the usual conditions of the 183-day rule?
    The person is present in the other country no more than 183 days in the stated period, the pay is not paid by or for an employer resident there, and it is not borne by a permanent establishment there. All three must be met and the treaty text sets the period.
    IQ3.A manager says an employee only works 'a few months' in another country. What do you check?
    Count the days against the treaty period, confirm who pays and whether a permanent establishment bears the cost, check domestic law and social security. Document the position.
    IQ4.A treaty claim is rejected. What do you do?
    Read the reason, correct missing proof or facts, and re-file or appeal within the time limit. Record the cause so the checklist improves.
    IQ5.Worldwide income $200,000, foreign income $80,000, home tax $60,000 and foreign tax $30,000. What is the credit?
    Limit = 60,000 × 80 ÷ 200 = $24,000. Credit = $24,000.

    💬 Interview Questions: Split payroll coordination

    IQ1.What is a shadow payroll?
    A payroll run in the host country that records pay made from elsewhere so host tax and social security can be calculated, reported and paid.
    IQ2.Why agree one exchange rate source?
    Different rates in different payrolls create reconciliation gaps and disputes. One source and date rule keeps the totals comparable.
    IQ3.An assignee changes their split mid-year. What do you do?
    Get a written change with an effective date, update both payrolls before the cut-off, check tax and social security effects and note the change in the audit file.
    IQ4.Home and host payroll totals differ by 3 percent. How do you respond?
    Compare by assignee, check exchange rates, allowances and dates, correct the cause and document it. Report the difference and the fix.
    IQ5.Annual pay is $90,000 and the employee spent 73 days in the host. What is the host share?
    90,000 × 73 ÷ 365 = $18,000.

    💬 Interview Questions: Non-resident withholding compliance

    IQ1.What is a withholding agent?
    The payer that must withhold tax from a payment to a non-resident and send it to the authority. It is responsible if tax is not withheld.
    IQ2.Why collect a residence certificate before paying?
    A treaty rate needs proof of residence and entitlement. Without it the payer should withhold at the statutory rate.
    IQ3.A supplier asks for the full amount because it is 'covered by a treaty'. What do you do?
    Ask for the certificate and treaty claim form, check the payment type and treaty text, and apply the right rate. If proof is missing, withhold at the statutory rate and explain.
    IQ4.You find that tax was not withheld on a payment last year. What do you do?
    Calculate the unwithheld tax, interest and penalty, inform the tax adviser and decide on voluntary disclosure. Fix the checklist.
    IQ5.The net payment is $68,000 and the rate is 15 percent. What is the gross payment?
    68,000 ÷ 0.85 = $80,000.

    Course Summary

    📚 Course Summary: what you covered

    Tax equalization schemes

    • The company pays actual tax and the employee bears hypothetical tax.
    • Hypothetical tax uses stay-at-home income and is settled at year end.
    • Gross-up is net ÷ (1 − marginal rate).
    • Equity pay is shared by days worked, subject to local rules.

    Double taxation treaty application

    • Treaties allocate taxing rights and give relief.
    • Apply the tie-breaker in order and test the short-stay conditions.
    • Limit the credit to home tax on the foreign income.
    • Value risk as tax at risk times probability.

    Split payroll coordination

    • Split and shadow payrolls serve different needs.
    • Use cut-offs, one rate source and monthly reconciliation.
    • Check social security certificates.
    • Protect payroll data.

    Non-resident withholding compliance

    • Classify, check residence and rate, collect proof, withhold and remit.
    • Gross = net ÷ (1 − rate).
    • Treaty relief = rate gap × gross payment.
    • Exposure = tax + interest + penalty.
