    Module 01

    Course Overview

    International Compliance Officer: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how an International Compliance Officer keeps a company lawful when people work across borders. It covers three skills: cross-border permanent establishment risk monitoring, Posted Workers Directive compliance and cross-border labor contracts.

    The role matters because employees who travel or work abroad can create tax, social security and employment duties for the company in other countries. A good officer finds these risks early, keeps records and fixes gaps before a regulator does. A weak one leaves the company open to tax bills, fines and claims.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. This course is training material, not legal advice. Thresholds, rates and fees are illustrative, and laws change, so confirm current rules with qualified tax and employment advisers in each country.

    🏢 Fundamentals: What a permanent establishment is and why it matters

    A permanent establishment (PE) is a taxable presence of a company in another country. Under common tax treaty models it can arise from a fixed place of business, such as an office, or from a person who regularly concludes contracts for the company there. When a PE exists, the host country may tax the profit linked to it.

    It matters because a company can create a PE without meaning to, for example when a manager works from a home office abroad for months. Monitoring is the way to see the risk before it becomes a tax bill.

    TermMeaning
    Permanent establishmentTaxable presence in another country
    Fixed place of businessOffice, branch, factory or similar place
    Dependent agentPerson who habitually concludes contracts for the company
    TreatyAgreement between two countries on taxing rights
    Attributable profitProfit linked to the PE
    Internal alert limitCompany's own day limit, set below the legal risk level
    formulaLimit used = Days in country ÷ Internal alert limit × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: An employee has spent 120 days in a country and the internal alert limit is 180 days. Limit used = 120 ÷ 180 × 100 = 66.7 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Thinking days abroad are the only risk
    • Having no internal limit
    • Ignoring home-office work abroad
    Hands-on PracticeAn employee has spent 90 days in a country and the internal limit is 180. What percent is used?
    90 ÷ 180 × 100 = 50 percent.

    🔎 Core Concepts: Triggers and the days remaining

    Common triggers are a fixed place of business, a dependent agent with authority to conclude contracts, a long construction or installation project (treaty models often use more than 12 months), and in some countries services performed for a set number of days. Rules differ, so every country needs its own check.

    Days remaining shows how much room is left before the internal limit. When few days are left, the officer acts: reduce travel, change the work, or take advice on registering.

    TriggerExampleAction
    Fixed placeRented office or deskReview lease and use
    Dependent agentSalesperson signing contractsLimit authority or take advice
    Long projectSite work over 12 monthsTrack start and end dates
    Service daysConsultants on siteCount days by country
    Home office abroadRemote work from another countryReview policy and approve
    formulaDays remaining = Internal limit − Days used
    🧪
    Practical Example: The limit is 183 days and 140 have been used. Days remaining = 183 − 140 = 43.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Counting calendar months, not days
    • Not tracking home-office work
    • Forgetting that rules differ by country
    Hands-on PracticeThe limit is 180 and 125 days are used. How many days remain?
    180 − 125 = 55 days.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Setting up PE monitoring step by step

    Follow seven steps. Build a list of everyone who works abroad: employees, contractors and managers. Collect travel and work-location data from travel systems and declarations. Count days by person and country. Add up person-days by country for a team view. Compare with limits and flag cases. Review flagged cases with tax advisers. Record decisions and report to management.

    Person-days show the total presence of a team in a country, which matters when several people work there at once.

    Data collectionTravel and HRDays by person and country
    Day countComplianceMonthly report
    FlaggingComplianceList above 75 percent of limit
    ReviewTax adviserWritten view
    Decision recordComplianceAudit file
    formulaPerson-days = Number of people × Average days each
    🧪
    Practical Example: Five employees each spend an average of 40 days in a country. Person-days = 5 × 40 = 200.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Relying on self-reporting only
    • Counting only employees, not contractors
    • Not recording decisions
    Hands-on PracticeEight people each spend an average of 30 days. How many person-days is that?
    8 × 30 = 240 person-days.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Monitoring KPIs and scenarios

    Track coverage of travellers, the number of cases above the alert level, the time to review a flagged case, the number of registrations and the cost of advice. Compare countries to find hot spots.

    Scenario: a manager works from a home office in another country for seven months and signs customer contracts. The officer treats this as high risk, stops signing authority there, asks for tax advice and records the decision.

    formulaMonitoring coverage = Travellers tracked ÷ Travellers × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 200 travellers, 188 are tracked. Coverage = 188 ÷ 200 × 100 = 94 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes

    • No action on flags
    • Not including contractors
    Hands-on PracticeOf 300 travellers, 285 are tracked. What is the coverage?
    285 ÷ 300 × 100 = 95 percent.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: exposure, treaties and records

    Experts estimate the tax exposure if a PE exists: the profit attributable to it multiplied by the local tax rate, plus interest and penalties. They read tax treaties, which can narrow or widen PE rules, and they keep clear records of where people worked and what they did.

    They also advise on fixes: change duties, limit authority to sign, register a branch or entity, or end the activity. Any decision is taken with tax advisers.

    RiskControl
    Unregistered PEReview and register if advised
    Wrong profit allocationTransfer pricing review
    Missing recordsTravel and work logs kept for the audit period
    Treaty not appliedAdviser reads the treaty
    Late actionAlert level at 75 percent of limit
    formulaTax exposure = Attributable profit × Local tax rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: Attributable profit is $800,000 and the local rate is 25 percent. Exposure = 800,000 × 0.25 = $200,000, before interest and penalties.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Estimating without taking advice
    • Forgetting interest and penalties
    • Ignoring the treaty
    Hands-on PracticeAttributable profit is $500,000 and the rate is 20 percent. What is the exposure?
    500,000 × 0.20 = $100,000.

    🇪🇺 Fundamentals: What the Posted Workers rules are and why they matter

    A posted worker is an employee sent by an employer in one EU or EEA country to work temporarily in another, to provide services. The Posting of Workers Directive (96/71/EC, amended by Directive 2018/957/EU) sets minimum host-country terms for such workers, so that posted workers are not paid or treated worse than local workers.

    It matters because posting companies must follow host-country rules on pay, working time and safety, register the posting and keep documents. Fines and bans can follow breaches. Always confirm current national rules with an adviser.

    TermMeaning
    Posted workerEmployee sent temporarily to work in another country
    Host countryCountry where the work is done
    Home countryCountry of the posting employer
    A1 certificateDocument showing which country's social security applies
    Posting declarationNotice to host authorities before work starts
    RemunerationPay elements required by host-country rules
    formulaMonths remaining = 12 − Months already posted (before any extension)
    🧪
    Practical Example: A worker has been posted for 7 months. Months remaining before the 12-month point = 12 − 7 = 5. A reasoned notification can extend the period to 18 months.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Treating posting like normal business travel
    • Not registering the posting
    • Forgetting social security paperwork
    Hands-on PracticeA worker has been posted for 9 months. How many months remain before the 12-month point?
    12 − 9 = 3 months.

    💶 Core Concepts: Host-country terms and the pay gap

    After the 2018 change, posted workers are entitled to the host country's remuneration rules, not only the minimum wage. These cover pay set by law or by widely applicable collective agreements, working time, paid leave, health and safety, and conditions for workers' accommodation. Allowances for travel and lodging costs of the posting are treated differently from pay.

    To check pay, compare what the worker receives with what the host-country rules require. A gap means the employer must raise the pay for the posting.

    TermMeaningCheck
    Host pay levelRequired remuneration in the host countryObtain from official source
    Posted payWhat the worker is paid nowPayroll
    Working timeHours and restCompare to host rules
    Paid leaveAnnual leave daysCompare to host rules
    AccommodationHousing provided by employerCheck host standards
    formulaPay gap = Host-required pay − Posted worker's pay
    🧪
    Practical Example: Host rules require €3,400 a month and the worker is paid €3,000. Gap = 3,400 − 3,000 = €400 a month.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Comparing with the home pay only
    • Counting travel allowances as pay
    • Using an old collective agreement
    Hands-on PracticeHost rules require €2,900 a month and the worker is paid €2,600. What is the gap?
    2,900 − 2,600 = €300 a month.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Posting a worker step by step

    Follow seven steps. Confirm the work is a real posting and the employer has real activity in the home country. Check the host country's rules, declaration system and required documents. Apply for the A1 certificate. Submit the posting declaration before work starts. Check the pay and conditions against host rules. Keep documents available, often in the host language, and name a contact person. Review the end date and the 12 or 18 month limit.

    Declarations may be needed for each worker or each project. Plan the workload and cost so none are missed.

    formulaDeclaration workload = Declarations × Time or fee per declaration
    🧪

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Filing after work has started
    • Missing the host-language documents
    • Not naming a contact person
    Hands-on PracticeThere are 20 declarations at an illustrative €40 each. What is the cost?

    Scenario: an inspector asks for documents on site and the file is in another country. The officer keeps a digital file that can be shown at once and trains site managers.

    Common mistakes

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: long postings, chains and exposure

    Experts watch postings that pass 12 months, or 18 with extension, because more host-country employment terms then apply. They also check subcontracting chains, where the main contractor may share liability for subcontractor breaches, and the special rules for sectors such as road transport.

    They estimate exposure from underpayment: the number of workers, the monthly gap and the months affected. They fix the pay, pay arrears and record the correction.

    formulaUnderpayment exposure = Workers × Monthly gap × Months
    🧪
    Practical Example: Ten workers have a gap of €400 a month for 6 months. Exposure = 10 × 400 × 6 = €24,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Ignoring the subcontractor
    • Waiting for an inspection
    • Not paying arrears
    Hands-on PracticeEight workers have a gap of €250 a month for 4 months. What is the exposure?
    8 × 250 × 4 = €8,000.

    📄 Fundamentals: What a cross-border labor contract is and why it matters

    A cross-border labor contract is an employment contract that involves more than one country, for example when an employee lives in one country, works in another or is hired by a foreign entity. The contract should state the parties, the place of work, the pay, the working time and the law that governs it.

    It matters because a missing or weak contract leads to disputes over pay, notice and termination. In many places, local mandatory employment rules apply whatever the contract says.

    TermMeaning
    Governing lawLaw used to interpret the contract
    JurisdictionCourt or forum that decides disputes
    Mandatory rulesLocal rules that cannot be removed by agreement
    Place of workWhere the work is usually done
    ProbationInitial period with shorter notice
    Required clausesTerms the contract must contain
    formulaContract completeness = Clauses present ÷ Clauses required × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: A contract has 18 of 20 required clauses. Completeness = 18 ÷ 20 × 100 = 90 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using one template for every country
    • Leaving out the place of work
    • Assuming the contract overrides local law
    Hands-on PracticeA contract has 21 of 25 required clauses. What is the completeness?
    21 ÷ 25 × 100 = 84 percent.

    ⚖️ Core Concepts: Governing law and key clauses

    In the EU, the Rome I Regulation lets the parties choose the governing law, but the employee cannot lose the mandatory protection of the law that would apply without the choice, usually the law of the country where the work is habitually done. Other regions have their own rules.

    Key clauses cover pay and currency, working hours, leave, notice, probation, confidentiality, intellectual property, termination, language and dispute settlement. Notice cost is a useful measure when you plan an exit.

    ClauseWhy it mattersCheck
    Governing lawDecides rightsChoice and mandatory rules
    Pay and currencyAvoids disputesAmount, date, currency
    Notice and probationExit rulesLocal minimums
    Working timeLegal limitsLocal limits
    LanguageClarity and validityLocal-language version where needed
    formulaNotice pay = Monthly salary × Notice months
    🧪
    Practical Example: Salary is €4,000 a month and notice is 3 months. Notice pay = 4,000 × 3 = €12,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Ignoring local minimum notice
    • Using a foreign-language contract only
    • Not stating the currency
    Hands-on PracticeSalary is €3,500 a month and notice is 2 months. What is the notice pay?
    3,500 × 2 = €7,000.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Preparing a cross-border contract step by step

    Follow seven steps. Confirm where the person will work and which entity employs them. Check local mandatory rules with a local adviser. Choose a template and adapt clauses to local law. Translate where needed and have the translation checked. Agree and sign before the start date. Store the signed copy and register the employee where required. Review the contract when the role or country changes.

    Translation is a real cost. Estimate it from the word count and the rate per word.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: conflicts of law and expected claims

    Experts handle conflicts between the chosen law and local mandatory rules, workers who move between countries, remote workers abroad and unclear employer identity, for example when a person works for a group company. They avoid disguised employment and keep contracts consistent across the group.

    They estimate expected claim cost so they can decide where better contracts and local advice are worth the spend.

    • No review of remote work abroad

    Tools & Workflows

    The tools and working procedure for each skill.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: Cross-border permanent establishment risk monitoring

    A travel and work-location tracker, a days-by-country report, a PE risk checklist, a treaty summary sheet and a decision log. The routine is to collect, count, flag, review and record.

    Tool or techniqueUsed forExample
    Travel trackerDays by person and countryName, country, entry and exit dates
    Days reportMonthly count against limitDays used and remaining
    PE risk checklistTriggers to reviewFixed place, agent, project length
    Treaty summaryTreaty rules by countryPE definition and thresholds
    Flag listCases above 75 percentPerson, country, days left
    Decision logAdvice and decision recordCase, advice, decision, date

    Working procedure

    1. List everyone who works abroad.
    2. Collect travel and location data each month.
    3. Count days by person and country.
    4. Add up person-days by country.
    5. Flag cases above 75 percent of the limit.
    6. Send flagged cases for adviser review.
    7. Record decisions.
    8. Report trends to management.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: Posted Workers Directive compliance

    A posting register, a host-country rules sheet, a declaration tracker, an A1 certificate log, a pay comparison sheet and a document file. The routine is to check, apply, declare, pay-check and file.

    Working procedure

    1. Confirm the posting is genuine.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Compliance officers hold sensitive evidence: travel logs, contracts, posting declarations, pay data and sometimes whistleblower reports. If this evidence leaks, changes or goes missing, the company loses its defence and people can be harmed.

    Security here means keeping records accurate and tamper-resistant, limiting access, protecting people who report concerns, and sharing information with authorities only through proper channels. Confirm legal duties with legal counsel in each country.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Altered or missing recordsNo defence in an auditVersion control, logs and retention rules
    Leak of travel and location dataPrivacy and personal safety riskAccess limits and data minimisation
    Exposure of a reporterRetaliation and loss of trustConfidential reporting and anti-retaliation rules
    Fake inspector or authority requestData given to the wrong personVerify identity and use legal counsel
    Unsigned or unapproved contractsDisputes and penaltiesBlock start until signed
    Data kept too longBreaks retention rulesRetention schedule and secure deletion

    🛡️ Security for: Cross-border permanent establishment risk monitoring

    • Use travel data only for compliance purposes and limit who can see it.
    • Keep decision logs that cannot be edited by the requester.
    • Share data with tax authorities only through legal counsel.

    🛡️ Security for: Posted Workers Directive compliance

    • Hold posting declarations and A1 certificates in a secure digital file that can be shown to inspectors.
    • Check the identity of anyone asking for documents on site.
    • Keep pay records that show the host-country terms were met.

    🛡️ Security for: Cross-border labor contracts

    • Store signed contracts in a controlled system with version history.
    • Restrict access to contracts with salary data.
    • Keep translations in the same secure system as the original.

    🧪 Measuring security

    formulaControl test pass rate = Controls passed ÷ Controls tested × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: A review tests 50 compliance controls and 46 pass. Pass rate = 46 ÷ 50 × 100 = 92 percent. The four failed controls get owners and dates for repair.

    Security checklist

    • Records are version-controlled and logged.
    • Access to travel and pay data is limited.
    • A confidential reporting route exists.
    • Anyone asking for documents is identified first.
    • Contracts are signed before work starts.
    • Retention and deletion rules are followed.

    Module 11

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Cross-border permanent establishment risk monitoring

    IQ1.What is a permanent establishment?
    A taxable presence in another country, such as a fixed place of business or a dependent agent who concludes contracts. The host country may tax the profit linked to it.
    IQ2.Why set an internal alert limit?
    To act before the legal risk point. It gives time to take advice and change plans.
    IQ3.A manager works from home abroad and signs contracts. What do you do?
    Treat it as high risk, limit signing authority, take tax advice and record the decision.
    IQ4.An employee has used 125 of 180 days. Days remaining?
    180 − 125 = 55 days.
    IQ5.Profit $500,000 and local rate 20 percent. Exposure?
    $100,000 before interest and penalties.

    💬 Interview Questions: Posted Workers Directive compliance

    IQ1.What is a posted worker?
    An employee sent by an employer in one EU or EEA country to work temporarily in another country to provide services.
    IQ2.Why compare pay with host rules?
    Posted workers are entitled to host-country remuneration rules. A gap must be closed.
    IQ3.An inspector asks for documents on site. What helps?
    A complete digital file with the contract, A1, declaration and payslips that can be shown at once.
    IQ4.A worker has been posted for 9 months. Months remaining before the 12-month point?
    12 − 9 = 3 months.
    IQ5.Eight workers have a €250 gap for 4 months. Exposure?
    8 × 250 × 4 = €8,000.

    💬 Interview Questions: Cross-border labor contracts

    IQ1.Can a contract choose any governing law?
    Often the law can be chosen, but the employee keeps the mandatory protections of the law that would otherwise apply.
    IQ2.What are key clauses in a cross-border contract?
    Parties, place of work, pay and currency, hours, leave, notice, probation, confidentiality, termination, law, jurisdiction and language.
    IQ3.An employee started work before signing. What do you do?
    Get it signed at once, record the reason and fix the failed step, for example by blocking payroll until signing.
    IQ4.A contract has 8,000 words at $0.12 per word. Translation cost?
    8,000 × 0.12 = $960.
    IQ5.150 contracts, 2 percent dispute chance, €4,000 each. Expected cost?
    150 × 0.02 × 4,000 = €12,000.
