    Course Overview

    Global Mobility Manager: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a Global Mobility Manager runs international assignments for employees. It covers four skills: expatriate policy design, global assignment lifecycle management, international relocation vendor oversight and cost-projection modeling.

    The role matters because each assignment costs the company far more than the employee's salary and affects tax, immigration, family life and careers in two countries. Done well, the company moves the right people at a controlled cost and the assignee succeeds. Done badly, costs rise, compliance slips and talent is lost.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples in the main lessons. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures are illustrative and use US dollars and euros. Tax, immigration and employment rules change often and differ by country, so always confirm current rules with qualified advisers.

    📘 Fundamentals: What an expatriate policy is and why it matters

    An expatriate policy is the written set of rules that says who can go on an international assignment, what pay, benefits and support they receive, and who approves exceptions. It turns a one-off negotiation into a fair and repeatable process.

    It matters because international assignments are expensive and emotional. Without a policy, every assignee negotiates alone, costs drift, similar people get different packages and the company carries legal and tax risk it cannot see. A good policy is clear, affordable, fair and tied to the business reason for sending someone abroad.

    TermMeaning
    Home countryWhere the employee is normally based and employed
    Host countryWhere the employee works during the assignment
    AssigneeEmployee on an international assignment
    Assignment letterContract summary of terms for one assignee
    PackageAll pay, allowances and benefits for the assignment
    ExceptionApproved deviation from the written policy
    formulaCost multiple = Total annual assignment cost ÷ Home base salary
    🧪
    Practical Example: Base salary is $100,000. Housing and cost-of-living support is $30,000, education $15,000, tax cost $20,000 and relocation spread over the year $10,000. Total = 100,000 + 30,000 + 15,000 + 20,000 + 10,000 = $175,000. Cost multiple = 175,000 ÷ 100,000 = 1.75 times base salary.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Treating the policy as a pay document only and ignoring tax, immigration and duty of care
    • Writing the policy without a business case or budget owner
    • Letting each manager promise benefits before HR has costed them
    Hands-on PracticeBase $80,000, housing $28,000, education $12,000, tax cost $16,000, relocation (annual share) $8,000. What is the cost multiple?
    Total = 80,000 + 28,000 + 12,000 + 16,000 + 8,000 = $144,000. Multiple = 144,000 ÷ 80,000 = 1.8.

    🧭 Core Concepts: Assignment types and policy tiers

    Not every international move needs the same package. Policies usually split moves into types by length and purpose, so cost and support match the need. Common types are long-term assignment, short-term assignment, commuter or rotational work, permanent transfer and localisation, where the assignee becomes a local employee.

    Duration limits differ between companies and are partly driven by tax and immigration rules, so each company should confirm its own thresholds with advisers. A typical split is short-term below one year and long-term from one to five years. Short-term packages are usually lighter, often built on a daily allowance.

    TypeTypical lengthTypical support
    Short-term assignmentUnder about 1 yearPer diem or temporary housing, home leave, minimal relocation
    Long-term assignmentAbout 1 to 5 yearsFull package: housing, schooling, tax support, relocation
    Commuter or rotationalRepeated tripsTravel and accommodation, no household move
    Permanent transferNo return plannedOne-time relocation, local terms
    LocalisationConverted from assignmentPhased removal of expatriate benefits
    formulaShort-term cost = Days abroad × Daily allowance
    🧪
    Practical Example: An engineer spends 90 days on a project abroad at a daily allowance of $120. Allowance cost = 90 × 120 = $10,800, before flights and tax support.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using one package for all assignment types
    • Letting short-term trips quietly become long-term without a review
    • Forgetting that tax or immigration rules may change at a time limit
    Hands-on PracticeA consultant is abroad 150 days at $100 per day. What is the allowance cost?
    150 × 100 = $15,000.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Designing the policy step by step

    Start with the business case: why are we sending people, how many, and for how long? Then choose the policy approach. The balance sheet approach keeps the assignee at home-country purchasing power and adds allowances for extra host costs. The host-based or local-plus approach pays closer to local terms with a few extras. A lump-sum approach gives one amount and lets the assignee manage costs.

    Next define each component: housing, cost-of-living support, education, home leave, relocation, tax support and medical cover. Set eligibility, approval levels and an exception process. Have legal, tax and finance review it, then communicate it with a short guide and an FAQ for assignees and managers.

    ApproachHow it worksBest for
    Balance sheetKeeps home purchasing power, adds host-cost allowancesLong-term assignments of key talent
    Host-based / local-plusLocal pay with selected extrasPermanent moves, early-career staff
    Lump sumSingle payment, assignee manages costsShort assignments, simple moves
    Per diemDaily amount for living costsShort-term and project work
    formulaHousing allowance = Host housing cost − Housing norm (home-country housing share)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Host rent is $4,000 a month and the policy housing norm is $1,200. Allowance = 4,000 − 1,200 = $2,800 a month, or $33,600 a year.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Copying another company's policy without checking cost and culture
    • No written exception process
    • Skipping tax and legal review before launch
    Hands-on PracticeHost rent is $3,500 and the housing norm is $1,000. What is the monthly allowance?
    3,500 − 1,000 = $2,500 a month.

    📊 Intermediate Concepts: Policy KPIs: cost, exceptions and budget

    A policy is only useful if you can measure how it behaves. Four KPIs are standard: cost per assignee, budget variance, exception rate and early return rate. Track them quarterly and review the reasons behind every large variance.

    Budget variance shows whether costs match the plan. Exception rate shows whether the policy is realistic. A high exception rate usually means the policy is wrong, not that managers are difficult.

    KPIFormulaTypical use
    Budget variance(Actual − Budget) ÷ BudgetShow whether cost is under control
    Exception rateExceptions ÷ RequestsTest whether the policy fits reality
    Cost per assigneeTotal cost ÷ AssigneesCompare cohorts and countries
    Early return rateEarly returns ÷ Assignments endedSpot selection or support problems
    formulaBudget variance % = (Actual cost − Budget) ÷ Budget × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The budget for 12 assignees is $2,400,000. Actual cost is $2,640,000. Variance = (2,640,000 − 2,400,000) ÷ 2,400,000 = 10%. Separately, 9 exceptions out of 60 requests give an exception rate of 15%.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Reporting only total cost and not cost per assignee
    • Ignoring a rising exception rate
    • Comparing actual cost with a budget that was never updated for exchange rates
    Hands-on PracticeBudget is $1,800,000 and actual is $2,070,000. What is the budget variance?
    (2,070,000 − 1,800,000) ÷ 1,800,000 = 15%.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: equity, risk, exceptions and repayment terms

    At expert level you protect fairness and the company at the same time. Internal equity means similar assignees in similar locations receive similar packages. Review hardship locations, dual-career couples, single parents and employees with safety concerns, such as those who may face legal or personal risk in some host countries, so the policy meets duty of care.

    Control exceptions with named approvers, a log and a yearly review. Use repayment (clawback) clauses so that if an assignee leaves early by choice, part of the one-time relocation cost is recovered. Add a sunset or review date so the policy is refreshed with market data. Confirm legal enforceability of repayment terms in each country before relying on them.

    RiskControl
    Unequal packagesBenchmarking and a policy matrix
    Frequent exceptionsApprover sign-off, exception log, yearly review
    Early voluntary exitRepayment clause and retention check-ins
    Duty-of-care gapsRisk review per host country, family support
    Outdated policyAnnual review with survey data
    formulaUnrecovered cost = One-time relocation cost × (Months remaining ÷ Planned months)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Relocation cost is $30,000 for a 24-month assignment. The assignee resigns at month 8, leaving 16 months. Unrecovered cost = 30,000 × 16 ÷ 24 = $20,000, which the repayment clause lets the company claim back, subject to local law.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Writing repayment terms without legal review in the host country
    • Approving exceptions by email with no record
    • Never refreshing the policy after the first launch
    Hands-on PracticeRelocation cost is $36,000 for a 36-month assignment. The assignee leaves at month 12. What is the unrecovered cost?
    Months remaining = 24. 36,000 × 24 ÷ 36 = $24,000.

    🔄 Fundamentals: What the assignment lifecycle is and why it matters

    The global assignment lifecycle is the full journey of an assignee: business case and selection, assessment, pre-departure preparation, relocation, time on assignment, and the end of assignment, which may be repatriation, localisation or a next assignment. A mobility manager owns this journey from first idea to final outcome.

    It matters because most failures happen at handover points: a visa started too late, a payroll set up wrongly, a family unhappy after arrival, a returnee with no role. Managing the lifecycle as one process, with owners and dates, removes those gaps.

    StageMain outputs
    Business case and selectionApproved need and candidate
    AssessmentReadiness of employee and family
    Pre-departureVisa, tax briefing, assignment letter, move plan
    On assignmentPayroll, support, compliance monitoring
    Repatriation or next stepReturn role, localisation or new assignment
    formulaClosing assignees = Opening assignees + New starts − Ended assignments
    🧪
    Practical Example: A company starts the quarter with 120 assignees, 28 new starts and 22 ended. Closing = 120 + 28 − 22 = 126 assignees.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Managing only the move and ignoring the return
    • No single owner for the whole journey
    • Keeping lifecycle data in separate spreadsheets
    Hands-on PracticeOpening 200, new starts 35, ended 41. What is the closing number?
    200 + 35 − 41 = 194.

    🎯 Core Concepts: Initiation, selection and assessment

    Assignments start for business reasons: filling a skills gap, starting or running an operation abroad, transferring knowledge, or developing a leader. Each should have a written business case showing objective, duration, cost and the plan for the role afterwards.

    Selection looks at technical fit and also at adaptability, family situation, partner work options, language and health or safety needs. Assessing the family early prevents many early returns.

    DriverTypical question to ask
    Skills gapCan the role be filled locally at lower cost?
    Business start-upHow long is the build phase?
    Knowledge transferWho will take over and when?
    Leadership developmentWhat role waits at the end?
    Client or project needIs a short-term assignment enough?
    formulaCost per assignment month = Total assignment cost ÷ Assignment months
    🧪
    Practical Example: A 24-month assignment costs $175,000 in total. Cost per month = 175,000 ÷ 24 = about $7,292.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Approving assignments without comparing local hire or short-term options
    • Ignoring the partner or family during selection
    • No planned role for the employee on return
    Hands-on PracticeA 36-month assignment costs $210,000. What is the cost per month?
    210,000 ÷ 36 = about $5,833.

    🧳 Intermediate Concepts: Pre-departure process step by step

    Work backwards from the planned arrival date. The longest task, usually immigration, sets the latest start date. Typical steps: approve the assignment, issue the assignment letter, start the visa or work permit, arrange a tax briefing and payroll set-up, book medical checks, start the household move, find housing and schools, and deliver cultural orientation.

    Use a checklist with an owner and a due date for each step. Lead times differ by country and visa type, so confirm them with the immigration provider rather than assuming them.

    StepOwnerCheck
    Assignment letter signedMobility teamBefore any move spend
    Visa or permit filedImmigration providerReceipt or reference saved
    Tax and payroll set-upTax and payrollBriefing done, payroll form filed
    Housing and school searchDestination servicesShortlist shared with family
    Household goods bookedMoving vendorPickup date confirmed
    formulaLatest start date = Planned arrival date − Longest lead time (days)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The planned arrival is 1 September and the work permit needs 75 days. Latest start = 1 September − 75 days = 18 June. Starting later makes arrival on time unlikely.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Starting immigration last because it feels like paperwork
    • Relying on a verbal lead time instead of the provider's current estimate
    • Booking the household move before the permit is secure
    Hands-on PracticeThe planned arrival is 1 July and the longest lead time is 45 days. When must work start?
    1 July − 45 days = 17 May.

    📡 Intermediate Concepts: During assignment: monitoring, compliance and KPIs

    Once the assignee is abroad, monitoring protects the person and the company. Track day counts in each country, visa and permit expiry dates, social security certificates, payroll accuracy, and wellbeing check-ins. Start renewals early so no document expires while the person is working.

    KPIs make the work visible: document compliance rate, on-time arrival rate, payroll error rate and assignee satisfaction. Review expiry reports monthly and escalate anything close to its deadline.

    On-time arrivalArrived by planned date ÷ Starts90% or higher
    Payroll error ratePayroll corrections ÷ Payroll runsBelow 2%
    Assignee satisfactionSurvey scoreSet by the company
    formulaDocument compliance rate = Assignees with valid documents ÷ Total assignees × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 150 assignees, 141 have valid permits and certificates. Compliance = 141 ÷ 150 = 94%. Nine assignees need urgent renewal action.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Reviewing expiry dates only when the assignee asks
    • Counting days in a country by memory
    • Treating a satisfied assignee as a low-risk family
    Hands-on PracticeOf 80 assignees, 76 have valid documents. What is the compliance rate?
    76 ÷ 80 = 95%.

    🏁 Advanced Concepts: Expert: repatriation, retention and next assignments

    Repatriation should be planned about six months before the end date: confirm the return role, agree the date, arrange the household move and tax exit steps, and hold a career conversation. Many returnees leave within two years because the role, status or pay on return feels like a step back.

    Options at the end include return home, a new assignment, localisation or permanent transfer. Expert managers also capture what the assignee learned and use returnees as mentors, so the company gets value from the experience.

    OptionWhen it fitsWatch-out
    Return homeBusiness need metRole and status on return
    Next assignmentAssignee mobile, need elsewhereFatigue and family impact
    LocalisationLong-term need in host countryPhase out expatriate benefits fairly
    Permanent transferPlanned moveLocal terms and contract change
    formulaReturnee retention rate = Returnees still employed at 12 or 24 months ÷ Total returnees × 100

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Waiting until the last month to plan return
    • No career plan agreed before departure
    • Never measuring retention after return
    Hands-on PracticeOf 25 returnees, 20 are still employed after 24 months. What is retention?
    20 ÷ 25 = 80%.

    🚚 Fundamentals: Relocation vendors and why oversight matters

    Mobility teams rarely deliver every service themselves. They buy services from relocation management companies (RMCs), moving companies, destination services providers (DSPs), temporary housing suppliers, immigration firms and tax advisers. Vendor oversight means making sure these suppliers deliver the agreed service at the agreed price and protect the assignee and company data.

    It matters because vendors touch the assignee at the most stressful moments. A late shipment, a poor housing shortlist or an unclear invoice damages trust and costs money.

    TermMeaning
    RMCRelocation management company that coordinates moves
    DSPDestination services provider who helps in the host country
    HHGHousehold goods, the shipment of belongings
    SLAService level agreement with measurable targets
    RFPRequest for proposal used to select a vendor
    Pass-through costThird-party cost billed on at cost or with a fee
    formulaVendor spend share = Vendor spend ÷ Total mobility spend × 100
    🧪

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Treating the vendor as responsible for everything with no internal oversight
    • No named vendor manager
    • Not tracking spend by vendor
    Hands-on PracticeTotal spend is $3,200,000 and the DSP accounts for $800,000. What is the share?
    800,000 ÷ 3,200,000 = 25%.

    🧩 Core Concepts: Vendor models and service scope

    There are three common models. A single RMC manages everything and subcontracts the rest. A multi-vendor or unbundled model buys each service separately. A hybrid keeps a main RMC and buys specialist services directly. Some companies also offer lump-sum or self-service moves for simple cases.

    Choose the model by volume, complexity and in-house capacity. Fees can be a fee per file, a percentage of pass-through spend, or a fixed annual retainer.

    ModelStrengthWatch-out
    Single RMCOne point of contact, simple governanceLess price visibility on subcontractors
    Multi-vendorBest supplier per service, price controlMore management work
    HybridBalance of control and simplicityNeeds clear role split
    Lump sum / self-serviceLow admin for the companyAssignee carries the risk
    formulaManagement fee = Number of files × Fee per file
    🧪

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Picking a model only on the lowest fee
    • Not asking for subcontractor rates
    • Mixing fee types in one comparison
    Hands-on PracticeAn RMC charges $1,000 per file for 80 files. What is the fee?
    80 × 1,000 = $80,000.

    📝 Intermediate Concepts: Selecting vendors: RFP scoring and SLA design

    Run a structured selection. Define needs, issue an RFP, score replies with agreed weights, interview the best vendors, check references and negotiate. Weights show what matters most: price, service quality, network coverage and technology are common choices.

    Then write an SLA with measurable targets, for example first contact with an assignee within one working day, a destination quote within three working days, and monthly reporting by a set date. Add service credits, audit rights, data protection terms and exit clauses.

    RFP criterionWeight
    Price40%

    Network coverage20%

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Changing weights after seeing the replies
    • Writing SLAs that cannot be measured
    • Signing without audit and exit rights

    30 + 25.5 + 18 + 7 = 80.5.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Monitoring performance: SLA KPIs and invoice audit

    Review vendors monthly with a scorecard: SLA compliance, assignee satisfaction, cost per move, invoice accuracy and claims. Use the vendor's data but verify a sample against your own records, such as case timestamps and assignee feedback.

    Audit invoices against the contract and the approved quote. Typical errors are charges outside the rate card, duplicate items, missing discounts and services not delivered.

    • Paying invoices without checking the quote
    • Reviewing performance only once a year

    🔐 Advanced Concepts: Expert: cost control, data privacy and vendor risk

    Experts negotiate volume rebates, benchmark rates against the market and review concentration risk, so no single vendor can disrupt the program. Ask for business continuity plans and check that shipping insurance reflects the declared value of goods.

    Vendors handle passports, addresses, family details and medical data. Confirm data protection terms, where data is stored, how it is transferred between countries and how it is deleted. Check customs, import and sanctions rules for destinations with the vendor and legal team, because some goods and countries have special restrictions.

    💰 Fundamentals: What cost projection is and what it includes

    Cost-projection modeling estimates the full cost of an international assignment before it is approved, then tracks actual cost against that estimate. It helps leaders decide whether the assignment, a local hire or another option is the best value.

    A projection has recurring costs, paid every month or year, and one-time costs, paid once. Recurring costs include salary, housing and education. One-time costs include relocation, immigration and set-up.

    CostType
    Base salaryRecurring
    Housing allowanceRecurring
    Education allowanceRecurring
    Tax support costRecurring
    Relocation and shipmentOne-time
    Immigration feesOne-time or renewal
    formulaTotal cost = (Monthly recurring cost × Months) + One-time costs
    🧪
    Practical Example: Recurring cost is $12,000 a month for 24 months and one-time costs are $45,000. Total = 12,000 × 24 + 45,000 = 288,000 + 45,000 = $333,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Leaving out one-time costs
    • Quoting only salary as the assignment cost
    • Using the same cost for every host country
    Hands-on PracticeRecurring cost is $10,000 a month for 36 months and one-time costs are $30,000. What is the total?
    10,000 × 36 + 30,000 = 360,000 + 30,000 = $390,000.

    🧾 Core Concepts: Cost components and the tax gross-up

    Some benefits are taxable. If the company wants the assignee to receive a benefit net of tax, it must pay extra so that after tax the person keeps the full value. This extra amount is called the gross-up.

    Because of the gross-up, a taxable $2,000 allowance can cost the company much more than $2,000. Tax rates and rules differ by country and by person, so use figures from your tax advisers in real models.

    ComponentTaxable?Gross-up needed?
    Housing allowanceOften taxableOften yes
    Education allowanceVaries by countryCheck with tax adviser
    Cost-of-living allowanceUsually taxableOften yes
    Moving costsVariesCheck with tax adviser
    formulaGross-up cost = Net benefit ÷ (1 − Tax rate)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The assignee should receive $2,000 net and the tax rate is 40%. Cost = 2,000 ÷ (1 − 0.40) = 2,000 ÷ 0.60 = about $3,333.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Multiplying by (1 + tax rate) instead of dividing by (1 − tax rate)
    • Using a home tax rate for a host-country benefit
    • Forgetting the gross-up in the total budget
    Hands-on PracticeThe net benefit is $3,000 and the tax rate is 35%. What is the gross-up cost?
    3,000 ÷ 0.65 = about $4,615.

    🧮 Intermediate Concepts: Building the model step by step

    Use a model with four parts. An inputs sheet holds the assumptions: salary, exchange rates, rent, tax estimates and duration. A calculation sheet builds each cost line. An output sheet shows the total, the cost per month and a comparison to budget. A scenario sheet tests changes in the key assumptions.

    Keep one assumptions register with the source and date of every input. Convert host-currency costs into the reporting currency at a stated exchange rate and state the date of that rate.

    SheetContents
    InputsSalary, rent, rates, tax estimates, duration
    CalculationOne line per cost, monthly and total
    OutputTotal, monthly cost, budget comparison
    ScenariosBest, base and worst cases
    Assumptions registerSource and date for every input
    formulaCost in reporting currency = Host-currency cost × Exchange rate
    🧪
    Practical Example: Rent is €4,000 a month, the rate is 1 euro = 1.10 US dollars and the assignment lasts 24 months. Monthly cost = 4,000 × 1.10 = $4,400. Total = 4,400 × 24 = $105,600.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Hard-coding numbers inside formulas
    • No source or date for assumptions
    • Using one exchange rate forever without review
    Hands-on PracticeRent is €3,500 a month at a rate of 1.08 for 12 months. What is the total in dollars?
    3,500 × 1.08 = 3,780. 3,780 × 12 = $45,360.

    ⚖️ Intermediate Concepts: Tax equalization cost and forecast accuracy

    In a tax equalization scheme the assignee pays a hypothetical tax, roughly what they would have paid at home, and the company pays the actual home and host taxes. The company's cost is the gap between actual and hypothetical tax. Real calculations are complex, so mobility managers use figures from tax advisers.

    After the assignment begins, compare actual cost with the projection. Forecast accuracy shows how reliable the model is and where assumptions need improving.

    MeasureFormula
    Tax equalization costActual tax − Hypothetical tax
    Variance %(Actual − Projected) ÷ Projected × 100
    Forecast accuracy100% − Variance %
    FX impactCost in host currency × Change in rate
    formulaTax equalization cost = Actual tax − Hypothetical tax   |   Accuracy = 100% − |Actual − Projected| ÷ Projected
    🧪
    Practical Example: Actual tax is $62,000 and hypothetical tax is $35,000, so the company bears $27,000. Separately, a project projected at $300,000 ended at $327,000. Variance is 27,000 ÷ 300,000 = 9%, so accuracy is 91%.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Treating hypothetical tax as the company's cost
    • Never reviewing projections against actuals
    • Measuring accuracy only at the end of the assignment
    Hands-on PracticeActual tax is $48,000 and hypothetical tax is $30,000. What is the company cost?
    48,000 − 30,000 = $18,000.

    🎲 Advanced Concepts: Expert: scenarios, risk and decision support

    Build best, base and worst cases and give each a probability. Use the probability-weighted cost to set the budget. Test exchange rates, rent increases and tax changes. Add a contingency, often 10 to 15 percent, and state it openly.

    Compare the assignment with alternatives such as a local hire, a short-term assignment, remote work or a business traveller arrangement. Each has different cost, speed, tax exposure and business value. Present the recommendation with the assumptions and the risks, so leaders can decide with a clear view.

    CaseCostProbability
    Base case$300,00060%
    High case (FX and rent rise)$360,00030%
    Low case$270,00010%
    formulaExpected cost = Σ (Probability × Cost)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Expected cost = 0.60 × 300,000 + 0.30 × 360,000 + 0.10 × 270,000 = 180,000 + 108,000 + 27,000 = $315,000. Budget the expected value plus a stated contingency.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Presenting one number with no range
    • Hiding contingency inside other lines
    • Comparing the assignment only with itself
    Hands-on PracticeCases: $200,000 at 50%, $250,000 at 30%, $180,000 at 20%. What is the expected cost?
    100,000 + 75,000 + 36,000 = $211,000.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Global mobility handles some of the most sensitive employee data in a company: passports, visas, family details, medical and school records, salary, tax status and home addresses. This data crosses borders and passes through many vendors, so one weak link can cause a breach.

    Security here means protecting people and the company: keeping data private, controlling who can see and change it, preventing fraud in payments and allowances, and following the privacy, tax and immigration laws of each country. Confirm the exact legal duties with your privacy and legal teams.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Leak of passport or family dataIdentity theft and legal claimsEncrypt files, share through secure portals and limit who can open them
    Vendor data breachThe company stays responsibleData protection terms, security review and a breach notice duty in contracts
    Cross-border data transferSome countries limit transfersUse approved transfer methods and record the legal basis
    Allowance or invoice fraudMoney is lostDual approval, rate cards and invoice audits
    Too much accessStaff see data they do not needRole-based access and a quarterly access review
    Data kept too longBreaks retention rulesRetention schedule and secure deletion

    🛡️ Security for: Expatriate policy design

    • Keep policy files on a controlled site and version them so everyone uses the current rules.
    • Restrict who can approve exceptions and keep a log that cannot be edited by the requester.
    • Never put personal case details inside policy examples.

    🛡️ Security for: Global assignment lifecycle management

    • Limit the assignment tracker to those who need it and hide family and medical fields from most users.
    • Alert the owner when a permit or document expires, and store scans in a secure folder, not in email.
    • Remove access when someone leaves the team.

    🛡️ Security for: International relocation vendor oversight

    • Check each vendor's security controls before sharing any assignee data.
    • Share the minimum data needed and use secure transfer, not email attachments.
    • Audit vendor invoices and bank detail changes to catch fraud.

    🛡️ Security for: Cost-projection modeling

    • Cost models contain salary and tax data, so protect and restrict the workbook.
    • Lock formula cells and keep version history so figures cannot be changed unnoticed.
    • Remove names from models used for training or sharing.

    🧪 Measuring security

    formulaAccess review completion = Reviews completed ÷ Reviews due × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: Of 40 quarterly access reviews due, 38 are completed. Completion = 38 ÷ 40 × 100 = 95 percent. The two late reviews are finished within a week and the owners are reminded.

    Security checklist

    • Personal data is encrypted and shared only through approved channels.
    • Every vendor has data protection terms and a breach notice duty.
    • Access is role-based and reviewed each quarter.
    • Payment changes need two approvals.
    • A retention schedule exists and deletion is recorded.
    • Staff are trained on privacy each year.

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Expatriate policy design

    IQ1.What is the difference between the balance sheet approach and the host-based approach?
    The balance sheet approach keeps the assignee at home purchasing power and adds allowances for host costs, so it suits key talent on long-term assignments. The host-based approach pays closer to local terms with a few extras, so it suits permanent moves and early-career staff. The balance sheet approach is usually more expensive.
    IQ2.Why do policies split assignments into types?
    Length and purpose change cost, tax and support needs. A short project needs a light package and a long-term assignment needs housing, schooling and tax support. Separate tiers keep cost proportionate and fair.
    IQ3.A senior executive demands a housing allowance far above policy. What do you do?
    Explain the policy and its fair basis. Check the market data for that city in case the housing norm is wrong. If an exception is justified, cost it, record the reason and get the named approver's sign-off in writing.
    IQ4.Assignment cost is 15 percent above budget. How do you respond?
    Break variance down by cost line to find the cause, such as tax cost, housing or exchange rates. Check whether the budget was set with full cost. Then fix the estimate, adjust the policy or add controls, and report the finding.
    IQ5.Base is $120,000, housing and cost-of-living $36,000, education $18,000, tax cost $24,000 and relocation share $12,000. What is the cost multiple?
    Total = 120,000 + 36,000 + 18,000 + 24,000 + 12,000 = $210,000. Multiple = 210,000 ÷ 120,000 = 1.75.

    💬 Interview Questions: Global assignment lifecycle management

    IQ1.Describe the stages of the assignment lifecycle.
    Business case and selection, assessment, pre-departure, relocation, time on assignment, and the end stage, which is repatriation, localisation or a next assignment. A mobility manager owns the whole journey.
    IQ2.Why start immigration steps early?
    Immigration is usually the longest and least controllable step. Starting late risks late arrival, lost project time and extra cost, and a late permit may block other steps such as shipping.
    IQ3.An assignee says the family is unhappy three months in. What do you do?
    Talk to the assignee and partner to find the cause, such as housing, school or isolation. Involve the destination services provider and the manager, agree actions and review in a few weeks. Early action reduces the risk of early return.
    IQ4.A permit expires in 45 days and renewal has not been filed. How do you respond?
    Escalate to the immigration provider today, check the earliest filing route and whether the assignee may keep working while the application is pending. Tell the manager and plan fallback options. Then fix the expiry report process.
    IQ5.Opening headcount 90, starts 18, ends 12. What is closing headcount?
    90 + 18 − 12 = 96.

    💬 Interview Questions: International relocation vendor oversight

    IQ1.What do an RMC and a DSP do?
    A relocation management company coordinates moves and suppliers. A destination services provider helps the assignee in the host country with tasks such as home search, schools and registration.
    IQ2.Why do SLAs need numbers?
    Measurable targets make performance visible and enforceable. 'Quote within three working days' can be checked, while 'prompt service' cannot.
    IQ3.A vendor misses its SLA for three months. What do you do?
    Verify the data, then hold a formal review to find the root cause. Agree a written recovery plan with dates and apply service credits if the contract allows. If there is no improvement, start a backup vendor process.
    IQ4.An invoice shows charges not in the quote. What do you do?
    Compare it with the quote and rate card, dispute the unsupported lines, and ask for a corrected invoice. Record the error in the vendor scorecard and check other invoices for the same issue.
    IQ5.There were 360 cases and 324 met the SLA. What is compliance?
    324 ÷ 360 = 90 percent.

    💬 Interview Questions: Cost-projection modeling

    IQ1.Why do we gross up taxable benefits?
    If the company wants the assignee to keep the full value after tax, it must pay more than the net value. The gross-up is net value ÷ (1 − tax rate). It is a real cost that must be in the projection.
    IQ2.Why add a contingency to a projection?
    Inputs such as rent, exchange rates and tax change over time. A stated contingency of about 10 to 15 percent covers normal movement and avoids hidden padding.
    IQ3.Finance says actual cost came in 12 percent above projection. What do you do?
    Compare actual with projected by cost line to find the cause. Check missing items, FX moves and outdated assumptions. Update the model and assumptions register and report what changed.
    IQ4.Exchange rates are volatile. How do you handle that in a projection?
    Use a stated rate and date, run sensitivity at plus and minus a few percent, and show the range. Consider with finance whether hedging is suitable for large programs.
    IQ5.A taxable allowance of $1,500 must be paid net, and the tax rate is 25 percent. What is the gross-up cost?
    1,500 ÷ (1 − 0.25) = 1,500 ÷ 0.75 = $2,000.
