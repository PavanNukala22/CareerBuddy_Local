    Module 01

    Course Overview

    Head of International Recruitment: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how a Head of International Recruitment builds and leads a lawful, ethical and efficient system for hiring people across borders. It covers four skills: inter-country talent corridor development, overseas sourcing agency networks, global deployment compliance and international labor pacts.

    The role matters because hard-to-fill roles often need people from other countries. A good head builds lasting pipelines, chooses and controls agencies, makes sure every deployment is lawful and uses agreements between countries to make hiring faster. A weak one pays too much, takes legal risk or harms workers and source countries.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures are illustrative and use US dollars. Laws, quotas and agreements change, so confirm current rules with legal and immigration advisers before acting.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Explain a talent corridor and measure pipeline yield and cost per placement.
    2. Size a demand-supply gap and the candidates needed for a target.
    3. Assess the effect of a corridor on the source country.
    4. Vet overseas agencies with a weighted score and apply the employer-pays principle.
    5. Calculate agency fill rate, fees and quality of hire.
    6. Measure the rate of fee violations and respond to them.
    7. Build a pre-deployment checklist and measure compliance.
    8. Plan lead times for visas and permits and measure on-time starts.
    9. Estimate penalty exposure from compliance failures.
    10. Use bilateral and regional labor agreements, measure quota use and estimate the return on a pact.
    11. Protect candidate and worker data, prevent exploitation and fraud in agency networks and keep compliance evidence.

    🛤️ Fundamentals: What a talent corridor is and why it matters

    A talent corridor is a planned, lasting route that moves skilled people from one country to jobs in another. It links training, language, certification, visas, placement and settling. It is built with partners such as schools, governments and employers in both countries.

    It matters because one-off hiring is slow and costly. A corridor gives a steady supply of ready candidates, shortens time to fill and, if designed well, benefits both countries.

    TermMeaning
    Talent corridorPlanned route for skilled people between two countries
    Source countryCountry the candidates come from
    Destination countryCountry where they will work
    Pipeline yieldShare of entrants who are placed
    Partner institutionSchool or body that trains or certifies
    PlacementCandidate starts a job in the destination
    formulaPipeline yield = Placements ÷ Candidates entered × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: 500 candidates enter the corridor and 320 are placed. Yield = 320 ÷ 500 × 100 = 64 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Treating a corridor as a single campaign
    • Ignoring the source country
    • No clear partner roles
    Hands-on Practice400 candidates enter and 240 are placed. What is the yield?
    240 ÷ 400 × 100 = 60 percent.

    ⚖️ Core Concepts: Demand, supply and the gap

    Start with the need. Count the vacancies you expect over the plan period, then estimate the supply you can fill from the local market and from internal moves. The difference is the gap the corridor must help to fill.

    Use reliable data: labour market statistics, workforce plans and past hiring. Review the gap each quarter, because demand changes.

    ItemSourceNote
    VacanciesWorkforce planBy role and location
    Local supplyMarket data and internal poolRealistic, not hopeful
    GapVacancies − Local supplyTarget for the corridor
    Time framePlan periodUsually 12 months
    ReviewQuarterlyUpdate numbers
    formulaGap = Vacancies − Local supply
    🧪
    Practical Example: There are 1,200 vacancies and local supply is 700. Gap = 1,200 − 700 = 500.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Overstating local supply
    • Planning with no review
    • Ignoring role differences
    Hands-on PracticeThere are 900 vacancies and local supply is 550. What is the gap?
    900 − 550 = 350.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Building a corridor step by step

    Follow eight steps. Define the roles and the gap. Choose the source country and check its rules and needs. Select partners for training, language and certification. Design the programme and agree the standards. Set up selection and fair recruitment rules. Handle visas, licences and relocation. Place candidates and support them for the first year. Measure results and improve.

    Plan the intake from the target and the expected yield. If only part of those who enter are placed, you must start with more.

    StepOwnerOutput
    Gap analysisHead of recruitmentRoles and numbers
    Source choiceHead and legalCountry shortlist and risk view
    PartnersCorridor managerSigned agreements
    Programme designCorridor managerCurriculum and standards
    SelectionRecruitersFair selection rules
    Placement and supportMobility teamStarts and first-year care
    formulaCandidates needed = Target placements ÷ Pipeline yield
    🧪
    Practical Example: The target is 300 placements and the yield is 60 percent. Candidates needed = 300 ÷ 0.60 = 500.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Starting with exactly the target number
    • No first-year support
    • Weak partner agreements
    Hands-on PracticeThe target is 240 placements and the yield is 80 percent. How many candidates are needed?
    240 ÷ 0.80 = 300.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Corridor KPIs and scenarios

    Track pipeline yield, cost per placement, time from entry to placement, retention at 12 months, candidate satisfaction and employer satisfaction. Compare corridors to decide where to invest.

    Scenario: yield is low because many candidates fail the language test. Add more language training early, set realistic entry levels and track progress each month.

    KPIFormulaUse
    Pipeline yieldPlacements ÷ EntrantsEfficiency
    Cost per placementTotal cost ÷ PlacementsCost
    Time to placePlacement date − Entry dateSpeed
    Retention at 12 monthsStill employed ÷ PlacedQuality
    SatisfactionAverage scoreExperience
    formulaCost per placement = Total corridor cost ÷ Placements
    🧪
    Practical Example: A corridor costs $960,000 and places 320 people. Cost per placement = 960,000 ÷ 320 = $3,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Leaving out partner costs
    • Counting entrants as placements
    • No retention data
    Hands-on PracticeA corridor costs $660,000 and places 200 people. What is the cost per placement?
    660,000 ÷ 200 = $3,300.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: ethics and the source country

    Experts check what a corridor does to the source country. If it draws scarce skilled workers, such as health workers, from places that need them, harm can result. They follow codes such as the WHO Global Code of Practice on the International Recruitment of Health Personnel, avoid active recruitment from countries on its list of those with the most serious shortages, and invest in training in the source country.

    A simple measure is the outflow ratio: the share of the source country's workforce in that profession that the corridor takes. A low and sustainable ratio, combined with training investment, shows responsibility.

    RiskControl
    Brain drainOutflow ratio limits and training investment
    Unfair selectionPublished criteria and non-discrimination
    Debt or fees for candidatesEmployer-pays rule
    Candidates strandedGuaranteed placement or support
    Reputation harmOpen reporting to partners
    formulaOutflow ratio = Placements ÷ Source-country workforce in the profession × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: The corridor places 320 nurses from a source workforce of 16,000 nurses. Outflow ratio = 320 ÷ 16,000 × 100 = 2 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Ignoring official shortage lists
    • No investment in source-country training
    • Charging candidates fees
    Hands-on PracticeThe corridor places 150 people from a source workforce of 10,000. What is the ratio?
    150 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = 1.5 percent.

    🤝 Fundamentals: What overseas sourcing agency networks are and why they matter

    An overseas sourcing agency network is a group of recruitment agencies in other countries that find, screen and present candidates for your company. They bring local knowledge, language skills and reach. You manage them with contracts, standards, fees and regular reviews.

    It matters because agencies can speed up hiring, but they also carry risk. A poor agency may charge workers illegal fees, mislead candidates or break local law, and the hiring company can be held responsible.

    TermMeaning
    AgencyBusiness that finds candidates for a fee
    RequisitionApproved request to fill a role
    Fill rateShare of requisitions filled
    Placement feeFee paid by the employer on a hire
    Preferred supplierAgency approved for use
    ScorecardRegular review of an agency's results
    formulaAgency fill rate = Hires ÷ Requisitions given × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: An agency is given 48 requisitions and fills 36. Fill rate = 36 ÷ 48 × 100 = 75 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using agencies with no contract
    • Judging only on speed
    • Not checking how agencies treat candidates
    Hands-on PracticeAn agency is given 60 requisitions and fills 42. What is the fill rate?
    42 ÷ 60 × 100 = 70 percent.

    🔍 Core Concepts: Vetting and selecting agencies

    Vet each agency before use. Check its licence or registration where required, its ethics and fee policy, references from other clients, its capacity and its use of data and privacy rules. Follow the principle that the employer pays the recruitment costs and workers pay no fees, in line with ILO guidance on fair recruitment.

    Use a weighted score so decisions are fair and consistent. The weights should be set before you look at agencies.

    CriterionWeightCheck
    Licence and legal status30%Registry or official proof
    Ethics and fee policy30%Written policy and worker interviews
    References20%Two or more clients
    Capacity20%Staff, reach and past volumes
    Data protectionPass or failPrivacy practices
    formulaVetting score = Sum of (Score × Weight)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Licence 90 at 30 percent, ethics 80 at 30 percent, references 70 at 20 percent and capacity 60 at 20 percent. Score = 27 + 24 + 14 + 12 = 77.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Setting weights after seeing results
    • Skipping worker interviews
    • Ignoring unlicensed sub-agents
    Hands-on PracticeLicence 80 at 30 percent, ethics 70 at 30 percent, references 90 at 20 percent and capacity 50 at 20 percent. What is the score?
    24 + 21 + 18 + 10 = 73.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Managing the network step by step

    Follow seven steps. Define the need and the standards. Invite and vet agencies. Sign contracts that set fees, standards, audit rights and a ban on charging workers. Brief agencies on roles and rules. Run the requisitions and track results. Review each agency each quarter with a scorecard. Reward good results and end contracts that fail.

    Agree fees clearly. A common fee is a percentage of first-year salary, paid only after the candidate starts and stays through a guarantee period.

    StepOwnerOutput
    StandardsHead of recruitmentCode of conduct
    VettingProcurement and recruitmentApproved list
    ContractLegalSigned terms
    BriefingRecruitersRequisitions and rules
    TrackingRecruitersFunnel data
    ReviewHead of recruitmentQuarterly scorecard
    formulaPlacement fee = First-year salary × Fee %
    🧪
    Practical Example: The salary is $60,000 and the fee is 18 percent. Fee = 60,000 × 0.18 = $10,800.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Paying fees before the candidate starts
    • No guarantee period
    • Verbal agreements
    Hands-on PracticeThe salary is $45,000 and the fee is 15 percent. What is the fee?
    45,000 × 0.15 = $6,750.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Agency KPIs and scenarios

    Track fill rate, time to fill, cost per hire, quality of hire (still employed at 12 months), offer acceptance and candidate complaints for each agency. Share the scorecard and use it to decide who gets more work.

    Scenario: an agency has a high fill rate but low retention. Quality is poor. Review its screening, reduce its share and add a longer guarantee period.

    KPIFormulaUse

    Quality of hireStill employed at 12 months ÷ HiresQuality
    Complaint rateComplaints ÷ CandidatesEthics
    formulaQuality of hire = Hires still employed at 12 months ÷ Hires × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: An agency placed 36 people and 30 are still employed after 12 months. Quality = 30 ÷ 36 × 100 = 83.3 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Rewarding speed only
    • No retention data
    • Ignoring complaints
    Hands-on PracticeAn agency placed 30 people and 27 are still employed. What is the quality of hire?
    27 ÷ 30 × 100 = 90 percent.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: fee abuse and forced labour risk

    Experts audit for illegal fees and forced labour indicators such as debt bondage, passport retention, deception about the job, and threats. They interview workers away from the agency, review payments and keep a way for workers to report problems.

    If a violation is found, they stop the agency's placements, make sure workers are repaid, report where the law requires and review the whole network. Zero tolerance should be written in contracts.

    IndicatorCheckResponse
    Fees charged to workersWorker interviews and payment recordsRepay and suspend
    Passport kept by agencyWorker interviewsReturn and end contract
    Job differs from offerCompare offer and contractCorrect and review
    Debt bondageLoans and deductionsInvestigate and report
    No complaint routeTest the hotlineSet up and publicise
    formulaFee violation rate = Workers who paid fees ÷ Workers surveyed × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: In a survey of 60 workers, 3 say they paid fees. Rate = 3 ÷ 60 × 100 = 5 percent. The target is zero, so the agency is suspended and the workers are repaid.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Interviewing only in front of the agency
    • No repayment for workers
    • Accepting 'it is local custom'
    Hands-on PracticeIn a survey of 50 workers, 2 say they paid fees. What is the rate?
    2 ÷ 50 × 100 = 4 percent.

    ✅ Fundamentals: What global deployment compliance is and why it matters

    Global deployment compliance means making sure every person sent to or hired in another country meets the legal requirements before they start work. It covers the right to work, visa or permit, licences, medical and background checks, tax, social security and the employment contract.

    It matters because a mistake can lead to fines, deportation, bans on hiring and damage to the company and the worker. It is far cheaper to check first.

    TermMeaning
    DeploymentSending or placing a person to work in a country
    Right to workLegal permission to work in a country
    Work permit or visaOfficial document giving that permission
    Compliance rateShare of deployments that meet all rules
    SponsorEmployer who supports a work permit
    AuditReview of records
    formulaDeployment compliance rate = Compliant deployments ÷ Deployments × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 200 deployments, 188 are fully compliant. Rate = 188 ÷ 200 × 100 = 94 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Starting work before the permit is approved
    • Relying on the employee to arrange papers
    • No audit
    Hands-on PracticeOf 150 deployments, 135 are compliant. What is the rate?
    135 ÷ 150 × 100 = 90 percent.

    📋 Core Concepts: The pre-deployment checklist

    Use one checklist for every deployment. Typical items are passport validity, visa or permit, professional licence or recognition, medical checks, police or background checks where lawful, employment contract, tax and social security registration, health insurance, and housing and school plans for families.

    Each country adds its own items, so keep a country schedule. Do not allow a start date until the checklist is complete.

    ItemEvidenceOwner
    Right to workPermit or visa copyImmigration team
    Licence or recognitionCertificateRecruiter
    Medical and background checksReportsHR
    ContractSigned copyHR
    Tax and social securityRegistration proofPayroll
    formulaChecklist completeness = Items done ÷ Items required × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: 17 of 20 items are done. Completeness = 17 ÷ 20 × 100 = 85 percent. The deployment cannot start until all 20 are done.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using one checklist for all countries
    • Allowing an early start
    • Keeping no evidence
    Hands-on Practice22 of 25 items are done. What is the completeness?
    22 ÷ 25 × 100 = 88 percent.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Planning lead times step by step

    Follow seven steps. Confirm the role and start date. Check the permit route and processing time. Add a buffer for questions and delays. Work back from the start date to the latest date to apply. Submit complete applications. Track status weekly. Move the start date if the permit is late.

    Count days carefully. The latest application date is the start date minus the processing days minus the buffer.

    StepOwnerOutput
    Route checkImmigration teamPermit type and time
    Date planImmigration teamLatest application date
    ApplicationImmigration team and employeeSubmitted file
    TrackingImmigration teamStatus report
    DecisionAuthorityPermit or refusal
    Start approvalHRCleared start date
    formulaLatest application date = Start date − (Processing days + Buffer days)
    🧪
    Practical Example: The start date is 30 June, processing takes 45 days and the buffer is 15 days. 60 days before 30 June: 30 days back is 31 May and 30 more days back is 1 May. Latest date = 1 May.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • No buffer
    • Counting working days as calendar days
    • Moving no dates when late
    Hands-on PracticeThe start date is 31 July, processing takes 30 days and the buffer is 10 days. What is the latest application date?
    40 days before 31 July: 31 days back is 30 June, and 9 more days back is 21 June.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Deployment KPIs and scenarios

    Track compliance rate, checklist completeness before start, on-time start rate, permit refusal rate, document expiry alerts and audit findings. Compare countries and agencies.

    Scenario: a permit is refused. The immigration team reviews the reasons, decides whether to appeal or reapply, informs the manager and employee, and moves the start date.

    formulaOn-time start rate = Deployments started on the planned date ÷ Deployments × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 50 deployments, 46 start on the planned date. Rate = 46 ÷ 50 × 100 = 92 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Pushing people to start without papers
    • No expiry alerts
    • Hiding refusals
    Hands-on PracticeOf 40 deployments, 38 start on the planned date. What is the rate?
    38 ÷ 40 × 100 = 95 percent.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: penalties and control design

    Experts estimate penalty exposure by multiplying the number of likely violations by the average penalty. They then design controls to cut the number: system holds that block payroll until the checklist is done, regular audits and training for managers and agencies.

    They also control permits that expire. A register with alerts at 90, 60 and 30 days prevents illegal working.

    ControlPurposeTest
    Payroll holdBlocks pay until checks are doneTry a test case
    Expiry registerAlerts before permits endCheck alert dates
    Audit sampleFinds errorsAudit each quarter
    Manager trainingPrevents early startsAttendance and quiz
    Agency clausesPasses duties to agenciesContract review
    formulaPenalty exposure = Violations × Average penalty
    🧪
    Practical Example: Six violations with an average penalty of $10,000. Exposure = 6 × 10,000 = $60,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • No payroll hold
    • Alerts that arrive too late
    • No manager training
    Hands-on PracticeFour violations with an average penalty of $7,500. What is the exposure?
    4 × 7,500 = $30,000.

    🌐 Fundamentals: What international labor pacts are and why they matter

    International labor pacts are agreements between governments, or between groups of countries, that set rules for moving workers. Examples are bilateral labor agreements, memoranda of understanding, regional free movement rules, working holiday schemes and mutual recognition of qualifications.

    They matter because they can offer faster visas, quotas, recognised qualifications and protections for workers. A Head of International Recruitment uses them to build supply and to avoid slow, case-by-case routes.

    TermMeaning
    Bilateral labor agreementAgreement between two countries on worker movement
    Memorandum of understandingLess formal agreement on cooperation
    QuotaNumber of places allowed
    Free movementRight to work across member countries
    Mutual recognitionEach side accepts the other's qualifications
    Working holiday schemeShort stay work for young people
    formulaQuota use = Places used ÷ Quota × 100
    🧪

    ⚠️
    Common mistakes

    • Not reading the terms

    Hands-on PracticeA pact gives 400 places and 300 have been used. What is the quota use?

    📘 Core Concepts: Types, terms and what to read

    Read each pact for who is covered, which jobs, the annual quota, the time limits, the documents required, the fees, rights of workers and rules for family. Note the start and end dates and any review points.

    Remaining quota shows how much room is left in the period. If you use places faster than planned, you may run out.

    Term to readQuestionWhy
    CoverageWhich jobs and countries?Eligibility
    QuotaHow many places?Planning
    DurationHow long is the pact?Renewal planning
    DocumentsWhat proof is needed?Processing
    Worker rightsWhat protections apply?Compliance
    formulaQuota remaining = Quota − Places used
    🧪

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    A Head of International Recruitment is accountable for large amounts of candidate and worker data held by the company and its agencies. The role also carries ethical and legal responsibility for how workers are recruited. Illegal fees, deception and forced labour harm workers and can lead to prosecution and reputational damage.

    Security here means protecting data across the agency network, requiring audit rights and data protection terms in contracts, guarding against forged documents and fake agencies, protecting workers from exploitation, and keeping evidence of compliance. Confirm legal duties with counsel in each country.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Agency data breachCompany remains responsibleContract terms, security checks and audit rights
    Worker fees or deceptionHarm to workers and legal riskEmployer-pays rule, worker surveys and a complaint route
    Forged documents or fake agenciesIllegal deployment and fraudVerify with issuers and registers
    Passport retention or debt bondageForced labourZero tolerance and immediate escalation
    Poor control of sub-agentsHidden risksBan or approve sub-agents in contracts
    Weak recordsCannot prove complianceKeep deployment and vetting files

    🛡️ Security for: Inter-country talent corridor development

    • Check what a corridor does to the source country and avoid active recruitment from countries with serious shortages.
    • Protect candidate data shared with training partners.
    • Give candidates clear, honest information about the job and conditions.

    🛡️ Security for: Overseas sourcing agency networks

    • Include audit rights, data protection and no-fee terms in every agency contract.
    • Survey workers away from the agency to detect fees and deception.
    • Verify agency licences against official registers.

    🛡️ Security for: Global deployment compliance

    • Block payroll until the pre-deployment checklist is done.
    • Keep evidence of each check in the file.
    • Use alerts for permit expiry to prevent illegal working.

    🛡️ Security for: International labor pacts

    • Check the official text of each pact and do not rely on rumours.
    • Protect the data you share with government bodies and follow transfer rules.
    • Engage governments openly and ethically.

    🧪 Measuring security

    formulaAudit-rights coverage = Agencies with an audit-rights clause ÷ Agencies × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: 30 agencies are in the network and 27 have an audit-rights clause. Coverage = 27 ÷ 30 × 100 = 90 percent. The three without a clause are paused until the contract is changed.

    Security checklist

    • Every agency has data protection, audit-rights and no-fee terms.
    • Workers are surveyed away from agencies.
    • Licences are checked on official registers.
    • Documents are verified with issuers.
    • Payroll is blocked until deployment checks are done.
    • Evidence of compliance is kept in each file.

    💬 Interview Questions: Inter-country talent corridor development

    IQ1.What is a talent corridor?
    A planned, lasting route that moves skilled people from one country to jobs in another, with partners for training, certification, visas and settling.

    Not every entrant is placed. Dividing the target by the yield tells you how many to start with.
    IQ3.How do you protect the source country?

    $3,300.

    💬 Interview Questions: Overseas sourcing agency networks

    IQ1.What is the employer-pays principle?
    Employers pay recruitment costs. Workers pay no fees, in line with ILO fair recruitment guidance.
    IQ2.How do you vet an agency?
    Check licence, ethics and fee policy, references, capacity and data protection with a weighted score.
    IQ3.A worker says an agency charged a fee. What do you do?
    Investigate, suspend placements, make sure the worker is repaid, review the network and report where required.
    IQ4.Salary $45,000 at 15 percent fee. Fee?
    $6,750.
    IQ5.27 of 30 hires are still employed at 12 months. Quality of hire?
    90 percent.

    💬 Interview Questions: Global deployment compliance

    IQ1.What goes into a pre-deployment checklist?
    Right to work, permit or visa, licence, medical and background checks, contract, tax and social security, insurance and family plans.
    IQ2.A manager wants someone to start before the permit. What do you say?
    No. Starting without permission is illegal and risks fines and bans. Move the start date.
    IQ3.Why alert at 90, 60 and 30 days before expiry?
    To give time to renew, so no one works illegally.
    IQ4.Start 31 July, processing 30 days, buffer 10 days. Latest date to apply?
    40 days before 31 July is 21 June.
    IQ5.Four violations at $7,500. Exposure?
    $30,000.

    IQ3.Processing under a pact is slow. What do you do?
    Find the cause with the government contact, fix document errors and agree a service level.
