    Course Overview

    Emergency Evacuation & Travel Risk Specialist: overview, objectives and prerequisites.

    🧭 Course Overview

    This course teaches how an Emergency Evacuation and Travel Risk Specialist protects people who travel or live abroad for work. It covers three skills: geopolitical risk monitoring, oversight of an international assistance and duty-of-care system such as International SOS, and crisis extraction workflow planning.

    The role matters because employers have a duty of care to their people. A good specialist sees danger early, knows where every traveller is, uses assistance providers well and has a tested plan to move people to safety. A weak one finds out too late and has no plan.

    Each skill has four levels, formulas and worked examples. The extra material adds tools, testing, good practice, projects, troubleshooting and interview practice. Figures, times and quantities are illustrative. Real plans must follow the law, the advice of official authorities and trained security professionals. In a real emergency, follow official guidance and contact the emergency services and your assistance provider.

    🎯 Learning Objectives

    When you finish this course you will be able to:

    1. Explain travel risk management and the idea of duty of care.
    2. Calculate a risk score from likelihood and impact and place it in a risk band.
    3. Combine source ratings and measure exposure and alert speed.
    4. Estimate expected loss from a risk.
    5. Explain what an assistance provider does and how to oversee the contract.
    6. Calculate registration coverage, cost per traveller, service level and response time.
    7. Account for people during an incident and count those not yet located.
    8. Plan vehicles, travel time, total evacuation time and supplies.
    9. Explain trigger levels and the stages of an extraction plan.
    10. Test plans with drills and report with clear key performance indicators.
    11. Protect traveller location and medical data, secure crisis communications and plan with safety first.

    🌐 Fundamentals: What geopolitical risk monitoring is and why it matters

    Geopolitical risk monitoring means watching political, security, health and natural hazards that could harm travellers or assignees, and turning that information into clear actions. Examples are unrest, conflict, terrorism, crime, disease outbreaks, severe weather and changes to travel advice.

    It matters because early warning gives time to act. Moving a trip, adding security or leaving early costs far less than a rescue. The aim is not to stop all travel but to manage risk sensibly.

    TermMeaning
    Duty of careEmployer's duty to protect people it sends abroad
    HazardSource of possible harm
    LikelihoodChance that the event happens, rated 1 to 5
    ImpactSeriousness of harm, rated 1 to 5
    Risk scoreLikelihood × Impact
    Travel risk managementProcess to assess, control and review travel risks (see ISO 31030)
    formulaRisk score = Likelihood × Impact (each from 1 to 5)
    🧪
    Practical Example: Civil unrest in a city is rated likelihood 4 and impact 5. Risk score = 4 × 5 = 20, which is in the extreme band.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Watching news only after an event
    • Rating risk with no scale
    • Treating every country the same
    Hands-on PracticeLikelihood is 3 and impact is 4. What is the risk score?
    3 × 4 = 12.

    📊 Core Concepts: Sources, risk bands and combined ratings

    Use several sources: official government travel advice, assistance provider alerts, local contacts, trusted news and security reports. Do not rely on one source. Different sources may disagree, so compare them and use judgment.

    Place the score in a band to decide the action. A simple scale is: 1 to 5 low, 6 to 11 medium, 12 to 19 high and 20 to 25 extreme. Each band has a standard response.

    BandScoreTypical response
    Low1 to 5Normal travel and awareness
    Medium6 to 11Briefing and check-in
    High12 to 19Manager approval and extra controls
    Extreme20 to 25No travel unless essential, with senior approval
    ReviewAny changeRe-rate immediately
    formulaAverage rating = Sum of source ratings ÷ Number of sources
    🧪
    Practical Example: Four sources rate a country 3, 4, 4 and 5. Average = (3 + 4 + 4 + 5) ÷ 4 = 16 ÷ 4 = 4.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using one source only
    • Ignoring local contacts
    • No fixed action for each band
    Hands-on PracticeFour sources rate 2, 3, 3 and 4. What is the average?
    (2 + 3 + 3 + 4) ÷ 4 = 12 ÷ 4 = 3.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Running a monitoring process step by step

    Follow seven steps. List the countries and cities where people are. Choose sources and set alert rules. Rate the risk daily or weekly. Match the rating to the traveller list to find who is exposed. Send alerts with clear action. Record actions and confirm receipt. Review events and update ratings.

    Exposure shows how many of your people are in the affected area. It helps you decide the scale of the response.

    StepOwnerOutput
    Location listTravel risk teamPeople by city
    Source setTravel risk teamApproved sources
    Risk ratingAnalystScore and band
    Exposure matchAnalystPeople in area
    AlertDuty officerMessage with action
    ReviewTravel risk teamUpdated rating
    formulaExposure = Travellers in the affected area ÷ Total travellers × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: 45 of 600 travellers are in the affected city. Exposure = 45 ÷ 600 × 100 = 7.5 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • No up-to-date traveller list
    • Alerts with no clear action
    • No confirmation of receipt
    Hands-on Practice36 of 400 travellers are in the affected area. What is the exposure?
    36 ÷ 400 × 100 = 9 percent.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Monitoring KPIs and scenarios

    Track alert speed (time from event to alert), the share of alerts acknowledged, the number of high-risk trips approved, the number of trips changed because of risk and the accuracy of ratings after events.

    Scenario: protests begin near a hotel. The duty officer rates the risk, finds who is nearby, sends an alert to stay indoors and avoid the area, and checks that everyone has acknowledged.

    KPIFormulaUse
    Alert latencyAlert time − Event timeSpeed
    Acknowledgement rateAcknowledged ÷ SentReach
    High-risk approvalsApproved ÷ RequestsControl
    Trips changedChanged ÷ TripsPrevention
    Rating accuracyCorrect ratings ÷ EventsQuality
    formulaAlert latency = Alert time − Event time
    🧪
    Practical Example: An event is reported at 14:10 and the alert is sent at 14:40. Latency = 30 minutes.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Measuring volume of alerts, not speed
    • Not confirming receipt
    • Not reviewing past events
    Hands-on PracticeAn event is at 08:15 and the alert is sent at 08:57. What is the latency?
    08:57 − 08:15 = 42 minutes.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: expected loss and trigger levels

    Experts estimate expected loss: the probability of an event multiplied by the cost if it happens. This helps to compare controls such as extra security, a change of route or an evacuation plan.

    They also set trigger levels. A trigger is a clear event or rating that starts an action, for example moving from monitoring to preparing to leave. Triggers avoid delay and argument in a crisis. Good practice follows ISO 31030 on travel risk management.

    Trigger levelExampleAction
    Level 1: MonitorRising tensionWatch and brief travellers
    Level 2: PrepareProtests and warningsStop new travel and update plans
    Level 3: RestrictViolence nearbyMove to safe locations
    Level 4: EvacuateAirport or roads at riskStart extraction
    Level 5: RecoverDanger passedCare and review
    formulaExpected loss = Probability × Cost if it happens
    🧪
    Practical Example: There is a 10 percent chance of an event that would cost $500,000. Expected loss = 0.10 × 500,000 = $50,000.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • No fixed trigger levels
    • Waiting for certainty before acting
    • Ignoring low-probability high-impact events
    Hands-on PracticeThere is a 5 percent chance of an event that would cost $400,000. What is the expected loss?
    0.05 × 400,000 = $20,000.

    🩺 Fundamentals: What duty-of-care system oversight is and why it matters

    Employers must take reasonable steps to protect the health and safety of people they send abroad. Many use an assistance provider, such as International SOS, that gives 24-hour medical and security advice, alerts, tracking and evacuation help. The specialist oversees this system: setting it up, checking it works and holding the provider to account.

    It matters because a provider is only useful if travellers are registered, know the number to call and the provider performs. Oversight finds gaps before an emergency.

    TermMeaning
    Assistance providerCompany that gives medical and security help abroad
    RegistrationRecord of traveller details in the system
    Assistance centre24-hour call centre
    TrackingKnowing where travellers are
    Service levelPromised speed or quality of service
    CaseOne request for help
    formulaRegistration coverage = Travellers registered ÷ Travellers × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 600 travellers, 570 are registered. Coverage = 570 ÷ 600 × 100 = 95 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Buying a service but not registering travellers
    • Not telling travellers the number to call
    • Never testing the provider
    Hands-on PracticeOf 480 travellers, 432 are registered. What is the coverage?
    432 ÷ 480 × 100 = 90 percent.

    📞 Core Concepts: Services, contract and cost

    Typical services are medical advice and evacuation, security advice and extraction, pre-trip briefings, risk alerts, location tracking and mental health support. Check what is included and what costs extra, such as evacuation limits and regions.

    Compare the annual fee with the number of travellers. Cost per traveller helps to judge value but quality and coverage matter as much as price.

    ServiceCheckWhy
    Medical assistanceHours, languages, regionsFast help
    Security assistanceExtraction included?Safety in crisis
    AlertsSpeed and relevanceEarly warning
    TrackingPrivacy and consentLocate people
    ReportingMonthly case and usage dataOversight
    formulaCost per traveller = Annual fee ÷ Travellers covered
    🧪
    Practical Example: The annual fee is $54,000 for 450 travellers. Cost per traveller = 54,000 ÷ 450 = $120.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Comparing price only
    • Not reading exclusions
    • Ignoring data privacy in tracking
    Hands-on PracticeThe annual fee is $75,000 for 500 travellers. What is the cost per traveller?
    75,000 ÷ 500 = $150.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Overseeing the provider step by step

    Follow seven steps. Define needs by region and traveller type. Agree the contract and service levels. Load and update the traveller list. Brief travellers with the number to call and the app. Test the service with a mock call. Review monthly reports. Meet the provider each quarter to fix issues.

    Measure the service level: the share of calls answered within the agreed time.

    TestSpecialistMock call result
    ReviewSpecialist and providerQuarterly action list
    formulaService level = Calls answered within target ÷ Calls × 100
    🧪
    Practical Example: Of 200 calls, 188 are answered within the target time. Service level = 188 ÷ 200 × 100 = 94 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Never testing the service
    • Not reading reports
    • Outdated traveller data
    Hands-on PracticeOf 480 calls, 456 are answered in time. What is the service level?
    456 ÷ 480 × 100 = 95 percent.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Duty-of-care KPIs and scenarios

    Track registration coverage, service level, mean time to respond, case closure time, traveller awareness of the number and satisfaction. Compare regions and provider performance.

    Scenario: a traveller falls ill in a remote area and the local clinic is poor. The assistance centre arranges transfer to a better hospital, informs the manager and family with consent, and records the case.

    KPIFormulaUse
    Registration coverageRegistered ÷ TravellersReach

    Mean time to respondTotal response minutes ÷ CasesSpeed
    Closure timeClose date − Open dateEfficiency
    AwarenessTravellers who know the number ÷ SurveyedPreparedness
    formulaMean time to respond = Total response minutes ÷ Number of cases
    🧪
    Practical Example: Five cases took 20, 35, 15, 40 and 30 minutes. Mean = 140 ÷ 5 = 28 minutes.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using averages that hide long delays
    • Not tracking awareness
    • Ignoring complaints
    Hands-on PracticeFour cases took 10, 20, 30 and 40 minutes. What is the mean?
    100 ÷ 4 = 25 minutes.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: accounting for people in an incident

    In an incident, the first task is to find out who is in the area and who is safe. Experts compare the traveller list with confirmations by call, app, text or manager. The number not yet confirmed is the number to chase first.

    They also protect privacy: tracking is for safety, with consent and clear rules, and data is kept only as long as needed. They work with local employees and record every step for later review.

    StepAction
    IdentifyList people in the area from the traveller list
    ContactCall, text and app message
    ConfirmRecord each person as safe or not yet reached
    EscalateUse managers and local contacts for those not reached
    RecordLog times, actions and decisions
    formulaUnaccounted = Travellers in the area − Confirmed safe
    🧪
    Practical Example: 45 travellers are in the area and 41 are confirmed safe. Unaccounted = 45 − 41 = 4, so the team chases 4 people. Accounted rate = 41 ÷ 45 × 100 = 91.1 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • No single list of who is where
    • Stopping when most are safe
    • Keeping tracking data with no limit
    Hands-on Practice30 travellers are in the area and 27 are confirmed safe. How many are unaccounted?
    30 − 27 = 3.

    🚐 Fundamentals: What crisis extraction planning is and why it matters

    Crisis extraction planning means preparing in advance how to move people from danger to safety. A plan names who decides, how people are told, where they gather, how they travel, where they go and how to care for them. Plans are written, tested and updated.

    It matters because in a crisis there is little time. A plan made beforehand avoids confusion, protects lives and reduces cost. Extraction should always follow official advice and use trained professionals.

    TermMeaning
    ExtractionMoving people out of danger
    Assembly pointPlace where people gather
    Safe havenPlace of safety, for example a secure hotel or embassy
    Primary and alternate routeMain way out and a back-up
    TriggerEvent or level that starts the plan
    DrillPractice of the plan
    formulaVehicles needed = People ÷ Seats per vehicle, rounded up
    🧪
    Practical Example: 45 people must move and each van seats 14. 45 ÷ 14 = 3.21, rounded up to 4 vans.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • No assembly point
    • Counting only people, not luggage and medical needs
    • A single route with no back-up
    Hands-on Practice30 people must move and each vehicle seats 8. How many vehicles?
    30 ÷ 8 = 3.75, rounded up to 4 vehicles.

    🗺️ Core Concepts: Routes, time and safe havens

    Plan a primary route and at least one alternate to the border, airport or safe haven. Check the roads, checkpoints, curfews and fuel. Know the entry rules at the destination, including visas, which may be waived or fast-tracked in an emergency but not always.

    Travel time is distance divided by average speed. Add time for checkpoints and for slower speed at night or in bad weather.

    OptionStrengthWeakness
    Road to borderFlexible, no flights neededCheckpoint and road risk
    Commercial flightFast and cheapSeats may sell out
    Charter flightLarge groups, controlled timingHigh cost and approvals
    SeaAlternative where ports are openSlow and weather-dependent
    Shelter in placeSafe if the area is secureRisk of being cut off
    formulaTravel time = Distance ÷ Average speed
    🧪
    Practical Example: A route is 180 km and the average speed is 60 km/h. Travel time = 180 ÷ 60 = 3 hours.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Using the speed limit as the average
    • No allowance for checkpoints
    • Not checking entry rules
    Hands-on PracticeA route is 90 km and the average speed is 45 km/h. What is the travel time?
    90 ÷ 45 = 2 hours.

    🛠️ Intermediate Concepts: Building an extraction workflow step by step

    Follow eight steps. Name the decision makers and the backups. Set the trigger levels. Identify people, including families and those with medical needs. Plan the message system and test it. Choose assembly points, routes and safe havens. Contract the transport and security suppliers. Prepare documents and supplies. Test with drills and update after each drill or event.

    Work out the total evacuation time by adding decision, assembly, travel and border time. Compare this with the warning time you expect.

    StepOwnerOutput
    Decision groupCrisis leadNamed people and deputies
    TriggersSpecialistLevel table
    People listSpecialist and HRNames, needs, location
    Comms planCrisis leadTested message chain
    Routes and havensSpecialist and providerRoute cards
    SuppliersProcurementStandby contracts
    formulaTotal evacuation time = Decision + Assembly + Travel + Border time
    🧪
    Practical Example: Decision 1 hour, assembly 2 hours, travel 3 hours and border 2 hours. Total = 1 + 2 + 3 + 2 = 8 hours.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Plans no one has read
    • No deputy for the decision maker
    • No plan for people with medical needs
    Hands-on PracticeDecision 0.5 hour, assembly 1.5 hours, travel 2 hours and border 1 hour. What is the total?
    0.5 + 1.5 + 2 + 1 = 5 hours.

    📈 Intermediate Concepts: Plan KPIs and scenarios

    Track the share of locations with a tested plan, drill results, time to assemble in drills, the share of people with up-to-date contact details, and supplier readiness. After any real event, review what worked and what failed.

    Scenario: a drill shows that 20 percent of people did not receive the message. The specialist fixes the contact data, adds a second channel and repeats the drill.

    Supplier readinessSuppliers tested ÷ SuppliersCapacity
    Actions closedActions closed ÷ ActionsLearning

    🧪
    Practical Example: 14 of 20 location plans were tested this year. Rate = 14 ÷ 20 × 100 = 70 percent.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Writing plans but never testing
    • Not fixing drill findings
    • Out-of-date contacts
    Hands-on Practice18 of 24 plans were tested. What is the rate?
    18 ÷ 24 × 100 = 75 percent.

    🛡️ Advanced Concepts: Expert: supplies, welfare and ethics

    Experts plan supplies for the longest likely wait: water, food, medicine, power, cash, communications and documents. A common planning figure for drinking water is about 3 litres per person per day, with more in hot climates and for people with medical needs. Use official guidance for exact amounts.

    They also care for welfare: family support, medical and mental health care, and a debrief. Ethical issues include local staff, who may be at greater risk and should be included in plans, and fair rules on who is moved first.

    ItemPlanCheck
    WaterAbout 3 litres per person per dayCount people and days
    Food and medicineDays of supply and special needsExpiry dates
    CommunicationsPhones, power banks, satellite optionTest monthly
    Cash and documentsCopies and secure originalsUpdate yearly
    Local staffInclude in the planAgree policy in advance
    formulaWater needed = People × Days × Litres per person per day
    🧪
    Practical Example: 45 people for 3 days at 3 litres each. Water = 45 × 3 × 3 = 405 litres.
    ⚠️
    Common mistakes
    • Forgetting local staff
    • No medical supplies
    • Planning for hours when waits last days
    Hands-on Practice30 people for 2 days at 3 litres each. How much water?
    30 × 2 × 3 = 180 litres.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: Geopolitical risk monitoring

    A risk register, a source list, a country risk rating sheet, an alert template, a traveller location list and an event log. The routine is to collect, rate, match to people, alert and review.

    Tool or techniqueUsed forExample
    Risk registerHazards and ratingsCountry, hazard, likelihood, impact, score
    Source listApproved information sourcesGovernment advice, provider alerts, local contacts
    Rating sheetAverage of source ratingsFour sources per country
    Alert templateShort message with actionWhat, where, what to do
    Traveller location listWho is whereName, city, dates, contact

    Working procedure

    1. List countries and cities in use.
    2. Choose sources and alert rules.
    3. Rate risk and place it in a band.
    4. Match the risk to the traveller list.
    5. Send alerts with clear actions.
    6. Confirm receipt.
    7. Log actions and times.
    8. Review after each event.

    🛠️ Tools & Workflows: International SOS / duty-of-care system oversight

    The provider contract, a traveller registration list, a briefing pack, a mock-call script, monthly provider reports and a case review sheet. The routine is to register, brief, test, review and meet the provider.

    Security & Compliance

    Data protection, access, fraud and legal risk for this role. Each skill has a security question in the Assessment.

    🔐 Security & Compliance: the big picture

    Travel risk specialists hold some of the most sensitive data: where people are, their medical needs, family contacts and emergency plans. This data can protect lives, but if it leaks it can put people in danger. Plans and routes are also sensitive, because they show how people will be moved.

    Security here means collecting and using location data with consent and a clear safety purpose, limiting who can see it, keeping crisis messages secure and authentic, and deleting data when it is no longer needed. In real emergencies, follow official guidance and professional security advice.

    Security riskWhy it mattersControl
    Location data leakPersonal safety riskAccess limits, encryption and consent
    Fake alert or spoofed messagePanic or wrong actionsAuthenticated channels and known sender codes
    Medical data exposedPrivacy breachShare only in an emergency and with those who need it
    Extraction plan leakedPlan compromisedRestrict plan access and use code words for movements
    Provider data breachCompany stays responsibleSecurity review and contract terms
    Tracking kept foreverBreaks retention rulesDelete data on schedule

    🛡️ Security for: Geopolitical risk monitoring

    • Use trusted sources and do not forward unverified rumours.
    • Keep the traveller list encrypted and restricted to the duty team.
    • Mark risk assessments confidential.

    🛡️ Security for: International SOS / duty-of-care system oversight

    • Check the provider's security certification and data terms.
    • Make sure travellers consent to tracking and know how data is used.
    • Test that the 24-hour line verifies caller identity.

    🛡️ Security for: Crisis extraction workflow planning

    • Do not publish routes or times in open channels.
    • Use agreed code words and secure messaging for movements.
    • Keep documents and cash secure during transport, and brief staff on personal security.

    🧪 Measuring security

    formulaTracking data retention compliance = Records deleted on time ÷ Records due for deletion × 100
    🧪
    Worked example: 200 location records are due for deletion and 190 are deleted on time. Compliance = 190 ÷ 200 × 100 = 95 percent. The ten late records are removed and the schedule is automated.

    Security checklist

    • Tracking has consent and a safety purpose.
    • Traveller and medical data is encrypted and restricted.
    • Crisis messages use authenticated channels.
    • Extraction plans are restricted and drilled.
    • Providers are reviewed for security.
    • Location data is deleted on schedule.

    Interview Preparation

    Five questions per skill with model answers: concept, scenario and numeric.

    💬 Interview Questions: Geopolitical risk monitoring

    IQ1.What is duty of care?
    The employer's duty to take reasonable steps to protect the health and safety of people it sends abroad.
    IQ2.How do you rate a risk?
    Rate likelihood and impact from 1 to 5, multiply them and place the score in a band that has a set response.
    IQ3.Why set trigger levels?
    They start action at a clear point and avoid delay and argument during a crisis.
    IQ4.Likelihood 3 and impact 4. Score and band?
    12, which is high on the scale used in this course.
    IQ5.5 percent chance of a $400,000 event. Expected loss?
    $20,000.

    💬 Interview Questions: International SOS / duty-of-care system oversight

    IQ1.How do you know the assistance provider works?
    Check registration coverage, service level, response times and mock calls, and review reports with the provider.
    IQ2.Why is registration important?
    A provider can only help and locate people who are in the system.
    IQ3.An incident starts. What is the first step?
    Identify who is in the area, contact them and record who is safe and who is not yet reached.
    IQ4.30 travellers are in the area and 27 are confirmed. How many are unaccounted?
    3.
    IQ5.A provider fee is $75,000 for 500 travellers. Cost per traveller?
    $150.

    💬 Interview Questions: Crisis extraction workflow planning

    IQ1.What goes into an extraction plan?
    Decision makers, triggers, people list, communications, assembly points, routes, safe havens, suppliers, supplies and drills.
    IQ2.Why plan an alternate route?
    The main route may be blocked, so a back-up avoids delay.
    IQ3.A drill shows many people did not get the message. What do you do?
    Fix the contact data, add a second channel and repeat the drill.
    IQ4.30 people and vehicles seat 8. How many vehicles?
    30 ÷ 8 = 3.75, so 4.
    IQ5.30 people for 2 days at 3 litres. Water?
    180 litres.
